## I. Position — Recovery Work, Held Honestly

The West African Diaspora spiritual stream that lives through Mia's paternal line is real, ancestrally accessible to her, and distinct from generic engagement with the tradition. Her father's people were enslaved West Africans brought to the Carolinas; the cultural-spiritual inheritance comes through that line. The work is recovery — the ancestors did this work; the descendants are reckoning with what survived, what was suppressed, what is being remembered.

Three complications need to be named directly because they shape what the practitioner can honestly do and what the document should treat carefully.

**The continuity question.** Most contemporary descendants of enslaved African ancestors did not receive continuous lineage transmission of West African or Diaspora spiritual practice. The institutions of slavery, forced Christianization, family separation, and the post-emancipation pressure to assimilate into white-dominated American life broke most direct transmission lines. Some lineages preserved practice — the Gullah Geechee specifically, certain Louisiana families, certain Black church traditions that retained syncretic substrate — but most Black Americans, including most descended from enslaved Carolinas families, are doing recovery work rather than continuous inheritance. This is consistent with the framing the new doc applies to Welsh tradition: the recovery is real, but the continuous-tradition claim isn't accurate.

**The cross-cultural-transmission debate.** Within contemporary Black hoodoo and conjure communities, there is a substantial ongoing argument about who can engage these traditions. The major positions: ancestry-required (only people of African descent should practice hoodoo, with the secondary question of whether mixed-race practitioners with Black ancestry have full standing); ancestry-strongly-preferred-but-not-required (non-Black people can engage with substantial humility, citation of Black sources, and refusal to commercialize); transmission-required (regardless of ancestry, lineage from a Black teacher is what makes the practice legitimate); commercial-syncretic (the Yronwode position — hoodoo has always been syncretic and is open to anyone). These positions are not equally weighted in contemporary Black practitioner communities; the ancestry-required and transmission-required positions are dominant in serious Black hoodoo writing of the last decade. The Yronwode commercial-syncretic position has been substantially critiqued for whitewashing.

For a mixed-race practitioner with documented Black ancestry, the standing question is real. Most contemporary Black hoodoo practitioners would acknowledge ancestral standing while flagging the recovery dimension. The honest practitioner-position: this is your inheritance to recover; you have standing; the recovery is harder for you than for someone raised in continuous transmission, and easier for you than for someone with no ancestry; the work involves not just learning the tradition but reckoning with what your ancestors carried and what was taken from them.

**Carolinas vs. Gullah Geechee specificity.** The Carolinas Black population includes both mainland Carolinas families (whose ancestors worked plantations across the inland regions) and Gullah Geechee families (whose ancestors were concentrated in the Sea Islands and coastal Lowcountry, where geographic isolation preserved a higher percentage of African cultural continuity). These are related but distinct populations. Mia's specific genealogy will determine which is ancestrally relevant — or whether both are. The document treats both honestly: Gullah Geechee as a culturally-distinct preservation of West/Central African inheritance, and broader Carolinas Black hoodoo / conjure / rootwork as the African American folk-magical tradition the keyed reader's ancestors more likely participated in (depending on where exactly her line was enslaved).

## II. Gullah Geechee — Cultural-Historical Frame

The Gullah Geechee are Black Southerners who predominantly live in the Lowcountry region of North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida — specifically the coastal islands and adjacent mainland from approximately Wilmington, NC down through Jacksonville, FL. They are descendants of West and Central Africans who were enslaved and trafficked to the coastal regions to labor on rice, indigo, and cotton plantations. Their geographic isolation on the Sea Islands — where the absence of bridges, the malaria-bearing mosquitoes that drove white planters to inland summer homes, and the post-emancipation continuity of Black-owned land — preserved a higher percentage of African cultural and linguistic continuity than survived in most of the American South.

The Gullah language (sometimes called Geechee in Georgia) is an English-based creole with substantial West African vocabulary and grammar from Mende, Vai, Wolof, Yoruba, Twi, Igbo, Kikongo, and other West African and Central African languages. Cultural elements that survived more completely than in most African American communities include: ring shout (a circular dance-and-song religious practice); seeking (a coming-of-age spiritual practice involving solitary visions); rice cultivation methods directly traceable to West African (especially Sierra Leonean) techniques; sweetgrass basket weaving; traditional foodways; specific spiritual and folk-magical practices.

**Spiritual practices.** Common Gullah Geechee spiritual practices include conjuring or root work — a form of West and West-Central African folk-magical practice that combined elements from many African belief systems and that was practiced outside of or alongside Christian religious life. Conjurers, both women and men, served the community by appealing to spirits and ancestors for purposes ranging from healing and protection to love and harm. Conjurers held social authority comparable to preachers and midwives. The carving of snakes onto "conjure sticks" to remove curses and bring healing — found in Sea Island communities — traces directly to West African snake reverence traditions.

The Gullah Geechee retained a more visible Africa-derived substrate than mainland Black populations did. The continuity matters because contemporary recovery work for someone with Gullah Geechee ancestry has more substantively-preserved material to recover, with living tradition-bearers still present in the community.

## III. The Gullah Geechee Nation and Contemporary Preservation

**Queen Quet (Marquetta L. Goodwine), Chieftess of the Gullah Geechee Nation.** Born 1968 on St. Helena Island, South Carolina; founded the Gullah/Geechee Sea Island Coalition (gullahgeecheenation.com) in 1996. In 1999 she became the first Gullah to speak before the United Nations, giving testimony at the Commission on Human Rights in Switzerland. Her advocacy was instrumental in the 2006 passage of the Gullah/Geechee Cultural Heritage Act by the U.S. Congress. Awarded the Order of the Palmetto by South Carolina in 2022. She is the most visible contemporary cultural-political leader of Gullah Geechee preservation work, with substantial focus on land rights, climate-change adaptation (rising seas threaten Gullah Geechee Sea Island land directly), and cultural continuity.

For a practitioner with Gullah Geechee ancestral connection, Queen Quet's work is not a religious teacher relationship but a cultural-political one. Her organization is the central coordinating body for Gullah Geechee preservation work and the appropriate first point of contact for the cultural-historical engagement layer.

**Gullah/Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor Commission (gullahgeecheecorridor.org).** Established by the 2006 Cultural Heritage Act. A federal-recognition entity that coordinates preservation, education, and cultural-resource work across the four-state corridor. Maintains educational and cultural resources accessible to the public.

**Penn Center, St. Helena Island, South Carolina (penncenter.com).** Founded 1862 as Penn School — one of the first academic schools in the South to provide formal education for previously enslaved Black people. After the school closed in 1948, Penn became the first African American site in South Carolina whose primary purpose was to safeguard the heritage of a Gullah Geechee community. The Penn Center History and Culture Program preserves and interprets Sea Islands and Lowcountry African American culture. Penn Center was added to the UNESCO Network of Places of History and Memory in 2024. The York W. Bailey Museum at Penn Center is a National Trust for Historic Preservation distinctive destination. For a practitioner with Carolinas Lowcountry ancestral connection, Penn Center is the central cultural-educational institution for Gullah Geechee history.

**Lowcountry Digital History Initiative (College of Charleston).** Web-based scholarly resources on Lowcountry Black history, including substantial material on enslaved women's spiritual lives, Gullah Geechee culture, and Lowcountry African American religious tradition. Open-access; serious scholarly material.

## IV. Hoodoo / Conjure / Rootwork — What It Is

Hoodoo, also known as conjure or rootwork, is the African American folk-magical tradition that developed during the period of slavery in the American South. Its origins are predominantly West and Central African, with substantial input from the African belief systems of the people brought to the Americas as enslaved labor. Over the centuries it has incorporated influences from Indigenous American (especially Cherokee and other Southeastern peoples), European (folk-Christian, Irish, Scots-Irish), and other sources, but its fundamental character is African.

Several distinctions matter for the practitioner.

**Hoodoo is not Vodou.** Haitian Vodou is a distinct religion with its own pantheon, initiation structure, and ritual apparatus, descended primarily from West African Fon and Yoruba traditions through the specific historical circumstances of Haiti. Louisiana Voodoo is a related but distinct tradition. Hoodoo is folk-magical practice; Vodou is religion. The two are related through shared West African ancestry but operate differently and are commonly conflated in popular sources.

**Hoodoo is not Lucumí / Santería.** Lucumí (also called Santería) is a Cuban-developed Yoruba-derived religion with its own initiation structure (the *kariocha* or *making santo*), pantheon (the Orishas), and ritual apparatus. It is not hoodoo, though hoodoo and Lucumí have shared West African substrate.

**Hoodoo is "magic/spiritual work" rather than religion.** Hoodoo Sen Moise's framing: "Conjure is not a religion or spiritual path, per se, but rather magic/spiritual work that is done to bring about change in a situation." Hoodoo can be practiced by Black Christians, by Black non-religious practitioners, by Black practitioners of other African Diaspora religions. The relationship between hoodoo and Christianity in particular has been historically complex — Yvonne Chireau's *Black Magic* (2003) argues that conjure and Christianity have worked together in a complex complementary fashion to provide spiritual empowerment for African Americans, both enslaved and free.

**Regional variation.** Katrina Hazzard-Donald's *Mojo Workin'* (2012) argues that hoodoo emerged first in three distinct regional clusters before consolidating into a national tradition after the turn of the nineteenth century. The Carolinas Lowcountry (where Gullah Geechee culture developed), the Mississippi Delta and Louisiana, and the broader Southeast each developed somewhat distinct hoodoo traditions with overlapping core practices and distinct local flavoring.

**"Old tradition Black Belt Hoodoo" vs. commercialized forms.** Hazzard-Donald distinguishes between the historically-continuous practice of hoodoo within Black communities and the commercially-marketed forms that have been "controlled, modified, and often fabricated by outsiders." The latter category is substantial; much of what is sold under "hoodoo" branding online is commercial product oriented to non-Black audiences with limited connection to actual practitioner tradition. The serious practitioner working in this stream needs to develop the discernment to distinguish.

## V. Recent (2018-2024) Black-Authored Practitioner and Scholarly Work

v10's bibliography on hoodoo / conjure was compiled with Stephanie Rose Bird's *Sticks, Stones, Roots and Bones* (2004) as the primary citation. Substantial Black-authored scholarship and practitioner-text has emerged since then. The deliberate gap-fill:

**Hoodoo Sen Moise, *Working Conjure: A Guide to Hoodoo Folk Magic* (Weiser, 2018).** Hoodoo Sen Moise was raised in Southern conjure and hoodoo, taught by his grandfather from age four. He has been a practitioner for nearly forty years and is also an initiated practitioner in several African Diaspora traditions. He resides in New Orleans and co-owns Conjure New Orleans with author Starr Casas. *Working Conjure* explores the history, culture, principles, fundamentals, and ethics of conjure while serving as a practical how-to guide. From within the lineage; substantial credibility. The major Black-authored practitioner-text of the late 2010s.

**Sherry Shone (That Hoodoo Lady), *Hoodoo for Everyone: Modern Approaches to Magic, Conjure, Rootwork, and Liberation* (North Atlantic Books / Cleis Press, 2022).** Shone is a Black, lesbian hoodoo worker with substantial client practice, openly acknowledging her Black, white, and First Nations heritage and working honestly with the question of inclusivity. The book presents hoodoo as a folklore tradition created by and for enslaved African Americans in the Southern U.S., framed around three guiding tenets — Intention, Faith, Direction. The "for everyone" framing is honest about the appropriation question while developing an inclusive practitioner approach for Black, mixed-race, and respectful non-Black engagement. Nominated for the 2023 COVR Visionary Awards.

**Stephanie Rose Bird, *African American Magick: A Modern Grimoire for the Natural Home (Four Seasons of Rituals, Recipes, Hoodoo & Herbs)* (Weiser, 2022).** Bird's late-career synthesis. Organized around the rhythms of the seasons, the book functions as a grimoire that invites the reader to build on it — recording histories and practices for use now and as inheritance for future generations. The seasonal framing is useful; the grimoire-as-living-document framing aligns with v10's grimoire methodology. Bird remains the major contemporary Black practitioner-scholar of hoodoo.

**Stephanie Rose Bird, *The Healing Power of African-American Spirituality: A Celebration of Ancestor Worship, Herbs and Hoodoo, Ritual and Conjure* (Hampton Roads / Red Wheel, 2022).** Companion to *African American Magick*. Centers ancestor work specifically; useful for the keyed reader's ancestral-recovery dimension.

**Stephanie Rose Bird, *365 Days of Hoodoo: Daily Rootwork, Mojo & Conjuration* (Llewellyn, 2018).** Day-by-day practice book. Useful as a daily-practice resource within the broader hoodoo apparatus.

**Katrina Hazzard-Donald, *Mojo Workin': The Old African American Hoodoo System* (University of Illinois Press, 2012).** Academic scholarship rather than practitioner-text. Hazzard-Donald is professor of sociology at Rutgers-Camden. The first interdisciplinary academic examination to incorporate a full glossary of hoodoo culture; argues against conventional scholarship for the regional-cluster model of hoodoo's emergence; distinguishes "Old tradition Black Belt Hoodoo" from commercialized forms. The single most rigorous academic treatment of hoodoo as a system. Predates the 2018 cutoff but is the academic spine for any serious hoodoo bibliography.

**Yvonne P. Chireau, *Black Magic: Religion and the African American Conjuring Tradition* (University of California Press, 2003).** Chireau is Associate Professor of Religion at Swarthmore College. The book examines conjure from the slavery period into the twentieth century, with particular attention to the relationship between conjure and Christianity. Predates the 2018 cutoff but is foundational academic work that v10 did not cite.

**Beaucoup Hoodoo Fest** (founded 2023, Treme, New Orleans). Founder Shantrelle Lewis is a Lucumí priestess and scholar of African religions. Annual gathering of practitioners, scholars, and people interested in hoodoo, Vodou, and other African Diaspora practices. Lewis founded the festival in deliberate response to the New Orleans tourism industry's bastardization of African Diaspora sacred practices for commercial gain — Beaucoup Hoodoo treats these traditions as serious spirituality accessible to anyone in the African diaspora rather than as spectacle. **This appears to be the contemporary successor to or parallel of "Conjure Crossroads" as v10 named — that earlier festival doesn't surface as currently active in 2026 search; Beaucoup Hoodoo Fest is the major Black-led contemporary hoodoo festival event.**

**Crossroads University (crossroadsuniversity.com).** Online courses in Southern folkways, hoodoo, and conjure. Practitioner-led education; varies by instructor; useful for distance learning when in-person community access is limited.

## VI. The Yronwode Question — Cross-Cultural Transmission Held Honestly

Catherine Yronwode runs Lucky Mojo Curio Company (luckymojo.com) and has produced substantial published material on hoodoo, including the Lucky Mojo correspondence courses widely used by aspiring practitioners. Yronwode is white. The status of her work within Black hoodoo communities is internally contested.

The position v10 names — "Yronwode is white and the question of who can teach this is internally debated" — is accurate but understates the contemporary debate. Recent Black practitioner critique of Yronwode and Lucky Mojo is substantial: the argument that white practitioners should not be teachers of hoodoo regardless of their personal expertise; the argument that commercial hoodoo product enterprises run by white people extract value from Black tradition; the argument that the existence of a white-run hoodoo course as the de facto standard pedagogical resource is itself a continuing instance of cultural appropriation. The Aidan E. Kelly piece "Whitewashing Hoodoo: The Appropriation of Black Heritage for Profit" (Medium, available online) summarizes the critique. Tumblr blog *@justiceayo* maintains "A Working Bibliography of Books On Rootwork/Conjure/Hoodoo That Are Actually Authored by Black Folk" specifically as a corrective.

The countervailing position — that Yronwode's actual practical-magical content is well-researched, that her courses have produced effective practitioners, that hoodoo has historically been syncretic — is real but is no longer the dominant position in serious Black practitioner discourse.

For the keyed reader: Yronwode's *Hoodoo Herb and Root Magic* (2002) contains substantive correspondence and herbal information that is useful as reference, but the apparatus should be received with critical awareness of the cross-cultural-transmission question, with primary citation given to Black-authored sources (Bird, Sen Moise, Shone, Hazzard-Donald, Chireau) when available. The Lucky Mojo correspondence courses are not the recommended pathway; the Beaucoup Hoodoo Fest, Crossroads University with attention to instructor identity, and direct relationship with Black practitioner communities are.

## VII. The Carolinas Specificity for the Keyed Reader

For a practitioner with documented Carolinas ancestral connection, the regional Black folk-magical tradition of the Carolina Lowcountry — particularly Gullah Geechee practice — may be ancestrally specific. The genealogical research dimension matters here: knowing where exactly the practitioner's ancestors were enslaved (which county, which plantation if traceable, which port of origin if traceable) shapes which strand of the broader West African Diaspora inheritance is most relevant.

**Mainland Carolinas Hoodoo.** The broader Carolinas Black population practiced hoodoo as part of the regional Hoodoo cluster Hazzard-Donald identifies. This is the tradition the keyed reader's mainland Carolinas ancestors most likely participated in, with the standard hoodoo apparatus — herbal work, mojo bags, candle work, ancestor work, divination through cards or other methods, ritual baths and floor washes, the broader Black folk-magical practice of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

**Gullah Geechee specifically.** The Sea Islands and coastal-mainland Gullah Geechee population preserved a higher percentage of West African continuity than mainland Carolinas Black populations. Specific elements include: ring shout (the circular dance-song religious practice), seeking (the coming-of-age vision practice), distinct herbal traditions, snake-imagery conjure sticks, the specific Gullah Geechee burial and grave-decoration traditions, the specific funerary practices that preserve West African ancestral practice more visibly than mainland Black tradition.

**The genealogical research dimension.** For the keyed reader, the practical work of tracing the specific ancestral line — through Ancestry, FamilySearch, family records, oral history, and (for enslaved ancestors) through the specialized Black genealogical resources named in v10 § XIX (AAHGS, Nicka Sewell-Smith, Bernice Bennett, BlackProGen) — determines which specific strand is most relevant. The genealogical work and the spiritual work feed each other; they are part of the same recovery practice.

For a mainland Carolinas Black ancestral connection, the apparatus is broader American hoodoo with regional flavoring. For a Gullah Geechee ancestral connection, the specifically Sea Island tradition is the more direct inheritance. For a connection that is mixed across mainland and Sea Island lines, both are accessible.

## X. The Specific Configuration — Notes for Chapter Writing

The keyed reader's particular ancestral configuration carries specific weight that should be honored in chapter prose:

**The Jones-on-Jones connection under investigation.** The handoff document notes that the keyed reader's mother's extended family may have owned her father's ancestors — the Jones surname appears in geographically overlapping Carolinas areas, and genealogical research is underway to verify or disconfirm the connection. This is not just a research dimension; it is a specific spiritual-political configuration in which the practitioner is descended from both the enslaved and (possibly) the enslaver's family. **The standing of the Hermetic-Masonic stream comes through the same line.** The Continental European Hermetic-Masonic inheritance enters through the keyed reader's father's matrilineal Dutch ancestor — the white enslaver who raped one of her enslaved ancestors. This means the practitioner inherits the Hermetic-Masonic stream and the West African Diaspora stream from the same point of violence. The chapter writer should resist treating these as two separate inheritances to develop in parallel; they are one inheritance with two strands, with the violence at its origin as the binding fact. The reckoning frames are not therapeutic concept but ritual practice.

**Welsh inheritance is matrilineal and separate.** The Welsh stream comes through the mother's side; it is not entangled with the Diaspora-Hermetic configuration in the same way. The chapter writer should hold the three streams in relation: Welsh as the matrilineal Mother's-side inheritance, distinct; Diaspora-and-Hermetic as the entwined Father's-side inheritance, with the violence at its origin.

The chapter writing should hold all of this without flattening it. The recovery of the Welsh stream is one kind of work; the recovery of the West African Diaspora stream is another; the reckoning with the Hermetic-Masonic stream and its origin is a third.
