First Workings

The container is built, the rhythm holds, the body is learning to stay. Now the hand begins to work. The first spell is small — one candle, one cord, one statement of intent — and yet it is the threshold the practice has been building toward. After this chapter the practitioner is no longer only a reader and a sitter. They are someone who does.

· · ·

This is the chapter the previous version of the document deferred and the chapter the beginner most needs. The first two chapters built apparatus and rhythm; this one builds the operative core — the work itself. Across months two through six, the practitioner moves from preparing to do to doing, and the doing has a specific shape that is best learned through the smallest possible workings repeated with attention until the structure becomes legible from the inside.

Six territories open in this chapter. Spellwork as the foundational operative practice — what a working actually is, the structure that holds across every magical tradition the document touches, and the first three workings to do. Divination beyond the daily card — scrying, runic and ogham work for the lineage-keyed practitioner, geomancy, bone-throwing, the cleromantic and bibliomantic register. The four elements as practice domains — fire, water, earth, and air not as abstract correspondences but as actual relationships the practitioner develops. The witch's calendar beyond the wheel of the year — the saint days, the planetary days and hours, the astrological apparatus that layers underneath the eight stations. The ethical-operative territory — protection, healing, money work, love work, and the binding-and-cursing question every adult practitioner has to think through rather than avoid. The grimoire tradition — your introduction to the medieval and early-modern primary sources whose lineage your contemporary practice descends from.

None of this material is optional in a serious witchcraft practice and almost none of it lives in the contemporary practitioner-author register at the depth this chapter requires. The reading lists across these six sections lean primary-source and small-press more than any other chapter in the document. The work is operative; the reading is the literacy that develops alongside, not the curriculum itself. Do the workings. Read what they ask you to read.

§ III.I spellwork

The structure of a working

A spell is a working — a deliberate act, performed inside a held container, that uses material and symbolic operations to participate in a desired change. That is the definition. Strip the gloss and the genre conventions and what remains is structural: a container is held; an intention is named; a sympathetic action is performed; the container is released; the work is left to act. The structure is the same in a Hoodoo honey jar made for a specific person on a specific Sunday and in a Solomonic angelic conjuration timed to a planetary hour and in the Cornish village witch's pin-and-paper binding. What varies is the apparatus — the candles, the herbs, the words, the timing — not the structure underneath.

The foundational logic underneath almost every operative tradition is what James Frazer in The Golden Bough (1890) named sympathetic magic — the principle that like affects like (homeopathic or imitative magic) and that things once in contact remain in contact (contagious magic). The poppet shaped to resemble a person is imitative; the lock of that person's hair sewn into the poppet is contagious. The candle dressed with money-drawing oils and inscribed with a target sum is imitative; the dollar bill folded and burned with it is contagious. Frazer's framework was developed as anthropological theory of "primitive religion" and the cultural-evolutionary scaffolding around it is now broadly rejected, but the operative principle is the working logic of folk magic across cultures and is the framework the contemporary practitioner inherits. Read Frazer with critical context (Mary Douglas's Purity and Danger, 1966, for the foundational anthropological corrective; Jonathan Z. Smith's Imagining Religion, 1982, for the theoretical correction); use the principle as a working tool.

The structure of a complete working has five movements that the beginner does well to make explicit until the apparatus becomes internalized. Opening — the work begins by deliberately marking the threshold between ordinary time and ritual time. Lighting the candle, sounding the bell, naming the deity, stating I am beginning the work. Statement of intent — the desired change, named precisely, in the present or perfect tense rather than the conditional. Not I would like to find work; rather I am called into the work that serves me. The grammar is operative; speak as if the work is already moving. The operative act — the actual sympathetic work. Dress the candle, tie the knot, stitch the poppet, pour the jar, write the petition. This is the body of the spell. Sealing — the working is closed. The candle is left to burn or extinguished with the proper protocol; the cord is hidden; the jar is buried or kept; the petition is sealed. Release — the threshold is closed. Thank the deity, extinguish the marker flame, name the working complete. Then walk away. The work that is held tightly does not move.

The first three workings of this chapter are deliberately small. A single candle working for a single specific intention. A cord-of-three-knots binding for a clear personal protection. A simple jar working for a sweetening or settling intent. None require materials beyond what the apparatus from chapter two has assembled. None take more than thirty minutes to perform. None are demonstrations of skill; they are the curriculum the hand learns by doing.

build · the first candle working

One candle, one intent

Before any cord, jar, or poppet — a candle. The simplest possible operative working, repeated until the structure is in the hand.

  1. Choose a single specific intent. Not a wish or a vague desire — a clear sentence in the present-perfect tense. I am held in steady work that serves me; I am protected on the road north; I am released from the binding to the place I no longer live. Write it on a small slip of paper.
  2. Choose a candle. For a beginner, a simple chime candle (the small four-inch ritual candles) in a color matching the work — green for prosperity and growth, white for clarity or general work, black for protection and binding-out, red for vitality, blue for peace and healing. Beeswax over paraffin where budget permits; the candle is consumed in the working and beeswax burns cleaner and is sourced from living animals rather than petroleum.
  3. Inscribe the candle. Use a pin, a needle, or a clean blade to scratch your intent's keyword and any sigil or symbol that holds the intent into the wax. The inscription is operative; the act of cutting the words into the candle is part of the spell.
  4. Dress the candle. A few drops of an appropriate oil rubbed into the wax — for the beginner's first workings, plain olive oil is sufficient; later the dressing oils become specific (Hoodoo's Crown of Success oil, Money Drawing oil, Protection oil — Lucky Mojo Curio's catalog is the reliable contemporary source). For lineage-keyed work, the oil can be dressed with herbs ground from your own garden or wildcrafted from the land you live on.
  5. Set the candle on a fireproof surface — a plate, a candleholder, the altar itself if it can hold heat. Place the petition paper underneath if the working asks you to, folded toward you for drawing-in work and away for sending-out work.
  6. Open the working. Light the candle. Speak the statement of intent aloud. Sit with the candle through one full sitting (twenty to thirty minutes is sufficient for a chime candle — they burn in roughly that time). Do not leave the candle unattended. Watch the flame. Notice what arises.
  7. Close the working. When the candle has burned down or you must leave, snuff it (do not blow it out — blowing disperses the work; snuffing seals it). Speak the working complete. Thank whatever you addressed.
  8. Disposal. The wax remnant is buried at a crossroads or under a tree for sending-out work, or kept on the altar for ongoing drawing-in work. The petition paper is burned in a separate flame and the ashes scattered for sending-out, or kept folded in the grimoire for drawing-in. The disposal is part of the working.
practice · the candle ledger

Document each working

five minutes after each working · in the grimoire

Before any second working is performed, write the first one down. The candle ledger is a dedicated section of the grimoire that records every working: the date, the moon phase, the planetary day if known, the intent, the materials used, the candle color, the dressing oil, what arose during the burn, and what arrived in the days and weeks that followed.

The ledger is not for show. It is the only way a practitioner builds operative literacy across years. After fifty workings the patterns become visible — what works under what conditions, what materials suit your hand, what timing your particular practice responds to. Without the ledger, fifty workings produce fifty isolated incidents. With it, they produce a working file of your own developing tradition.

build · the cord of three knots

The first knot working

The witch's cord is one of the oldest and simplest operative instruments. The cord-of-nine-knots is the historically attested working in British and Cornish traditional witchcraft (documented in Paul Huson's Mastering Witchcraft, in Doreen Valiente's work, in Gemma Gary's Traditional Witchcraft: A Cornish Book of Ways); the cord-of-three is the beginner's version. Three knots is sufficient to learn the structure.

  1. Acquire a length of natural cord — wool, cotton, hemp, or linen. Synthetic cord is functionally inert. Length: a forearm's measure, roughly fourteen to eighteen inches. Color matched to the work as with the candle: black for binding, red for vitality, green for growth, blue for healing or peace, white for clarity.
  2. Choose the intent. The cord-of-three is well suited to protection, binding-in (holding something to you), or binding-out (preventing something from reaching you). For a first working, choose a protection — for yourself, for someone in your care, for a journey, for a project.
  3. Open the working as you did with the candle. Speak the statement of intent aloud.
  4. Tie the first knot near one end of the cord. As you tie it, speak the intent aloud once. Pull the knot tight.
  5. Tie the second knot in the middle. Speak the intent again as you tie. Pull tight.
  6. Tie the third knot near the other end. Speak the intent a third time. Pull tight.
  7. Seal the working. Hold the cord between both hands. Name what is bound into it. Close the threshold.
  8. Disposition. For protection, carry the cord on your person or hide it where it does its work — under your bed, in your car, near a threshold of the house. For binding-out, the cord is buried at a crossroads. For drawing-in, the cord stays on the altar until the work is complete; then it is burned and the ashes scattered with thanks.
practice · cord cycle

One cord per moon

monthly · timed to the dark or new moon · ongoing for at least six months

For the first six months of operative work, tie one cord per lunar cycle. Time it to the dark moon for binding-out and clearing work, the new moon for new beginnings, the waxing moon for drawing-in, the full moon for working at peak power. The repetition across six lunar cycles teaches the hand the structure without burdening it with ambition. After six months the cord becomes available as a working instrument the practitioner reaches for when the situation asks for it, rather than as a learned exercise.

build · a simple jar working

The third working — sweet jar or freezer jar

Jar working is largely a Hoodoo and African American folk-magical tradition (with cousins in Pennsylvania Dutch braucherei, Italian-American folk magic, and the wider European witchcraft tradition). The contemporary practitioner working outside the African Diasporic religions can engage the jar tradition with respect for its lineage; if your practice opens substantively into Hoodoo, the relationship requires direct engagement with Black practitioners and traditions, not extraction. Catherine Yronwode's Hoodoo Herb and Root Magic and the broader Lucky Mojo curriculum is the reliable contemporary teaching ground; Stephanie Rose Bird's Sticks, Stones, Roots & Bones is the practitioner-text by a Black author. For a beginner working from a non-Diasporic position, the structural form is portable; the lineage-specific names and traditions stay where they belong.

Two jar workings to learn: the sweet jar (for bringing harmony, sweetness, or favorable disposition to a relationship) and the freezer jar (for cooling, slowing, or holding an aggressive situation in suspension).

  1. For the sweet jar — a small clean glass jar with lid; a slip of paper with the petition (the desired sweetening, named); a sweetener (honey, sugar, molasses, syrup); herbs that hold the work (rose for love, lavender for peace, chamomile for calm). For the freezer jar — same vessel; petition (the situation to be cooled, named); a freezing medium (vinegar, lemon juice, water); cooling herbs (mint, parsley, peppermint).
  2. Write the petition. For the sweet jar, names of the parties and the relationship to be sweetened. For the freezer jar, the name of the person or situation to be cooled. The grammar is operative.
  3. Fold the petition toward you for the sweet jar (drawing-in), away for the freezer jar (sending-out, putting on ice).
  4. Place the petition in the jar. Add the herbs. Pour in the sweetener or freezing medium until the petition is covered.
  5. Seal the jar. Speak the working aloud. For the sweet jar, set on the altar and burn a candle on top of the lid (if the lid is metal, a candle can be set on it directly with care; otherwise on a holder near the jar) for one full burn each Sunday or Friday for as long as the work asks. For the freezer jar, place in the back of the freezer and leave there.
  6. Disposition. Sweet jar continues until the work concludes; the jar is then buried or its contents poured into running water with thanks. Freezer jar continues as long as the situation requires; when the situation has moved, the jar is removed from the freezer and the contents disposed at a crossroads with the working named complete.

The first three workings — candle, cord, jar — give the hand the foundational vocabulary. After six months of repeating them at the cycle the practice asks for, three further operative forms open: the poppet (sympathetic-representational figure work), the mirror (reflection, glamour, scrying-as-spell, protection), and the charm bag (mojo, gris-gris, sachet — portable working). Each is a sub-tradition with its own lineage and literature; each can be learned at the same small scale as the candle. None is more sophisticated than the candle, only more apparatus-dependent. The candle remains the foundational working across decades.

The ethical-operative territory the chapter develops in § III.V deserves a forward note here. Hex, curse, binding-out — work that constrains another person's agency — is a real territory of the tradition that adult practitioners have to think through rather than avoid. The dominant contemporary witchcraft register (Wiccan, neopagan-mainstream) has the threefold law and the rede as ethical structures intended to forbid harming-work. The traditional witchcraft register has no such prohibition; the cunning-folk historically did binding-out work, the Sabbatic-Cochranian tradition treats harming-work as a tool to be used judiciously, the Hoodoo and folk-Catholic traditions have substantial corpus on cursing, crossing, and uncrossing. The position the keyed reader will eventually take is hers; what matters in the first six months is that she understands the territory exists, knows that the first three workings here are structurally consent-respectful (working on yourself or a situation, not constraining another person against their will), and develops the habit of asking whose agency is involved in this working before performing it. Section § III.V in this chapter takes the question seriously.

Sympathetic magic, the operative tradition, and the contemporary practitioner-spell-corpus

Sequence: Months 2-6 (alongside the first three workings): Valiente's Natural Magic as the foundational mid-century operative-witchcraft text; Illes's Encyclopedia of 5,000 Spells as the working reference for forms; Gary's Traditional Witchcraft for the traditional-British operative register. Months 6-12: Yronwode for the Hoodoo and African American operative tradition (with awareness of the lineage question); Huson's Mastering Witchcraft for the unflinching mid-century operative-craft text. Year 1-3: Frazer's Golden Bough for the foundational theoretical framework (with critical context — Douglas, Smith); Leitch's Secrets of the Magickal Grimoires for the grimoiric-tradition operative spine. Year 3-5 (depth): Hoggard on the archaeological evidence for concealed magic; Davies's Popular Magic for the cunning-folk tradition; Williamson archives via Patterson. Reference / continuing: Illes returns as a reference work consulted across decades; the Lucky Mojo curriculum and the Cultus Sabbati corpus continue developing as the practice itself develops.

Natural Magic Doreen Valiente · Robert Hale · 1975/1985

Valiente (1922-1999) is the foundational figure of mid-century English witchcraft after Gardner — she co-authored much of the Gardnerian liturgy, then broke with Gardner and developed her own substantial operative corpus. Natural Magic is her most directly operative book, treating the working candle, the cord, herbs, charms, and the structure of folk-magical practice with the seriousness of a practitioner who had been doing the work for decades. Substantially better than her better-known An ABC of Witchcraft (1973) for the operative beginner. Out of print in some editions but reliably available used. Pair with Witchcraft for Tomorrow (1978) for her later thinking on solitary practice and ethics.

Mastering Witchcraft: A Practical Guide for Witches, Warlocks, and Covens Paul Huson · G.P. Putnam's Sons · 1970

Huson's Mastering Witchcraft is one of the most consequential and most controversial mid-century operative-witchcraft texts. Published the same year as Margot Adler's Drawing Down the Moon, it took the opposite path — unflinching, operative, willing to treat curse-and-binding work as part of the tradition rather than as territory to legislate around. The book has been consistently in print since 1970 (Putnam, Perigee, Berkley editions), used by generations of practitioners across traditions. Critical context: Huson's voice carries the period's casual sexism in places; his attitude toward gendered roles in working is dated; his treatment of love-work is more aggressive than contemporary consent-conscious practice tolerates. Read for the operative apparatus and the unflinching engagement with the full territory; bring contemporary ethical apparatus to the reading. The chapter on bewitchment specifically remains one of the clearest practitioner treatments of the cursing-and-binding tradition in print.

The Golden Bough · The New Golden Bough (abridged) James George Frazer · Macmillan · 1890-1915 (12 vols.) / 1959 (Theodor Gaster's abridgment)

Frazer's Golden Bough is the foundational comparative-folklore text in which sympathetic magic — homeopathic and contagious — was first systematically articulated. The cultural-evolutionary framework around the principle is now broadly rejected (Frazer treated "primitive" religion as an early stage of human development to be superseded); the operative principle remains the working logic of folk magic across cultures. Read the Gaster abridgment (1959) rather than the full 12-volume set; the relevant chapters are "The Principles of Magic," "Sympathetic Magic," and "The Magic Spring." Critical context required: pair with Mary Douglas, Purity and Danger (1966), for the foundational anthropological corrective on what Frazer called "primitive thought"; Jonathan Z. Smith, Imagining Religion (1982), for the theoretical correction; Wendy Doniger O'Flaherty's introduction to the Penguin Classics edition of The Golden Bough (1996) for the contemporary feminist-comparativist framing. Read Frazer for the framework, not for the worldview.

§ III.II divination beyond the daily card

Scrying, lots, runes, ogham, geomancy

Tarot is one tradition. Chapter 2 introduced it as the daily-practice divinatory form, and the years-long study develops in chapter 5. The wider divinatory landscape is what opens here. The contemporary witchcraft register treats tarot as if it were the divinatory practice; this is a recent and parochial position. The historical witch and cunning-folk repertoire was substantially wider — scrying in any reflective surface, the casting of bones and lots, the runes for the practitioners working in Northern-tradition currents, the ogham for those working Welsh-and-Irish-Celtic, geomancy for the Renaissance-grimoire and West African-derived registers, the playing-card tradition that is older than tarot in much of Europe, dream-incubation, augury read from birds and animals and sky-signs, bibliomancy from the household scriptures, the pendulum as a working tool, the sortes-and-lots traditions of Greek-Roman and medieval Christian practice. The tarot-only practitioner is reading from one keyboard.

The practitioner adds one divinatory technology beyond tarot during the first six months of operative work. Not three, not five — one. Sustained relationship with a single divinatory technology over months produces literacy that no amount of dabbling produces. The choice depends on what the practice is opening into. The Welsh-line keyed reader has direct lineage-access to ogham; the paternal-line keyed reader has lineage-access to bone-throwing in the African American and West African tradition; the practitioner whose work opens Hellenic has the Greek scrying and sortilege traditions; the Norse-tradition practitioner has the runes and the blótspánn (lot-casting). Or the practitioner can begin with the most universal — water-scrying or mirror-scrying — which crosses every tradition because every tradition has had reflective surfaces and sustained gazing into them. The choice is not load-bearing if the practice is sustained. The technology becomes a working instrument across years; what matters is that one instrument develops past the tarot-and-dabbling stage.

Scrying is the most universal divinatory technology. The principle: the practitioner gazes into a reflective surface — water in a dark bowl, a black mirror, a polished stone, a flame, smoke rising from incense, ink in a saucer, a crystal — until the visual field becomes labile and images, impressions, presences, or sustained perceptions arise that the practitioner did not deliberately produce. The technique is ancient (Pliny names water-scrying in Naturalis Historia; the Greek Magical Papyri contain scrying-procedures; John Dee's late-Tudor Enochian system is a substantial scrying corpus; the African and African Diasporic traditions have substantial mirror-and-water scrying; the Welsh and Celtic traditions have well-and-pool scrying). The contemporary practitioner's introduction is small: a dark bowl filled with clean water, the room dimmed, ten to twenty minutes of soft gaze, a question held but not pressed. Scrying-as-spell is a related technology where the surface is used not for reading but for working — sending intent into the reflection, drawing something out of it, using the surface as the locus of the working. Donald Tyson's Scrying for Beginners (Llewellyn, 1997) and How to Make and Use a Magic Mirror (Llewellyn, 2014) are the principal contemporary practitioner-instructional texts.

Cleromancy — the cast of lots. The most cross-cultural divinatory category. A set of objects (bones, sticks, stones, runes, ogham staves, knucklebones, coins, cards, beads, shells) is cast or drawn, and the configuration produces the reading. Every continent has cleromantic traditions; the major contemporary forms relevant to the Western practitioner are runes (Norse-Germanic), ogham (Welsh and Irish Celtic), bone-throwing (African and African Diasporic), sortilege bones / knucklebones / astragalomancy (Greek-Roman primary), geomantic figures (Western and West African), obi/cowries/diloggún (Yoruba and Diasporic religions, with substantial initiation-required practice), and tarot or playing-cards when used as a draw-and-read system rather than a spread-system.

The runes — the Elder Futhark (24 staves), Younger Futhark (16 staves), or Anglo-Saxon Futhorc (29-33 staves) — are one of the major Western cleromantic traditions. Substantial caveat: the contemporary runic-revival has serious political-baggage problems. Edred Thorsson (Stephen Flowers), the principal mid-twentieth-century practitioner-author of the runic revival, has documented affiliations with white-nationalist organizations and ideologies; the Asatru Folk Assembly under Stephen McNallen drifted into racial-exclusivist positions; the broader Heathen tradition has fought a sustained internal struggle against far-right capture. Read with explicit awareness. The contemporary practitioner-scholar to read first is Maria Kvilhaug — Norwegian scholar of Norse mythology, author of The Seed of Yggdrasill (Whyte Tracks, 2013), The Poetic Edda: Six Cosmology Poems (Lulu, 2011), and the substantial Lady of the Labyrinth youtube corpus. Kvilhaug works the matriarchal-feminine-substrate of the Old Norse material with rigorous primary-source engagement and is one of the principal contemporary correctives to the masculinist-and-political distortions of the field. Diana L. Paxson's Taking Up the Runes (Weiser, 2005) is the practitioner-accessible introduction with appropriate political and lineage awareness. Stephen Pollington's Rudiments of Runelore (Anglo-Saxon Books, 2008) is the philological-scholarly companion. Begin with Paxson; develop into Kvilhaug; consult Pollington for the Anglo-Saxon-philology depth.

The ogham — twenty Old Irish characters used in inscriptions and in the medieval scholastic tradition as a divinatory and mnemonic system — is the cleromantic technology directly relevant for the matrilineal Welsh-Celtic line. Substantial caveat from the other direction: the contemporary ogham-divination practice substantially post-dates the historical ogham, which was primarily an inscriptional and mnemonic system rather than a divinatory one. Robert Graves's The White Goddess (1948) introduced an ogham-tree-calendar that has been broadly disputed by Celtic scholars; much of contemporary popular ogham-tree-correspondence work descends from Graves and is reconstructed rather than attested. Erynn Rowan Laurie — Ogam: Weaving Word Wisdom (Megalithica, 2007) — is the principal contemporary practitioner-scholar working the boundary between attested medieval material and reconstructed contemporary practice rigorously; her ogham practice is grounded in the medieval Auraicept na n-Éces ("Scholar's Primer") rather than in Graves. John Michael Greer's The Druid Magic Handbook (Weiser, 2007) develops ogham-divination for the Druid-tradition practitioner in accessible practitioner-form. Damian McManus's A Guide to Ogam (An Sagart, 1991) is the academic-philological foundation — what the historical ogham actually is, separate from contemporary divinatory adaptation. The lineage-keyed reader working ogham should read all three.

Geomancy — the casting of sixteen figures from sixteen possible four-line patterns of dots, organized on a four-by-four grid called a "shield," with derived "witnesses" and a "judge" generating an answer to a posed question — is one of the major medieval and Renaissance divinatory systems and is almost lost in contemporary practice. The system has parallel traditions in West Africa (where it descends from the same Arabic-Islamic source-tradition that fed the European version, with substantial development in Yoruba Ifá), in the Islamic world (raml, the Arabic geomancy that arrived in medieval Europe through al-Andalus and Sicily), and in medieval and Renaissance Europe (Cornelius Agrippa's geomancy in Three Books of Occult Philosophy Book IV; the Renaissance grimoires generally). John Michael Greer has done more than anyone in the contemporary period to recover Western geomancy as a working practice — The Art and Practice of Geomancy: Divination, Magic, and Earth Wisdom of the Renaissance (Weiser, 2009). Stephen Skinner's Geomancy in Theory and Practice (Llewellyn, 2011) and his earlier Terrestrial Astrology: Divination by Geomancy (Routledge, 1980) are the scholarly companions. The Yoruba Ifá tradition is religion, not geomancy — engagement requires initiation through the babaláwo system; the diloggún (cowrie-shell divination) is the more accessible cousin within the Lukumí tradition but still requires engagement with practitioners and the religion.

The remaining cleromantic and divinatory forms in brief. Bone-throwing in the African American and West African tradition — Stephanie Rose Bird's Sticks, Stones, Roots & Bones develops the practice within the Hoodoo register; the broader Sangoma tradition in Southern Africa has substantial scholarship. The blótspánn (Norse lot-casting with twigs) and the Anglo-Saxon hlytmen are the cognate Northern forms. Astragalomancy — Greek-Roman knucklebone divination, currently being substantively recovered through Sara Mastros's The Sorcery of Solomon (Weiser, 2022) and the broader contemporary Hellenic-magic recovery. Cartomancy distinct from tarot: the playing-card tradition (substantially older than tarot in much of Europe), and the Lenormand tradition (the 36-card system descended from Marie Anne Lenormand's late-eighteenth-century practice). Camelia Elias's Marseille Tarot: Towards the Art of Reading (EyeCorner Press, 2014) and Read Like the Devil (EyeCorner, 2019) are the contemporary practitioner-foundations. Caitlín Matthews's The Complete Lenormand Oracle Handbook (Destiny Books, 2014) is the standard contemporary Lenormand text. Andy Boroveshengra's Lenormand: Thirty-Six Cards (self-published, 2013) is the working-practitioner depth. Bibliomancy and the sortes tradition (Sortes Vergilianae, Sortes Sanctorum, the medieval lot-from-scripture tradition) — open a book at random; read the line under the finger; take it as the response. Useful as a daily divination requiring no apparatus. The pendulum — practical, accessible, controversial; Christopher Bird's The Divining Hand (Dutton, 1979) is the historical-and-practical reference, complicated by Bird's later positions on related fringe-science topics, but the foundational survey.

The choice of which divinatory technology to add as the first beyond tarot is best made experimentally. The first scrying session, the first set of runes drawn, the first ogham staves cast, the first geomantic figure — each of these is a small commitment, easily reversed. Try one. Practice it daily for a week. Notice whether the technology pulls you to return or whether it feels inert in the hand. If the hand returns, build it as the second divinatory practice for the next six months. If the hand resists, try another. The technology that holds you across six months is the second instrument; the others are noted for later. Tarot remains the daily card practice across all of this; the second technology is added alongside, not in replacement.

build · the first scrying surface

Water bowl or black mirror

The simplest divinatory instrument the practice will use beyond the tarot deck. Build one this week. Two viable forms; choose the one that calls.

  • The water-bowl. A dark-interior ceramic, glass, or stone bowl, six to eight inches across, two to three inches deep. Black or deep-blue glaze inside is ideal. Fill with clean water before each session; pour out after. Used for scrying by gazing into the dark surface in dim light, sometimes with a single candle reflecting from above and behind.
  • The black mirror. A picture frame (eight by ten inches or larger), the glass painted black on the back with several coats of matte black acrylic until no light passes through, reassembled with the painted side facing back so the front-glass reflects as a dark surface. Tyson's How to Make and Use a Magic Mirror documents the construction in detail. The black mirror lives on the altar permanently; the water-bowl is set up and dismantled for each session.

Practical first session: dim the room, light a single candle off to the side or behind you so it reflects in the surface but doesn't directly illuminate it, sit with the surface at a comfortable angle, soft-gaze for ten to twenty minutes without straining for visual content. The work is not to see images. The work is to develop the soft-gaze that allows what surfaces, surfaces. Document what arises in the grimoire — sensations, impressions, half-images, shifts in perception, presences, the absence of all of these. The practice is the cumulative record across months, not the individual session.

build · the second divinatory technology

Choose one — six months of sustained practice

By the end of month three of operative work, choose the second divinatory technology beyond tarot. Spend a full week experimenting with two or three before committing; let the hand tell you which returns.

  1. Acquire the apparatus. Scrying surface (above) is the universal default. Runes — a set of wood, bone, or stone with the Elder Futhark inscribed (or tooled into clay or carved into wood by hand, which deepens the relationship). Ogham staves — twenty wooden staves with the ogham characters scored in by hand. Geomantic dots — a notebook and a pen are sufficient; the cast is made by tapping the pen against a page sixteen times in four rows, then counting the dots in each row. Bone-throwing requires acquiring or assembling a working set of bones, shells, or other lots — which is itself a months-long process for those serious about the practice. Cartomancy — a Marseille tarot deck (distinct from the Smith-Waite already in use) or a Lenormand deck. Pendulum — any weighted-on-a-chain object that hangs cleanly; many practitioners eventually find a specific working pendulum that the hand chose.
  2. Read the foundational text for the chosen technology before the first divinatory session. The reading list below names the principal entry-text per technology.
  3. Daily practice. One question, one cast or session, recorded in the grimoire's divinatory ledger (separate from the candle ledger from § III.I). Date, question, configuration or impression, response, and what arrives across the days that follow. After ninety days the patterns will start to be visible.
  4. At six months, evaluate. Has the technology become a working instrument or has it stayed inert? If working, continue; the technology is now the second instrument the practice carries forward. If inert, the choice can be revised — the technology is noted as not-yours-yet-or-ever, and a different one can be tried in the next six-month cycle. Most practitioners eventually carry two or three divinatory technologies as working instruments across decades, plus standing knowledge of three or four others.
practice · the divinatory ledger

The second working notebook

five minutes after each divinatory session · in the grimoire

Parallel to the candle ledger, the divinatory ledger records every reading across every divinatory technology you carry. Date. Technology used. Question asked. Configuration or impression received (the cards drawn, the runes cast, the figure produced, the scrying-vision or absence-of-vision). The reading-as-spoken (what the configuration meant in the moment). And — across the days and weeks following — what actually arrived.

The fourth column is the discipline. Most contemporary practitioners read divinatorily at length and never check what actually happened. Across years, the divinatory ledger's fourth column is the only way the practitioner develops calibration — what kinds of questions the technology answers reliably, what kinds it doesn't, where her hand reads accurately, where her hand projects, what timing and context make the reading more or less reliable. After two hundred entries the divinatory practice has become an empirical discipline; without the ledger it remains anecdotal.

§ III.III the four elements as practice

Fire, water, earth, air — as relationships

The elements appear in almost every Western practitioner-text as a list of correspondences — fire equals masculine equals south equals will equals candle; water equals feminine equals west equals emotion equals chalice; and so on. The list is portable, memorable, and almost entirely useless as practice. It tells the practitioner nothing about what the elements actually are in operative work, which is something more specific: four (or three, or five, depending on the tradition) developed working relationships that the practitioner builds the way she builds a relationship with a deity or a plant or a place — through sustained material engagement over months and years, not through memorization.

The framework comes through several traditions. The Hellenic four — Empedocles's rhizomata (roots), worked through pre-Socratic and Platonic philosophical apparatus — fire, water, earth, air. The Hermetic synthesis through Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy Book I treats the elemental hierarchy as the substrate of all material magic. The Wiccan and neopagan four-quarters tradition is a twentieth-century reorganization of the same material into ritual-circle structure. The Druidic three realms (sea / sky / land) plus the centering awen-fire is a different mapping closer to the Celtic primary material. The Daoist five-phase (wood, fire, earth, metal, water) and Vedic five (the four plus *akasha*, ether or space) develop the same territory through different ontological frames; the Hebrew kabbalistic four-worlds plus the four-elements-mapped-onto-tetragrammaton is a parallel structure. The fifth element — variously named *spirit*, *aether*, *quintessence*, *akasha*, the *awen* — is the unifying principle most traditions name. The contemporary practitioner inherits the four-element Hellenic-Hermetic framework as her default working scaffold, and adapts it as her practice opens into the more specific tradition-currents that work the elements differently.

Fire is the most concentrated element in the practitioner's daily apparatus. The candle from chapter 2 is fire; the candle workings from § III.I are fire-workings. Beyond the candle, fire develops across multiple registers depending on which tradition holds it. Hearth-fire — the household-goddess tradition (Brigid in the Welsh-Irish, Hestia in the Hellenic, Vesta in the Roman, Cerridwen at the cauldron in the Welsh-bardic) treats the kitchen flame as the centering presence of the home. The contemporary practitioner can build a small hearth-altar at the stove, light a flame at the start of the day, name the household-goddess. Ritual fire — the bonfire at Beltane and at the cross-quarter celebrations is the sustained fire-ritual the wheel-of-the-year apparatus eventually opens into; for the apartment-bound practitioner, a single substantial candle held through a sustained sitting is the available equivalent. The chthonic torches (Hekatean) are one specific operative-fire register, useful for the practitioner whose work has opened that direction. The awen-fire in the Druidic tradition is the bardic-inspirational fire, the kindling of the creative-prophetic faculty. Ṣàngó in the Yoruba tradition is the orisha of fire, lightning, and the operative-fire of justice — a religion not a pagan-elemental-system, requiring the appropriate engagement framework. The first sustained fire-engagement is one extra candle lit weekly, named for whichever tradition's fire the practitioner is exploring, with attention to the flame's behavior, the body's response, and what arrives.

Water develops similarly. The water-bowl on the altar from chapter 2 is the daily working-water; the scrying-bowl from § III.II is a specific water-practice. Beyond these, water is the offering-libation tradition (a small amount of water, milk, wine, or tradition-appropriate liquid poured at the altar's base or onto the earth as offering — universal across virtually every tradition with offering-practice), the natural-water tradition (sea, river, lake, pond, spring, well — the practitioner develops relationship with one specific natural-water locus over years; for the keyed reader's San Diego context, the Pacific Ocean, the San Diego River, Mission Bay, and the few seasonal creeks all qualify), and the deity-water tradition (specific waters belong to specific figures). The Welsh holy-well tradition is directly relevant for the matrilineal-line keyed reader — Francis Jones's The Holy Wells of Wales (University of Wales Press, 1954) documents over 1,179 wells with their saints and customs; specific wells (St. Winifred's at Holywell, St. Non's at St. Davids, the wells of Mona/Anglesey associated with the Druidic tradition) are sites of substantial inheritance. The Yoruba and Diasporic traditions hold Yemoja (sea, motherhood), Òṣun (sweet water, love, prosperity), Ọya (the river Niger, also wind), Erinle (river-hunter); these are religions not pagan-elemental-systems, requiring framework. The Hellenic naiads (river-spirits), the Aphrodite-of-the-sea (pelagia), the Hekatean Triodos at the threshold-water — the Hellenic-tradition water apparatus. The Norse Rán (sea-goddess of the drowned), the wells under Yggdrasill (Mímisbrunnr, Urðarbrunnr) — the Northern-tradition water apparatus. First sustained water-engagement: a small offering-vessel kept on the altar, refreshed weekly, with deliberate libation poured.

Earth is the element most easily under-treated by the apartment-bound contemporary practitioner. The body itself is earth in the Hermetic apparatus; the bones specifically, the dense and weight-bearing aspect. Beyond the body, earth-practice is dirt, stone, root, burial, and the bioregional engagement with specific land. The land sit from § IV.V (chapter 4) is foundational earth-practice. The witch's compass — a circle marked on actual earth (or its substitute on a working surface), with directions oriented to local landmarks rather than to an abstract grid — is the operative earth-form. Stone-work is a parallel sub-tradition: the gathered-from-the-land working stones (not the New Age crystal-mineral-shop stones, which carry substantial mining-and-extraction problems; gathered from your own bioregion, with permission asked of the land before taking, is the operative form), the holed stones (hag-stones — naturally water-bored stones with a hole through them, traditional protective and second-sight tools in British folk-magic), the local geology (San Diego's chaparral granite, the coastal sandstone, the mudstone of the foothills) as the practitioner's working stone-vocabulary. Burial-work — small objects buried at marked locations as part of a working — is one of the oldest operative earth-forms. Concealment magic (Hoggard's archaeology of the buried witch's bottle, the dried cat in the wall, the shoe in the chimney — § III.I reading list) is a specific earth-and-architecture tradition. First sustained earth-engagement: a small dish of dirt or stone gathered from a specific local place kept on the altar, tended deliberately.

Air is the subtlest element and the one most easily collapsed into "incense and breath." The substantive air-practice: breath as the foundational regulation work the body work in chapter 2 has already established; the Welsh anadl framing (breath as soul-wind, the dying breath as the soul's departure) brings devotional weight to ordinary respiration. Voice — speaking the working aloud rather than thinking it; chanting, singing, the tradition-specific vocal apparatus (the Druidic awen-chant, the Hebrew tetragrammaton-vibration, the Hellenic hymnic forms, the Yoruba praise-songs, the Hindu mantra-tradition); the Welsh-bardic cynghanedd as patterned-sound for operative effect. Smoke — incense, smudge (with the cultural-appropriation question — white sage from Indigenous traditions specifically; European-tradition smudge alternatives include mugwort, juniper, frankincense, copal); censer-work as developed practice. Wind — the four winds in the Welsh and Hellenic traditions (Boreas, Notus, Eurus, Zephyrus in the Hellenic; the four directional winds in the Welsh primary literature); Ọya as orisha of wind in the Yoruba tradition. First sustained air-engagement: ten minutes daily of deliberate breath-with-attention before lighting the candle, with the breath named (the Welsh anadl, the Sanskrit prana, the Hellenic pneuma, the Hebrew ruach — whichever tradition's vocabulary the practice is opening into).

The fifth element. Most traditions name a unifying principle that contains the four — aether in the Hellenic-Hermetic, quintessence in the Renaissance-alchemical, spirit in the contemporary Wiccan, akasha in the Vedic, awen in the Druidic, the Hebrew ruach Elohim as proto-creative breath in Genesis 1:2, asé in the Yoruba (the activating-power that flows through everything). The fifth element is what makes the four elements magical rather than merely materially present; it is the element of consciousness, intention, the operative dimension itself. In the practitioner's work, the fifth element is named when the candle is lit, the working spoken, the threshold opened. Without the fifth, the four are inert.

build · the elemental month

One element, one month — sustained engagement

Across the first six months of operative work, dedicate one month to each of the four elements in sequence (any order). The month-long sustained engagement is what builds the working relationship.

  1. Choose the element to begin. Most practitioners begin with whichever element the body resists or feels distant from, since the working relationship there has the most to develop. The element you already feel close to will accumulate without specific attention; the one you don't will not.
  2. Acquire or set the apparatus appropriate to the element. Fire — one additional candle dedicated for the month, lit daily during the month's working window. Water — an offering-vessel refreshed daily with deliberate libation. Earth — a dish of dirt or a small working-stone gathered from a specific local place, tended daily. Air — a daily breath-and-incense session of ten to fifteen minutes.
  3. Daily contact. Five to fifteen minutes per day with the element's apparatus. No grand ritual; just sustained attention. What does the flame do today; what does the water hold; what does the dirt feel like; what does the breath move through.
  4. Weekly substantive working. Once per week during the month, a longer working specifically with the element — a fire-working that uses the candle deliberately; a water-libation poured at a natural-water locus; an earth-burial of a small object as offering or working; an extended breath-and-voice session.
  5. The elemental ledger. A short section of the grimoire dedicated to each element, accumulated across the months and across the years. What you noticed; what arrived; what the relationship is becoming. After four months, the four-element ledger is the practitioner's first map of her actual relationship with the elemental apparatus, written from inside the practice rather than from the correspondence-table.

§ III.IV the witch's calendar layered

Saint days, planetary days, astrological timing

The wheel of the year mapped in chapter 2 is a foundational calendar with eight stations across the solar year. It is a thin calendar. Serious witchcraft uses several calendars layered, and the operative practice scales as the timing apparatus the practitioner uses scales. By the end of the first six months, the practitioner is layering at least three: the wheel, the lunar architecture from § IV.II, and the introductory astrological-and-planetary apparatus this section establishes. Across years, additional calendars accumulate — saint-day calendars for the Black-Catholic / Hoodoo / European-folk-Catholic register, planetary-hour timing for grimoiric workings, the lunar mansions, the fixed-stars timings, the tradition-specific feast days. The full calendar a mature practitioner runs is dense and layered; the introduction is two more layers added to what's already running.

The first layer to add: the saint days. The contemporary witchcraft register often treats Christianity as the rejected predecessor against which the practice defines itself — which is historically and operatively wrong. The European cunning-folk tradition operated almost entirely through Catholic and Anglican Christian apparatus until the eighteenth century; the African Diasporic religions in the New World syncretized substantively with Catholicism; the Welsh and Irish folk-religious traditions are inseparable from the Celtic-saints calendar. The contemporary practitioner working any tradition with European folk-magical or African Diasporic roots will use saint-day timing whether or not she identifies as Christian. The saints are operative figures who hold petitions, intercede, mediate between the practitioner and the broader supernatural apparatus. The Black-Catholic and Hoodoo register treats them most directly as working figures: St. Anthony for finding lost things; St. Michael for protection (specifically against malevolent forces); St. Expedite for fast urgent work (one of the most-petitioned saints in contemporary Hoodoo and Diasporic practice); St. Martha the Dominator for binding and dominating workings; St. Cyprian of Antioch as the patron of magicians (the substantial Books of St. Cyprian grimoire-tradition descends from his cult); St. Joseph for home and family; St. Jude for desperate cases; Santa Muerte in the Mexican-American tradition (controversial; growing devotion). The Welsh and Celtic saint days that hold the matrilineal-line keyed reader's tradition: St. Brigid at Imbolc (February 1, syncretized with the goddess Brigid; Mary Condren's The Serpent and the Goddess develops the substantive scholarship); St. Winifred (Welsh holy-well saint); St. Dwynwen the Welsh patroness of lovers (January 25); St. Non mother of St. David (March 3); St. David patron of Wales (March 1). The first sustained saint-engagement is selecting one saint whose territory aligns with current need or with lineage-relevance, marking her feast day in the calendar, learning the brief biography and traditional petitions, lighting a candle on the day. Whether or not the practitioner identifies as Catholic or Christian is not the load-bearing question; the saints are operative figures the working-tradition has used and continues to use, and engaging them through the appropriate framework produces actual effect.

The second layer: planetary days and hours. The Renaissance grimoire tradition timed every working to the planetary day (each day of the seven-day week ruled by one of the seven classical planets — Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn) and to the planetary hour (the day and night each divided into twelve unequal hours from sunrise to sunset and sunset to sunrise, each hour ruled by a planet in a fixed sequence). Sunday is Sun, Monday Moon, Tuesday Mars, Wednesday Mercury, Thursday Jupiter, Friday Venus, Saturday Saturn. The first hour of each day from sunrise is ruled by the day's planet; the sequence of hours follows the Chaldean order (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon, then repeats) outward from each starting hour. Working type matches planetary correspondence: love work on Friday in a Venus hour; protection or binding-out on Saturday in a Saturn hour or Tuesday in a Mars hour; healing on Sunday in a Sun hour or Wednesday in a Mercury hour; communication and divination on Wednesday in a Mercury hour; prosperity on Thursday in a Jupiter hour or Friday in a Venus hour. The apparatus seems precise to the point of fussiness; the substantive practitioner argument for it is that the planetary hours are real elements of the operative apparatus — they are part of how the working is constituted, not arbitrary constraints. The contemporary tools for tracking the planetary hours: free apps (Time Nomad and Astrolog are accessible; Christopher Warnock's electional astrology software for serious electional work), the printed planetary-hour tables in Skinner's Geomancy in Theory and Practice appendix and in numerous grimoiric texts. First sustained planetary engagement: timing one working per week to its appropriate planetary day-and-hour and observing whether the timing produces operative difference.

The third layer: astrological timing. The full apparatus is electional astrology — choosing the timing of a working to align with favorable astrological configurations — which is a years-long study. The introduction-level operative timings: moon void-of-course (the moon making no major aspects to other planets before changing signs; traditionally a poor time for new beginnings or formal workings; useful for working that wants to dissipate or for releasing-work), Mercury retrograde (three to four weeks per year when Mercury appears to move backward; traditionally avoided for communication-dependent or contract-related workings; useful for revisiting and revising), the moon's sign and phase (Aries moon for initiating; Taurus moon for stabilizing; Gemini moon for communication; Cancer moon for emotional and home work; Leo moon for confidence and visibility; Virgo moon for refinement and healing; Libra moon for relational; Scorpio moon for depth, sexuality, transformation; Sagittarius moon for travel and learning; Capricorn moon for structure and authority; Aquarius moon for community and innovation; Pisces moon for mystical and dream work — with the phase modulating: waxing for drawing-in, waning for releasing, full for completion, dark for hidden), the lunar mansions (twenty-eight divisions of the moon's monthly path through the zodiac, each with traditional meanings and operative applications — substantial Renaissance grimoire tradition through the Picatrix). For the keyed reader's first-six-months engagement, tracking moon sign and phase is sufficient; the void-of-course, retrogrades, and lunar mansions develop later. Christopher Warnock's contemporary work on traditional astrological magic — through his Renaissance Astrology school (renaissanceastrology.com) and his various publications — is the principal contemporary access point for serious electional and grimoire-derived astrological magic.

§ III.V the ethical-operative territory

Protection, healing, money, love, binding-out

Most contemporary witchcraft writing avoids this section. The Wiccan mainstream legislates around it through the rede ("an it harm none, do what ye will") and the threefold law (whatever you put out comes back to you three times); the goddess-spirituality register sometimes treats operative magic as primarily inner-transformational rather than world-affecting; the contemporary social-media witchcraft register often treats workings as aesthetic-affective practice with the operative dimension elided. The traditional witchcraft register, the cunning-folk tradition, the Hoodoo and African Diasporic traditions, and most of the historical witchcraft we have evidence for engage these territories directly — protection, healing, money, love, binding-out, hex, curse, uncrossing — as part of what witchcraft is for. The serious adult practitioner has to think through them rather than legislate around them. This section is the framework, not the prescription.

Two foundational orientations before the categories. The consent question is real and load-bearing, but it is more nuanced than the popular witchcraft register's usual articulation. Working for yourself or working on a situation that affects you is consent-respectful by definition — your own consent is the relevant one. Working that constrains another person's agency against their consent is the ethical territory most practitioners have to think carefully about. Between these poles sit the gradients: working that asks the universe or asks specific spirits to bring a particular outcome (without naming who or how — petitionary work), working that protects you from a specific person's actions (defensive, not constraining), working that binds another person from harming you or others (defensive-binding-out, distinct from offensive-binding), working that seeks favor or attention from another person (which crosses into consent territory if the working aims at compelled rather than freely-given response). The popular Wiccan rede collapses all of this into "harm none." That collapse is operative-evasion. The serious practitioner tracks the gradients explicitly. The second orientation: the field has substantively underweighted the question of defense and self-protection. The contemporary witchcraft mainstream encourages people who have been harmed to "send light" and "raise vibrations"; the traditional and operative-witchcraft register treats that as a category mistake. People who have been harmed have the right and the operative apparatus to defend themselves. Protection-and-binding-out work for the practitioner's own safety is part of the tradition.

Protection magic is the foundational ethical-operative territory. The apotropaic tradition (workings that turn aside or repel) is one of the oldest witchcraft technologies. The forms: the witch's bottle — a small bottle filled with iron nails or pins, urine of the protected person, sometimes herbs, sometimes thread or hair, sealed and concealed near the threshold of the home (under the hearth historically; behind the front door, in the basement, under the porch contemporarily). Brian Hoggard's Magical House Protection (Berghahn, 2019) documents the substantial archaeological evidence for the tradition across Britain. Iron at thresholds — iron horseshoes over doors, iron nails in the doorframe, iron under the bed. Salt — salt across thresholds, salt at the corners of rooms, salt at the perimeter of property. Protective sigils — the apotropaic marks scratched into beams and doorframes (the daisy-wheel, the W or VV for Mary as protectress, the X across thresholds; documented extensively in British vernacular architecture). Threshold-blessings — the periodic working of asperging the doorways with protective herbs and waters. Mirror-protection — a small mirror placed to reflect away anything approaching with ill intent. The ancestor-altar as protection — once the ancestral apparatus from chapter 5 is established, the well-tended ancestors are themselves protective presences. Protection is the territory the practitioner builds first and maintains continuously across decades; the witch who has not done sustained protection-work has skipped the foundation.

Healing magic distinct from herbalism (which § IV.VI develops) is the operative working for another person's healing, with consent. The ethical structure is direct: ask before working, name what you are working on, don't work without permission. The forms: healing candle workings for a named person; healing jar workings with appropriate sweetening or grounding herbs; healing-charm bags made for someone in active illness; laying-on-of-hands in the traditional folk-healing register (with the appropriate framing — the practitioner is not the source of the healing, she is the channel for it; the deity, the saint, the ancestral presences are what is being asked); petition-and-prayer working through a saint or deity associated with healing (St. Anthony in some traditions; Our Lady of Guadalupe in the Mexican-American tradition; Brigid in the Welsh-Irish; the Asclepian healing-tradition for the Hellenic; the Yoruba healing-orisha Erinle, Òṣun, Babalú-Ayé). The healing-magic apparatus is one of the oldest dimensions of the cunning-folk tradition; the contemporary practitioner can engage it once the basic operative apparatus is solid. Important caveat: healing magic is not a substitute for medical care. The practitioner working healing magic for another should always work alongside the person's medical treatment, not instead of it; the operative work supplements the clinical, never replaces it.

Money and prosperity work is one of the densest operative territories in the Hoodoo and Diasporic traditions and one of the territories most useful for the contemporary practitioner facing material conditions. The forms: money candle-workings (green candles, Money Drawing oil, attracting-jar petitions); honey jars and sweetening jars for prosperity; charm bags / mojo bags with prosperity herbs (High John the Conqueror root, Master of the Woods, Five Finger Grass, Devil's Shoestring, Magnetic Sand); petition-workings to specific saints or spirits associated with prosperity (St. Joseph for housing and home prosperity; St. Anthony for found prosperity; the Yoruba Òṣun for the prosperity that flows; the Hindu Lakshmi in the Hindu-tradition); the lottery-and-gambling tradition in Hoodoo specifically (the Lucky Mojo curriculum has substantial gambling and luck-drawing apparatus). The ethical question with money work is usually not consent-based — money work is generally working on your own situation — but it is sometimes worth asking at whose expense the prosperity is being drawn. The traditional-witchcraft answer is generally that money is not zero-sum at the cosmological level; the reflective practitioner might still attend to the question.

Love and relationship magic is the most ethically charged operative territory. The categorical distinction the practitioner has to internalize: self-attracting work versus compelling-another work. Self-attracting work — making yourself more available to love, more open to being seen, more attractive to whatever is approaching — is consent-respectful by structure. Compelling-another work — making a specific named person love you, return to you, sleep with you — is the consent-violating territory the popular witchcraft register often elides. The traditional-witchcraft and Hoodoo registers have substantial compelling-another apparatus (love-binding workings, return-to-me workings, follow-me workings); they are operative; they raise serious ethical questions. The practitioner working in any tradition with this apparatus has to think through the position she is taking. Most contemporary practitioner-authors who treat the territory directly (Yronwode, Stephanie Rose Bird, Gemma Gary, Catherine Yronwode) come down on the side of: compelling-another love work is operative but ethically loaded; the practitioner who chooses to do it has to live with the consequences of having done so. The chapter does not legislate. It names the territory.

Binding-out, hex, and curse work is the territory most contemporary witchcraft writing refuses to engage. Three categorical distinctions: defensive binding-out (preventing a specific person from harming you or others — generally considered consent-respectful in the traditional-witchcraft and operative registers, since the working is responsive to the person's harming actions), retributive working (returning harm-for-harm; the territory the contemporary "return to sender" / "mirror" working occupies; ethically more complex than defensive but with substantial historical precedent in the cunning-folk uncrossing tradition), and offensive cursing (workings aimed at harming someone who has not first harmed; the most ethically loaded territory; the territory most contemporary practitioners do not engage). Paul Huson's Mastering Witchcraft chapter on bewitchment treats the full territory directly; Doreen Valiente's Witchcraft for Tomorrow develops the ethics; Yronwode's Lucky Mojo curriculum treats the Hoodoo crossing-and-uncrossing tradition with substantial operative depth. Sarah Lyons's Revolutionary Witchcraft (Running Press, 2019) engages binding-out as a tool for political and collective response — the well-known "binding Trump" ritual cycle of 2017-2021 is the most-public contemporary working in this register. The keyed reader will eventually take a position; the chapter names the territory and points at the operative literature.

build · the witch's bottle

Foundational protection working

If only one working from this section is built in the first six months, build this one. Protection is the foundation of operative practice; the witch's bottle is the foundational protection-form.

  1. Acquire a small glass jar with tight-fitting lid (4-8 oz). Salvaged jars (cleaned-out jam, salsa, baby-food jars) work fine.
  2. Gather: iron nails, pins, or sharp scrap iron (rusty iron is traditional; new iron functions); a lock of your own hair or fingernail clippings (contagious magic — the bottle is keyed to you specifically); a small amount of your own urine (the historical practice; the contagious linking is even more direct; if uncomfortable, blood or saliva substitutes); protective herbs (rosemary, rue, hyssop, salt, black pepper); optionally, broken glass or thorns; optionally, red thread.
  3. Open the working. State the protective intent: protection of self, of household, of those in your care, against named or unnamed harm.
  4. Layer the materials into the bottle in deliberate sequence: iron at the bottom, hair/nails next, herbs, the contagious liquid, additional protective material on top. Speak the working as you layer.
  5. Seal the lid tightly. Wrap the bottle in red thread or wax-seal the lid for additional sealing.
  6. Conceal. Bury the bottle in your yard near the threshold of the home (front door is most traditional), or hide it under the porch, in a basement, behind a wall-mounted object, or in any concealed location near the home's primary threshold. The bottle is meant to remain hidden and undisturbed.
  7. The bottle works passively across years. Replace or refresh every five to seven years, or when the bottle is disturbed, broken, or the protective work feels weak. The old bottle is buried at a crossroads with thanks for service.

§ III.VI grimoires as primary sources — introduction

The medieval and early modern primary literature

The grimoire tradition is the operative-magical spine of the Western tradition. From the late-antique Greek Magical Papyri through the medieval Solomonic and Hermetic literature, through the Renaissance synthesis (Agrippa's Three Books, the Picatrix's Latin transmission of the Arabic Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm), through the early-modern operative manuals (the Key of Solomon in its many recensions, the Lesser Key with the Goetia, the Munich Manual), through the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century French and English magical literature, into the contemporary practitioner-edited and translated editions — the grimoire is the documented working-tradition that almost every Western operative practice descends from, including the contemporary witchcraft tradition that often defines itself against the ceremonial-magical register. Knowing the grimoire literature as the source-tradition of one's own working apparatus is foundational scholarship for the serious practitioner.

This section is introduction-level. The depth-engagement opens in chapter 6 § VI.X, where the practitioner whose work has matured operatively can engage the primary texts as actual working-references. The introduction here is the territory and the major references — what the books are, what they do, and which contemporary editions are the working-versions to read.

The Greek Magical Papyri (PGM) — late-antique Greek and Demotic Egyptian magical texts collected into a working corpus, surviving in fragmentary form across multiple papyri excavated and published from the eighteenth century onward. The Hans Dieter Betz edition (University of Chicago, 1986/1992; cited in chapters 2 and 3) is the standard English critical edition. The PGM is the source-stratum almost every subsequent Western practitioner-magic descends from — Hellenistic syncretism, Egyptian temple-tradition, Jewish magical practice, the Greek magical-religious tradition, all collected into working texts that include hymns, conjurations, talisman-construction, scrying-procedures, healing-workings, love-workings, defensive and offensive workings. Read the PGM not as historical curiosity but as foundational working-tradition.

The Picatrix (Latin translation, 1256 or earlier, of the Arabic Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm, 11th century) — the principal medieval text on astral-and-elemental-magic. The Greer-Warnock translation (Adocentyn, 2010-2011) is the contemporary practitioner edition; the Attrell-Porreca translation (Penn State, 2019) is the academic-scholarly edition. The Picatrix integrates the Hermetic-elemental apparatus with planetary and stellar magic; the planetary talismans, the elections, the stellar workings of the Renaissance and early-modern grimoires substantially descend from it. Read after Agrippa Book I (introductory) when the elemental and planetary apparatus has been established.

Three Books of Occult Philosophy (Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim, 1531-1533) — the foundational Renaissance synthesis of Western magical philosophy. Book I treats elemental magic; Book II treats celestial magic; Book III treats ceremonial-religious magic. The Tyson edition (Llewellyn, 1993) is the standard contemporary scholarly-practitioner edition with substantial introduction and apparatus. The pseudo-Agrippan Book IV (later compilation; not by Agrippa himself) is sometimes printed alongside; treat as separate text. Agrippa is the central figure of the Renaissance magical tradition; the contemporary witchcraft practice descends from him through many intermediaries, often without acknowledgment. Read Book I first; Books II and III at depth in chapter 6.

The Key of Solomon (Clavicula Salomonis) — the principal medieval Solomonic operative-magical text in multiple recensions and translations. The Joseph H. Peterson editions (Twilit Grotto, 2009, with multiple-recension scholarly apparatus; alternative: Mathers' 1889 translation with caveats — Mathers conflated several manuscripts) are the standard contemporary practitioner-and-scholarly editions. The Key contains the Solomonic ritual structure, the planetary-pentacle talismans, the conjuration and constraint apparatus for spirits — foundational for the Solomonic tradition that runs through Renaissance ceremonial magic into the contemporary Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn descendants and the broader contemporary ceremonial-magical and Solomonic-magic register. Read at depth in chapter 6.

The Lesser Key of Solomon (Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis) — the seventeenth-century compilation containing the Goetia (the seventy-two demons of Solomon, with their seals and constraint-procedures), the Theurgia-Goetia, the Ars Paulina, the Ars Almadel, and the Ars Notoria. Joseph Peterson's edition (Weiser, 2001) is the standard contemporary scholarly-practitioner edition. The Goetia specifically is one of the most-engaged grimoires in the contemporary practitioner literature; substantial caveats apply (the demon-conjuration tradition is the most ethically contested grimoire-tradition; engagement requires the appropriate framework and protective apparatus). Treat as depth-material; chapter 6 develops it.

The Munich Manual of Demonic Magic — fifteenth-century Latin manual of demonic and necromantic operative magic, edited and translated by Richard Kieckhefer in Forbidden Rites: A Necromancer's Manual of the Fifteenth Century (Penn State, 1998). The Munich Manual is one of the most substantive surviving primary texts of medieval operative-magical practice; its contents are demanding and ethically complex. Read for the historical depth and the working-tradition's actual texture rather than as introductory practitioner-material.

The Heptameron (attributed to Peter de Abano, 14th century) — short medieval Latin grimoire on planetary-spirit conjuration, organized around the seven days of the week and their planetary rulers. Often printed as appendix to the pseudo-Agrippan Book IV. Foundational for the planetary-conjuration tradition.

Beyond these foundational texts, the grimoire tradition extends into substantial additional literature. The Sworn Book of Honorius (early medieval; Joseph Peterson edition, Ibis Press, 2016). The Book of Abramelin (early-fifteenth-century German manuscript with Hebrew origin; the Mathers 1898 partial translation; the Georg Dehn edition 2006 / English translation 2015 from the more complete manuscript discovered later — substantial difference between the two). The Grimoire of Pope Honorius, the True Black Magic, the Black Pullet, the Grand Grimoire — the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century French grimoire-tradition, much of which Joseph Peterson has edited in scholarly editions. The Book of St. Cyprian in its multiple Iberian and Latin American manuscript traditions (José Leitão's and Humberto Maggi's editions through Hadean Press). The contemporary practitioner-grimoire literature (Andrew Chumbley's Azoëtia, Daniel Schulke's Veneficium and the broader Cultus Sabbati corpus, Jake Stratton-Kent's substantial Geosophia and Encyclopaedia Goetica volumes, Sara Mastros's Sorcery of Solomon and Orphic Hymns Grimoire) — these are not grimoires in the historical sense but contemporary practitioner-grimoires that develop the historical apparatus for contemporary working practice.

The standing references for the grimoire-tradition reading: Owen Davies's Grimoires: A History of Magic Books (Oxford, 2009) is the principal scholarly survey of the grimoire as Western book-form across history; cited in chapter 2 for the grimoire-as-personal-instrument register, cited here for the grimoire-as-tradition register. Stephen Skinner's Sourceworks of Ceremonial Magic series (Golden Hoard Press) provides scholarly editions of major grimoires for the practitioner-scholar. Joseph Peterson's Esoteric Archives (esotericarchives.com) is the principal free-online scholarly edition of substantial grimoiric primary material; the print editions through Twilit Grotto and other publishers are the working references. Aaron Leitch's Secrets of the Magickal Grimoires (already cited in § III.I) is the contemporary practitioner-introduction to the grimoire-tradition's operative apparatus.
