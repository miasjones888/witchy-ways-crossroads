The Long View

What sustained practice over decades produces is not a different person. It is the same person, more thoroughly. The wheel as one's calendar. The body as known territory. The deities as old company. The grimoire as one document among many. You as someone who has been practicing.

· · ·

The chapter is short. The long view does not need many words. After ten years, twenty, thirty of practice, if you began the document has become someone who has been practicing — which is a different kind of statement than "someone who practices." The grimoires are multiple volumes. The sigil archive is years deep. The tarot working library has accumulated to the point that the personal book of cards is foundational reference. The wheel has turned a dozen, two dozen, three dozen times. The lunar architecture is so deeply inhabited that the moon is no longer counted by phase but felt in the body. The ancestral apparatus is established. You have done the work of repair across decades and continues doing it. The teaching practice, if it has emerged, is what she does without forcing it. Death is the central work of late practice.

This chapter holds three movements: the shape of decades; death practice as the central late-practice discipline; teaching as practice in the kept-aside register the document points toward; and the closing return to chapter 1's orientation from the other end of the arc. The closing four-line of v10 returns at the chapter's end as the document's actual final word.

§ VII.I the shape of decades

What sustained practice produces

What sustained practice over decades produces is what cannot be rushed. If you has been doing the daily candle for ten years has developed a relationship with controlled fire that you of year one could not have. If you has been doing the weekly card pull for a decade has developed a relationship with each of the 78 cards that the new reader cannot have. If you has been sitting at the same place outdoors for five years has developed a relationship with that place that no shorter engagement produces.

The accumulating record is itself part of what late practice produces. The grimoires are multiple volumes — Volume I, Volume II, Volume III. The reading log is a book. The sigil archive is its own working object. The personal book of cards is reference for your specific relationship to each of the 78. The lunar working journal, the wheel observations year by year, the soul-memory inventory, the genealogical-ancestral records, the metaphysical journal across years, the recognition record — these together constitute substantial documentary evidence of an actual life of attention. After thirty years they are foundational; after fifty they are inheritance.

The recurring spiral that chapter 6 named continues. Same territories, different depths. The wheel of the year is not flat repetition; each turning is itself a turning of the spiral. Samhain in year fifteen is not Samhain in year five. You return to the same questions at greater depths — what is the practice for; what does she actually believe; what is the relationship with whichever deities, presences, or ancestors have come into the work; what is the relationship with the dead, with the land, with the body. The depth is not finite; the questions never close.

The acceptance work that years three to ten began — sustained relationship with what is carried, the sufficient-not-finite frame of repair — develops further across the decades. You do not become free of the trauma; she becomes a practitioner who can hold what she carries. The CPTSD does not go away; your capacity to bear what surfaces grows. The lineage inheritance does not stop being inherited; your relationship with what was carried becomes more articulated. None of this is dramatic. All of it is the work.

The wheel does not get smaller. You do not get larger. The wheel becomes more thoroughly the calendar.

→ what late practice produces is not different person. same person, more thoroughly.
→ the trauma is not gone. you have become someone who can hold it. this is the difference.
§ VII.II death practice — synthesis

What decades of death-adjacent practice have produced

Death practice in this chapter is not introduction. It is synthesis. The work has been death-adjacent throughout for any practitioner whose practice has run long enough to mature — through the candle work, through the wheel of the year (Samhain alone is the death-station every year, and most other stations have death-and-rebirth registers), through the dark moon work, through the ancestral work in chapters 4-6 as continuing-relationship-with-the-dead, through the accumulated grief work as sustained engagement with what cannot be repaired only borne. The Hellenic-chthonic register makes the death-orientation explicit (Hekate as goddess of the dead, Persephone as queen of the underworld, the Eleusinian mysteries as initiation into the mystery of death) and is one of the most theologically articulated frames; the Welsh-Celtic register names death-work through the otherworld figures (Arawn, Gwyn ap Nudd, Rhiannon's underworld associations); the Welsh gwylnos and the Gullah Geechee sit-up are death-vigil traditions that work the same territory operatively; the Yoruba and Diasporic frames hold the dead through the egungun and the orisha-of-the-dead apparatus; the Norse hold death through Hel, the dísir, and the wild hunt. Whichever frame your work has opened toward, late practice is the synthesis of decades of death-adjacent work, now becoming visible as the work the practice has actually been doing all along.

The conventional contemporary American assumption is that death is something that happens at the end of life, to be deferred and managed when it arrives. The contemplative-religious traditions across most of human history have held a different position: death is the constitutive horizon against which a life is lived, and developing the capacity to die well is one of the central works of mature practice. Stephen Jenkinson — who directed palliative care at Mount Sinai Hospital of Toronto for years before founding the Orphan Wisdom School in 2010 — names this as the recovery of "death literacy" in cultures that have lost it. For if your work has been any of the death-tradition currents named above, death literacy is not lost. It has been the substance of the practice. What late practice does is bring the long-running death-engagement into focus as you herself approaches the threshold she has been preparing for.

This chapter holds two movements that you have been doing across decades and that now become explicit. The first: your own dying is no longer abstract. The body is teaching her about her own mortality through accumulated minor illnesses, slowdowns, new aches, new limits. The will-and-instructions practice begun in chapter 6 deepens; the conversation with the ancestors specifically about what is coming becomes substantive. The second: you have become the person who can hold space at deathbeds for others. As she ages, more people in her life die. Her sustained chthonic practice has produced a body that can be present to dying without flooding, a vocabulary for grief that does not need to be invented at the moment, a relationship with the dead that includes welcoming new arrivals across the threshold. Both movements are continuations of work begun in the first weeks. Neither is new.

The three lineage streams contribute distinct death-practice material. The Welsh stream contributes the gwylnos (the night-vigil with the body), the elegiac form (sustained bardic-poetic engagement with death), the sin-eater logic (willingness to take on weight in service of release), the cross-way ritual (marking thresholds in the journey out), the anadl framing of breath as soul-wind and the dying breath as the soul's departure. The West African Diaspora stream contributes the sit-up (community vigil through the dying process), the body-washing as community ritual, the grave goods and bottle trees as continuing relationship with the deceased, the second-line procession (Louisiana specifically) as celebration of the soul's departure, the sustained ancestor relationship across the threshold. The Continental Hermetic-Masonic stream contributes the alchemical death-and-rebirth as the structure of mature practice (the nigredo dissolution that precedes new form), the Masonic third-degree raising as ritual processing of mortality, the Martinist Reintegration framework as the orientation that locates your death within the larger process of return.

The contemporary contemplative apparatus runs alongside. Stephen Jenkinson's Die Wise: A Manifesto for Sanity and Soul (North Atlantic, 2015) is the major contemporary text on death practice; Come of Age: The Case for Elderhood in a Time of Trouble (2018) extends the frame. Frank Ostaseski's The Five Invitations (Flatiron, 2017) — five contemplative invitations from his decades of bedside experience at the Zen Hospice Project. Roshi Joan Halifax's Being with Dying (Shambhala, 2008/reissued 2024) — the central practitioner-text from her Project on Being with Dying since 1994. Sallie Tisdale's Advice for Future Corpses (Touchstone, 2018) — practical-bodily-medical complement. Stephen Levine's A Year to Live (Three Rivers, 1997) — the year-long practice of treating each day as the last in one's life.

The shape of late-practice death work, drawing on all of these together. The daily practice deepens — the candle held with awareness of what it is doing in the body; the grimoire entries holding the body's changes as data; the card pull holding the question of what is being prepared for. The wake practice for others, as you age and more people in her life die — Welsh gwylnos and Gullah Geechee sit-up are both apparatus for this work; Ostaseski-Halifax bedside-vigil training is contemporary supplement. You becomes, slowly, the person who can hold space at deathbeds — for parents, siblings, friends, community members — by virtue of long practice. The will-and-instructions practice. The body's awareness practice — your own body becomes, late in practice, the primary teacher of death. The conversation with ancestors specifically about what is coming. The acceptance work that decades produce.

→ death is not addition to late practice. it is deepening of what has been death-adjacent throughout — across whichever tradition currents the practice has matured into.
→ the body becomes, late in practice, the primary teacher of death. accumulated minor illnesses, slowdowns, new aches, new limits. the body teaches what is coming.

§ VII.III teaching as practice

Kept-aside, not workshop-leader

The contemporary American spirituality marketplace is heavy with workshop-leader teaching: a teacher develops a methodology, builds a brand, runs workshops and intensives, certifies graduates, scales reach. This is not what late practice asks for. The model the document points toward is older and quieter: an experienced practitioner is kept aside as a resource for specific other practitioners who, over years, find their way to her by some combination of recognition, recommendation, and encounter. She does not seek students; she does not run workshops; she does not certify. She is available to the people who ask. Her teaching happens in particular conversations, in extended correspondence, in occasional formal apprenticeship that is itself rare.

The Welsh bardic tradition holds the model. The pencerdd (master poet) was the earned position; the nine-year apprenticeship in the bardic guild was the time-order-of-magnitude for transmission. The bardic lineages extending back through centuries anchored each generation in the work of those who came before. The transmission was oral, personal, slow. Welsh contemporary practitioner-teachers (Hughes at Anglesey Druid Order, Telyndru at Sisterhood of Avalon) operate on adapted versions of this model.

The Black church mother tradition holds a parallel model in the African Diaspora register. The church mother is the earned position; the spiritual-and-moral stature of the older woman in Black religious community is recognized by community rather than self-claimed. Custodian of tradition, bearer of wisdom, embodiment of spiritual guidance — the role's transmission happens through presence, conversation, modeling, and the oral tradition rather than through formal teaching. For a practitioner with Black ancestry whose practice eventually places her in this kind of role within her communities, the church mother is the available model.

The Hermetic-Masonic tradition holds another version through initiated transmission within bounded bodies. Co-Masonic and other Continental Hermetic-Masonic engagement, if pursued, eventually places you in the position of teaching within the lineage — institutionally bounded, lineage-specific, not the broader cross-tradition kept-aside model but the same principle of transmission as relationship rather than transaction.

The practical shape of late-practice teaching: the shortlist of practitioners-eventually maintained over years; the kept-aside availability (reachable, willing to respond, not seeking); the recognition work (discerning who can be taught, when the relationship is productive); the actual teaching as transmission of one's own developed practice (the grimoires, the sigil archive, the personal book of cards, the reading log, the ancestral records, the accumulated working knowledge of decades) rather than as curriculum; the transmission of inheritance specifically (the recovered three-stream material becomes available to specific practitioners who eventually have standing for it). The teaching is itself repair work. The transmission of what was preserved — Welsh material recovered from the matrilineal line, Diasporic material recovered through community engagement, Hermetic-Masonic material engaged through whatever pathway you have actually pursued — becomes inheritance offered to those who follow.

Parker J. Palmer's The Courage to Teach: Exploring the Inner Landscape of a Teacher's Life (Jossey-Bass, 1998; 10th anniversary edition 2007) is the closest contemporary cross-tradition reference. Palmer's argument: good teaching cannot be reduced to technique, is rooted in the identity and integrity of the teacher, and is fundamentally about guiding students on an inner journey toward more truthful ways of seeing and being in the world. Palmer's broader catalogue (Let Your Life Speak, 2000; On the Brink of Everything, 2018) develops the elder-as-teacher frame across the arc of a life.

→ kept-aside, not workshop-leader. do not seek students. be available.
→ teaching is itself repair work. transmission of recovered inheritance is participation in the broader work of repair.
§ VII.IV repair continued

Sustained across the long arc

The work of repair as sustained practice does not finish in years three to ten. It continues across the long arc. Decades into the practice, you are still engaging the inheritance, still doing the daily practice that holds it in the body, still in relationship with you support that has been with her for years. What changes across the long arc is the depth of the relationship you have with what she carries — not the elimination of inheritance, but your developed capacity to bear what was given to her.

The Menakem framework of clean pain vs. dirty pain becomes more refined across decades. You becomes increasingly able to distinguish the productive suffering that fosters growth from the recursive suffering that comes from avoidance. The Foor methodology's stages two through four, which required practitioner support when first engaged, become your own working ground over years; the relationship with the well-resourced ancestors who have already crossed becomes deep enough that the conversation with them is itself sustained practice. The Schulman frame of distinguishing repairable from unrepairable becomes lived rather than conceptual — you know from years of practice what her own work can do and what it cannot.

The work of repair becomes inheritance you offers to the practitioners who eventually find her. Her own decades of repair work become the substrate from which she can speak to younger practitioners about what is repairable and what is not, what the work asks of the body, what the lineage traditions specifically contribute, where practitioner support is necessary, what cannot be skipped. The teaching is itself the continuing repair work; the repair is itself the substantive material of the teaching.

§ VII.V the witch's calendar at depth

Planetary hours, astrological timing, the layered apparatus

Chapter 3 § III.IV established the layered calendar — wheel, lunar, planetary days and hours, saint days, introductory astrological timing. By the long view, the calendar has substantially deepened. You running planetary-hour timing for substantial workings; the lunar mansions tracked for if your practice has opened that direction; the fixed-stars timings for if your astrological-magical work has matured; the heliacal risings (the dates each year when specific stars first become visible at dawn after a period of invisibility) marked. The Christopher Warnock electional-astrology apparatus has become standing reference. The full grimoiric astrological-magical timing the Picatrix and the Renaissance grimoires assume is operative for if your work has matured.

The saint-days have substantially developed across decades. The lunar mansions specifically. The twenty-eight divisions of the moon's monthly path through the zodiac, each with traditional meanings and operative applications, are foundational in the Renaissance grimoire-tradition (substantially in the Picatrix). Christopher Warnock's Mansions of the Moon (Renaissance Astrology Press, 2018) is the principal contemporary practitioner-text. The mansion-timing apparatus is most developed in the Arabic raml-and-astrology tradition; the contemporary practitioner who has matured into substantial astrological-magical work integrates the mansion-timing as an additional layer of the working calendar.

The fixed-stars timings. The major fixed stars (Aldebaran, Regulus, Antares, Fomalhaut as the four royal stars; Spica, Algol, Sirius, the Pleiades) carry traditional astrological-magical associations and are used for substantial talisman-construction and electional timing. Bernadette Brady's Brady's Book of Fixed Stars (Weiser, 1998) is the principal contemporary scholarly-practitioner reference. Christopher Warnock develops fixed-stars-electional through Renaissance Astrology. For if your work has matured to the level of substantial talismanic and electional engagement, the fixed-stars layer becomes operative. Most practitioners' work never opens this depth; for those whose does, the framework is foundational.
