## I. Welsh Primary Sources

The medieval Welsh corpus the practitioner engages with primarily lives in three textual bodies:

**The Mabinogion.** A collection of eleven medieval Welsh tales preserved in two manuscripts — the *White Book of Rhydderch* (c. 1350) and the *Red Book of Hergest* (c. 1382-1410). The four core tales — known collectively as the **Four Branches of the Mabinogi** — are: the First Branch (*Pwyll, Prince of Dyfed*), the Second Branch (*Branwen, Daughter of Llŷr*), the Third Branch (*Manawydan, Son of Llŷr*), and the Fourth Branch (*Math, Son of Mathonwy*). The Four Branches contain the densest mythological material — Rhiannon and Pryderi, Branwen and Bran, Manawydan, Math and Gwydion and Arianrhod and Blodeuwedd. The other seven tales include the Arthurian *Culhwch and Olwen* (which contains Modron), the *Tale of the Dream of Macsen Wledig*, the *Lludd and Llefelys*, three tales related to the French Arthurian romances (*Owain*, *Geraint*, *Peredur*), and the *Tale of Taliesin* in some manuscripts.

The standard scholarly translation in English is **Sioned Davies, *The Mabinogion* (Oxford World's Classics, 2007)**. Davies is Chair of Welsh at Cardiff University and her translation is the first new English translation in thirty years; it is also the first to place due emphasis on the tales' oral origins, preserving the rhythm, formulaic elements, and rhetorical passages of the originals. The Modern Language Review described it as inaugurating "a new age of Mabinogion studies." For chapter writing, this is the citation. Earlier translations (Patrick K. Ford, Jeffrey Gantz, Lady Charlotte Guest, Gwyn and Thomas Jones) remain in print and have their uses but are superseded by Davies for serious work.

**The Welsh Triads (*Trioedd Ynys Prydein*).** A group of medieval Welsh texts preserving fragments of Welsh folklore, mythology, and traditional history in groups of three, intended originally as a mnemonic catalogue for bards to recall heroic figures and narratives. The earliest manuscripts date to the thirteenth century, with the corpus continuing to grow into the fifteenth century.

The standard scholarly edition is **Rachel Bromwich, *Trioedd Ynys Prydein: The Triads of the Island of Britain* (University of Wales Press, 1961; revised editions 1978, 2006, 2014)**. Bromwich's edition presents the full Welsh text alongside English translation with extensive notes. The fourth edition (2014, with Morfydd E. Owen as co-editor) is current. The "Notes on Personal Names" — over four hundred entries — function as a working dictionary of the Welsh Heroic Age and are themselves the most useful single resource on the named figures of Welsh tradition.

**Hanes Taliesin / Llyfr Taliesin (the Taliesin material).** Two distinct bodies. The *Llyfr Taliesin* (Book of Taliesin, manuscript c. 1275) preserves ancient poetry attributed to Taliesin, including poetry addressed to Urien Rheged and his son Owain — material likely composed in the sixth century and transmitted orally before being recorded. The *Hanes Taliesin* (Tale of Taliesin, primary literary source: a 16th-century manuscript compiled by Welsh chronicler Elis Gruffydd) is the prose tale of Cerridwen brewing the potion of inspiration, the servant boy Gwion Bach, the shape-shifting chase, and Gwion's rebirth as Taliesin. The *Hanes* is the source for nearly all contemporary engagement with Cerridwen as a figure; it is also the primary source the bardic-tradition practice of "I am Taliesin" descends from.

These three bodies — Mabinogion, Triads, Hanes Taliesin — are the practitioner's primary-source spine. Beyond them: the *Black Book of Carmarthen* (c. 1250, the earliest surviving manuscript in Welsh), the *Book of Aneirin* (c. 1265, contains *Y Gododdin*), the *Red Book of Hergest* (c. 1382-1410), and the broader Welsh poetic corpus of the *Cynfeirdd* (Early Welsh poets) and *Gogynfeirdd* (Poets of the Princes). These are scholarly territory rather than entry-level practitioner reading; they become relevant if the practitioner's engagement with Welsh tradition deepens to the level where primary sources beyond the Mabinogion are needed.

## II. Welsh Feminine Divine — By Attestation

Distinguishing what's in primary sources from what's reconstructed in modern revival is editorially important. The practitioner reading contemporary Pagan publishing on Welsh deities encounters a range of claims that vary substantially in attestation. This section names what's in the medieval Welsh corpus, what's reconstructed by scholarship, and what's modern revival or invention.

### Figures attested in the medieval Welsh corpus

**Rhiannon** (First and Third Branches). The most extensively developed feminine figure in the Mabinogion. Her name derives from Brittonic *Rīgantonā ("Great Queen") via the Proto-Celtic root *rīgan- ("queen"; cf. Welsh *rhiain* "maiden," Old Irish *rígain* "queen"). She is generally accepted by Celtic scholars as inheriting from earlier deity traditions — most plausibly the Gaulish horse goddess Epona, though Ronald Hutton remains skeptical of the direct Epona-Rhiannon connection. Proinsias Mac Cana developed the major contemporary scholarly position that Rhiannon embodies the Celtic sovereignty goddess: in marrying Pwyll, she ordains him legitimate king of the territory she personifies. Catherine McKenna's work on Rhiannon as a sovereignty goddess extends Mac Cana's frame, treating both Pwyll's and Manawydan's marriages to her as legitimating their right to rule.

**Branwen** (Second Branch). Daughter of Llŷr (an elusive Welsh father deity associated with the sea) and Penarddun (whose name means "Chief of Beauty"). Sister of Bran the Blessed. She marries Matholwch, King of Ireland; his betrayal sparks war between Britain and Ireland, with catastrophic consequences. Branwen is one of the major tragic figures of the Mabinogion; her name has been associated with white-bird imagery (*bran* = "raven" or "crow," *wen* = "white" or "blessed").

**Arianrhod** (Fourth Branch). Daughter of Dôn. Mother of Lleu Llaw Gyffes and his twin Dylan ail Don. Her name means "Silver Wheel," connecting her to the moon and its phases. In the Fourth Branch she works against her son Lleu — placing three *tynged* (geis, prohibitions) on him: that he should bear no name unless she gave it, no arms unless she armed him, and no wife of any race living on earth. Gwydion her brother circumvents each prohibition through trickery (the third leading to the creation of Blodeuwedd from flowers). Arianrhod is sometimes read as a sovereignty figure or an initiatory "Terrible Mother," sometimes as a defended-against-violation figure. Her castle Caer Arianrhod (corresponding to a real off-coast island visible at low tide on the Welsh coast) is one of the named geographies of the Mabinogion.

**Blodeuwedd** (Fourth Branch). Created by Math and Gwydion from the flowers of oak, broom, and meadowsweet to be a wife for Lleu, since Arianrhod's *tynged* forbade Lleu marrying any woman of a race living on earth. Her name means either "Flower Face" or, after her transformation, "Owl" (*blodeu* = "flowers"; the owl is the form she is changed into when she conspires against Lleu). Blodeuwedd is among the most theologically interesting figures in the Mabinogion: created without consent, she develops desire and agency, attempts murder, is punished by transformation into the night-bird. Telyndru's *Pagan Portals: Blodeuwedd: Welsh Goddess of Seasonal Sovereignty* (Moon Books, 2021) treats her in contemporary devotional register.

**Cerridwen** (Hanes Taliesin). The owner of the Cauldron of Inspiration (Awen). Her name has been variously read as "White Sow" or "White Crafty One"; her epithets include Dark Moon Goddess, White Lady of Inspiration and Death, Goddess of Nature, and Grain Goddess. She brews a potion in the cauldron for one year and a day to give wisdom to her physically-deformed son Afagddu. The servant Gwion Bach steals the three drops of inspiration; Cerridwen pursues him through shape-shifting transformations; she finally consumes him as a hen swallows a grain of wheat, conceives him, and gives birth to him as the bard Taliesin. This is the single most influential narrative in modern Welsh-tradition Pagan practice; Hughes's *From the Cauldron Born* organizes a year of ritual practice around the tale.

**Modron** (Mabinogion in *Culhwch and Olwen*). Her name derives from Gaulish Matrona ("Great Mother") — the same Indo-European root that gives "matron." She is daughter of Avallach (a ruler linked to Avalon) and mother of Mabon ap Modron, the divine son who is abducted from her at three nights old and held in a dungeon until rescued by Arthur's company. Modron-Mabon is the Welsh divine mother-and-son pair, paralleling the Romano-British Maponos (god of youth), and is the textual basis for the modern naming of the autumn equinox sabbat as "Mabon" (a 1970s renaming, not a medieval attestation — the autumn equinox was not historically called Mabon).

### Figures with limited Welsh-specific attestation but adjacent

**The Cailleach.** A widespread Celtic hag-goddess associated with winter, storms, mountains, and animals during the dark months. She is well-attested in Scottish and Irish tradition — *cailleach* in modern Gaelic means "old woman" or "hag" — but her Welsh attestation is much thinner. Sharon Blackie's *Hagitude* (September Publishing, 2022) treats the Cailleach across Celtic tradition broadly and gives her a place in contemporary Welsh-adjacent practice; the practitioner working specifically in the Welsh stream should be honest that the Cailleach is more directly Scottish-Irish than Welsh.

**Brigid.** Irish goddess of poetry, smithcraft, healing, and sacred fire; continues into Christian tradition as Saint Brigid of Kildare. Mary Condren's *The Serpent and the Goddess* (Harper, 1989) is the foundational feminist-historical treatment. Brigid is principally Irish; she has Welsh syncretic resonance through the broader Celtic devotional substrate but is not a Welsh-specific deity.

**Hen Wen.** The "Old White One" — a sow figure in Welsh folklore who appears in Triad 26 and in early Welsh poetry. She is sometimes read as a sovereignty figure or as an avatar of Cerridwen (whose name means "White Sow"); the connection is reconstructive rather than directly attested.

**The Lady of the Lake.** In the Welsh-Arthurian corpus, the Lady appears as Nimue / Vivien / Niniane in various sources; the "Welsh" Lady of the Lake is a complicated figure whose attestation is mostly post-medieval and continental-Arthurian rather than Welsh-medieval.

### What is not Welsh

Several figures commonly assigned to "Celtic" practice in contemporary Pagan publishing are not Welsh: Danu (Irish, *Tuatha Dé Danann*), the Mórrígan (Irish), Aine (Irish), Macha (Irish). The pan-Celtic register collapses real distinctions; the practitioner working in the Welsh stream specifically should know which figures are Welsh and which are Irish, Scottish, Gaulish, or other.

## III. Rhiannon at Depth

For the keyed reader given the call. This section develops Rhiannon-specific material at greater depth than v10's brief mention.

### Where she appears

**The First Branch of the Mabinogi (*Pwyll, Prince of Dyfed*)**. Rhiannon enters the tale as the lady on the white horse who appears at the mound of Arberth. Pwyll attempts to overtake her — his fastest riders cannot, despite her horse moving at a leisurely pace. Eventually Pwyll asks her to stop; she does. She tells him she has been seeking him; she has been promised to another man (Gwawl) but does not want him. Pwyll and Rhiannon plot together: at her wedding feast Pwyll arrives in disguise, tricks Gwawl into the magical bag, and Rhiannon and Pwyll marry. Years later their child is born and disappears on the night of his birth. Rhiannon, framed as the murderer (the women guarding the child smear blood and bones on her while she sleeps), is sentenced to sit at the gate of Arberth for seven years and carry visitors on her back like a horse. The child has been taken by an unseen otherworldly force and is recovered years later by Teyrnon Twrf Liant, who returns him; the boy is named Pryderi. Rhiannon is restored. The First Branch is the fullest narrative we have of her.

**The Third Branch of the Mabinogi (*Manawydan, Son of Llŷr*)**. After Pwyll's death, Rhiannon marries Manawydan. The kingdom of Dyfed is enchanted; the people vanish; Rhiannon and Pryderi are caught in an enchanted otherworld and trapped wearing collars on their necks like animals — explicitly an inversion of the horse imagery from the First Branch. Manawydan, by patient cunning rather than force, eventually wins her release.

### Etymology and divine inheritance

Rhiannon's name reconstructs to Brittonic *Rīgantonā, "Great Queen" — from *rīgan- "queen" (cf. Welsh *rhiain* "maiden," modern Welsh *brenhines* "queen," Old Irish *rígain* "queen") plus the augmentative suffix *-onā found in the names of several Celtic divinities (Epona, Maponos/Modron, Damona). The reconstructed name supports the scholarly position that she is a divinity in origin: a queen-goddess parallel to other Celtic figures whose names share the *-onā formation.

The connection to the Gaulish horse goddess Epona is the major scholarly hypothesis. Epona was widely worshiped across Gaul, Roman Britain, and Germania (the only Celtic deity with attested cult in the Roman pantheon, with a feast day in the Roman calendar — December 18). Epona is depicted with horses, often feeding them or riding them; she is associated with sovereignty, fertility, and the otherworld. The Rhiannon-Epona identification (or descent) is generally accepted among Celticists; Hutton's skepticism is the major dissenting position.

### Sovereignty goddess interpretation

The dominant contemporary scholarly reading of Rhiannon — Mac Cana 1958, McKenna 1980, and many subsequent — is that she is a Celtic sovereignty figure: the goddess who personifies the territory and who, by marrying the human candidate, legitimates his right to rule. The Celtic sovereignty motif is widely attested across Irish and Welsh tradition; the goddess of the land is wedded to the king, the well-being of the land mirrors the well-being of the relationship between sovereign and goddess, and political failure tracks the failure of that sacred contract. Rhiannon's marriages to Pwyll and Manawydan, on this reading, are sovereignty unions; her suffering during the seven years at the gate of Arberth tracks the kingdom's troubled state; her restoration tracks the kingdom's restoration. Caitlín Matthews's *King Arthur and the Goddess of the Land: The Divine Feminine in the Mabinogion* (Inner Traditions, 2002) is the major contemporary practitioner-scholarly treatment of this sovereignty frame.

### The Birds of Rhiannon

In the Second Branch and the Triads, the "Birds of Rhiannon" appear as three magical birds whose song wakes the dead and lulls the living to sleep. They are mentioned in passing but never described at length in primary sources; modern Pagan revival has developed the imagery substantially beyond what's directly attested. The birds are typically read as her otherworldly aspect — connecting her to the *Annwfn* (the Welsh otherworld) and to liminal time-and-space-bending phenomena.

### The narrative arc

The shape of Rhiannon's story across the First and Third Branches is significant for devotional engagement. She arrives unbidden, choosing her own marriage. She is wronged — falsely accused of murder, punished publicly, made to perform animal labor. Years later she is restored. In the Third Branch she is trapped again and again restored. The narrative arc is sustained suffering followed by sustained restoration, with neither suffering nor restoration being final. The contemporary reading — that this is the sovereignty goddess's enduring relationship with her territory across the cycles of fortune and misfortune — is theologically dense.

### Modern Rhiannon material

**Jhenah Telyndru, *Rhiannon: Divine Queen of the Celtic Britons* (Moon Books, 2018).** The dedicated devotional-scholarly volume. Telyndru holds a master's in Celtic Studies from the University of Wales, Trinity Saint David, and founded the Sisterhood of Avalon in 1995. The book treats Rhiannon as primary devotional subject with substantial scholarly grounding.

**Kristoffer Hughes, *From the Cauldron Born* (Llewellyn, 2012)** — engages Rhiannon in the broader Welsh-tradition context of the Cerridwen-Taliesin cycle. Hughes is Chief of the Anglesey Druid Order and a Mount Haemus Scholar; his catalogue (also: *The Book of Celtic Magic*, *Cerridwen*, *As the Last Leaf Falls*) is the most substantial contemporary Welsh-tradition practitioner-scholar work in English.

**Caitlín Matthews, *King Arthur and the Goddess of the Land* (Inner Traditions, 2002).** Treats Rhiannon within the broader sovereignty-goddess frame. Matthews has been writing on Celtic tradition since the 1980s; her catalogue is extensive (the *Encyclopedia of Celtic Wisdom*, *Sophia: Goddess of Wisdom*, others). Her early book on Rhiannon (out of print) was foundational.

**Sisterhood of Avalon (sisterhoodofavalon.org).** Founded by Telyndru in 1995. Offers structured engagement with the Welsh-Brythonic goddesses including Rhiannon. International women's spiritual organization with a developed program of study, sustained community, and devotional practice.

**Anglesey Druid Order (angleseydruidorder.co.uk).** Hughes is its Chief. Operates on Anglesey (Ynys Môn), the historical center of Welsh druidic tradition. Welsh-language and Welsh-tradition emphasis.

### The shape of Rhiannon-specific devotion (provisional)

For the practitioner who has been called by Rhiannon and is developing devotional practice — drawing on Telyndru's and Hughes's treatments and on the primary-source narrative arc:

- *White horse imagery* — central. The lady on the white horse at the mound of Arberth.
- *The three birds* — present at the threshold; sleep, awakening, the otherworld.
- *Sovereignty rather than sentimentality* — Rhiannon is a queen-goddess, not a comfort figure. The relationship is contractual and serious.
- *The long-suffering-restored narrative* — devotional engagement honors the full arc, not just the sovereign queen but the wronged-and-restored queen. Patience, endurance, justice that takes a long time to arrive — these are her qualities.
- *Connection to Annwfn* — the Welsh otherworld. Rhiannon's domain is liminal.
- *Welsh language minimum engagement* — even at the level of pronunciation. *Rhi-AN-non* (the *rh* aspirated as in Welsh; first syllable lighter, second stressed).
- *Geographic anchoring if possible* — Welsh sacred sites (Anglesey, Caer Arianrhod, Welsh holy wells) become real territory for the relationship if the practitioner can visit; alternatively, the practitioner finds analogous territory in their own land.

## V. Welsh Sacred Geography

The places where the tradition is rooted matter for the practitioner — both as scholarly context and as potential pilgrimage territory if the practice ever opens to physical engagement.

**Anglesey (Ynys Môn).** Historical center of Welsh druidic tradition; Tacitus records the Roman destruction of the druidic groves on Anglesey in 60-61 CE. The island carries one of Europe's densest concentrations of prehistoric ritual sites — passage tombs, henges, standing stones, stone circles, cup-and-ring carvings. **Bryn Celli Ddu** ("Mound in the Dark Grove") is the most famous: a Late Neolithic passage tomb dating to ~5,000 years ago, and the only site in Wales with a confirmed solar alignment (the sun casts a beam of light into the monument on the summer solstice). The Anglesey Druid Order holds public summer solstice gatherings at Bryn Celli Ddu. The island is the operational base of contemporary Welsh-tradition revival.

**Caer Arianrhod.** Off the Welsh coast at Dinas Dinlle, visible at very low tide; identified in tradition as the dwelling place of Arianrhod from the Fourth Branch. A real geographic feature; the ruined coastal fort visible at low tide is named for her.

**Welsh holy wells.** The Welsh holy-well tradition is one of the most substantial folk-religious continuities in the British Isles, with hundreds of named wells across Wales preserving pre-Christian sacred-site veneration (often with Christianized saints' names overlaid). St Winefride's Well at Holywell (Treffynnon) is the most famous and continuously visited; many smaller wells across rural Wales preserve older devotional practices. Francis Jones, *The Holy Wells of Wales* (University of Wales Press, 1954) is the standard scholarly reference.

**The Mabinogion's geography.** Each branch is rooted in named Welsh territory: Dyfed (the southwest, Pwyll's kingdom; Rhiannon's mound of Arberth is in modern-day Pembrokeshire), Gwynedd (the northwest, Math's kingdom in the Fourth Branch), Anglesey (Ynys Môn, named throughout), the Severn estuary, the Welsh-English border. The tales are not abstract mythology; they are mapped to a real landscape, and Welsh practitioners working in the tradition often visit these geographies as part of their practice.

For a Welsh-diaspora practitioner without immediate access to Wales: pilgrimage when possible is the substantive engagement; alternatively, finding analogous territory in one's own land — coastal sites, holy wells, ancient tombs, places where the otherworld is felt accessible — is the methodological move. The Anglesey Druid Order and similar organizations welcome visitors at public gatherings.

## VI. The Question of Historical vs. Reconstructed

Held honestly. What's attested, what's reconstructed, what's modern revival.

**Attested in medieval Welsh manuscripts**: the Mabinogion narrative material, the Triads' catalogue of figures, the *Hanes Taliesin* prose tale, the *Llyfr Taliesin* poetry corpus, named figures and their stories at the level of the medieval sources.

**Reconstructed by modern scholarship**: the Indo-European etymological roots (e.g., *Rīgantonā), the proposed connections between Welsh and continental Celtic figures (Rhiannon-Epona, Modron-Matrona, Mabon-Maponos), the sovereignty-goddess interpretive frame (Mac Cana 1958), the proposed pre-Christian theological structure beneath the Christianized medieval material. These are scholarly arguments with substantial evidence but are not directly attested as religious doctrine in any surviving Welsh primary source.

**Contemporary revival or invention**: the eight-station wheel of the year applied to Welsh tradition (post-1950s synthesis); the naming of the autumn equinox as "Mabon" (1970s, Aidan Kelly's coinage; not medieval); the contemporary Pagan-revival theology of the Maiden-Mother-Crone triple goddess applied to Welsh figures (a 20th-century framework, not medieval Welsh); much of contemporary "Druidic" ritual structure (post-Iolo Morganwg, with substantial 19th-century invention by Edward Williams — Iolo himself); the specific contemporary devotional structures around individual deities (Hughes, Telyndru, Matthews, others — these are real working practices but they are reconstructive and contemporary, not continuous medieval transmission).

**The pre-Christian Welsh substrate is fragmentary**. Most of what we have is medieval Welsh material recorded after Christianization, in which the older religious material is preserved selectively, mediated through monastic scribes, and often refunctioned for Christianized audiences. This is the honest position the practitioner has to hold: the engagement is real, the figures are real (in whatever sense the practitioner's metaphysics admits), but the continuous-unbroken-tradition claim that some popular Pagan publishing makes is not historically defensible. The relationship to the Welsh feminine divine that contemporary practitioners develop is recovery work — substantive recovery work, but recovery rather than continuous inheritance.

## VII. Welsh-Language Scholarship and Access

English-language Pagan publishing covers a small percentage of Welsh-tradition scholarly material. The practitioner who wants to engage the depth of the tradition eventually meets the limit of English-language sources and must reckon with Welsh-language material directly.

**Cardiff University Welsh department.** Sioned Davies is Chair of Welsh; the department is the leading center of Welsh-language Mabinogion and medieval Welsh literature scholarship. Public-facing resources, occasional public lectures, and graduate program publications are accessible.

**University of Wales Centre for Advanced Welsh and Celtic Studies (Aberystwyth).** Major center of Welsh-language and broader Celtic-language scholarship. Publications include the journal *Studia Celtica*, the *Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru* (the standard Welsh-language dictionary), and substantial digital archives of Welsh manuscripts.

**National Library of Wales (Aberystwyth).** Houses the *White Book of Rhydderch*, the *Black Book of Carmarthen*, and the *Book of Aneirin* among other foundational manuscripts. Many digitized and accessible online.

**Welsh-language access for the non-Welsh-speaking practitioner**:

- **learnwelsh.cymru** (free Welsh Government resources). The standard entry for Welsh language learning, with tiered courses available without cost.
- **Duolingo Welsh.** Free, gamified, suitable for early learners. Welsh is among Duolingo's most-completed courses globally.
- **SaySomethingInWelsh** (saysomethinginwelsh.com). Audio-based Welsh language learning with substantial practitioner adoption.
- **The Welsh course at Coleg Cymraeg** (the National Welsh-Language College). For more serious engagement.

The argument for engaging the language even minimally: reading the Mabinogion with the Welsh original alongside the English translation transforms the practice. The medieval Welsh names — *Rhiannon*, *Branwen*, *Arianrhod*, *Pryderi*, *Annwfn* — are pronounced differently in Welsh than English-speakers typically render them; the tales sound different in Welsh; the formulaic phrases that mark medieval Welsh oral tradition have a sonic register that translation flattens. The practitioner who does even basic Welsh language work shifts into a different relationship with the source material.

## VIII. What Remains Unaddressed (research gaps within this gap)

Even with this deliverable, some Welsh-specific research dimensions remain open:

- **Welsh folk-magical tradition as distinct from medieval Welsh literary tradition.** Hughes's work touches it; the broader Welsh folk corpus (*tylwyth teg* lore, the Welsh witch and conjurer traditions, charm-magic in Welsh, *swynwyr* and *dyn hysbys* as historical practitioners) deserves its own engagement. The boundary between the high-medieval Mabinogion register and the rural folk-magical register is real and not always honored in contemporary Pagan publishing.

- **Welsh-language primary sources for which no good English translation yet exists.** Substantial; varies by text. Davies's *Mabinogion* is well-served; the *Llyfr Taliesin* poetry corpus less so; the broader medieval Welsh poetic tradition (the *Cynfeirdd*, the *Gogynfeirdd*, the bardic schools) has scholarly translations but few practitioner-accessible ones.

- **Welsh diaspora communities in North America** — the practitioner who is Welsh-diaspora has to figure out what diaspora practice looks like. There is no centralized Welsh-American religious tradition equivalent to, e.g., Italian-American *stregheria* communities. Diaspora Welsh practice tends to be either (a) connection back to organizations like OBOD or Sisterhood of Avalon that operate transnationally, or (b) DIY recovery work.

- **The political dimension of Welsh tradition recovery.** Welsh language and cultural recovery is a real political project in contemporary Wales (Welsh-language education, Welsh-language media, the *Cymraeg 2050* national strategy). The Welsh-tradition Pagan practitioner is implicated in and adjacent to this; the recovery of Welsh religious tradition is not separable from the recovery of Welsh cultural-political identity. This deserves its own consideration in chapter writing.

- **Contemporary Welsh-language Pagan and reconstructionist communities.** Most of what's in English publishing is the diaspora-and-Anglophone-revival work. Welsh-language Pagan and reconstructionist communities exist but are less visible to English-language research; engaging them would require Welsh language access and likely direct relationship.

These are real gaps. They are smaller than the gap this document closes, but they are worth flagging for chapter writers.
