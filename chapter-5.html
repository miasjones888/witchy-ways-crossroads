§ V.I the feminine divine

A survey across traditions

The feminine divine across the world's religious traditions is plural, richly attested, and substantially more developed than the standard male-centered religious-historical canon presents. This section surveys figures across multiple traditions as orientation to the territory. Read as a map; notice what calls. None requires commitment.

The recovery of the feminine divine has been one of the central projects of women's spirituality and feminist religious scholarship across the last sixty years. The work of Carol P. Christ, Charlene Spretnak, Marija Gimbutas, Riane Eisler, Asphodel Long, Rosemary Radford Ruether, Elaine Pagels, and the broader goddess-spirituality movement has substantially recovered, reconstructed, and re-articulated traditions that the male-dominated academic religious studies of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries had marginalized or dismissed. The work continues across contemporary writers — Sharon Blackie, Sophie Strand, Mirabai Starr, Christena Cleveland, Rachel Pollack, Caitlín Matthews, Jhenah Telyndru — and across you traditions that have always carried the lineages disproportionately.

The Greek and Mediterranean

Hekate — pre-Olympian goddess of crossroads, doorways, the dead, the dark moon, and witchcraft. Among the most extensively documented deities in the Greek world. Hesiod's Theogony 411–452 is the foundational primary text. Sarah Iles Johnston's Restless Dead: Encounters between the Living and the Dead in Ancient Greece (Univ. of California, 1999) and Hekate Soteira (Scholars Press, 1990) are the standard scholarly treatments. Sorita d'Este's Hekate Liminal Rites (Avalonia, 2009) and the Circle for Hekate series (Avalonia, 2017–) are the contemporary devotional texts. The contemporary chthonic-Hekatean practitioner literature is unusually rich; Hekate is one of the figures most accessible through serious scholarship, which is part of why she appears across the document as a recurring example. This is reference, not prescription. Your actual figures, if any, emerge from the practice itself.

Persephone — the descended queen of the underworld, the Kore who eats the pomegranate. Her cult at Eleusis was the principal mystery tradition of the Greek world. Christine Downing's edited anthology The Long Journey Home (Spring, 1994) is the foundational women's-tradition collection. Sylvia Brinton Perera's Descent to the Goddess: A Way of Initiation for Women (Inner City, 1981) reads the descent through the parallel Sumerian myth of Inanna and is one of the most influential women's-spirituality texts on the descended feminine. Helene Foley's translation of the Homeric Hymn to Demeter (Princeton, 1994) is the standard primary source.

Demeter — the great mother in her grief-stricken seeking, the goddess of grain and the cycles of life and death. The Eleusinian mother. Charlene Spretnak's Lost Goddesses of Early Greece (Beacon, 1981) recovers the pre-patriarchal Demeter mythology that classical Greek religion partially overwrote.

Artemis — the wild huntress, protector of women in childbirth, the unmarried virgin who chooses her own company. Jean Shinoda Bolen's Artemis: The Indomitable Spirit in Everywoman (Conari, 2014) treats her in the contemporary depth-psychological register. Ginette Paris's Pagan Meditations (Spring, 1986) is the major archetypal-psychological treatment.

Aphrodite — far more substantial than the popular reduction to "love goddess." Goddess of generative power, of beauty as religious experience, of the unmanageable. Paris's Pagan Grace (Spring, 1990) develops the feminist-archetypal treatment.

The Celtic and Northern European

Brigid — Irish goddess of poetry, smithcraft, healing, and sacred fire. Continues into Christian tradition as Saint Brigid of Kildare; the syncretic figure is one of the most living folk-devotional figures in contemporary Celtic spirituality. Mary Condren's The Serpent and the Goddess: Women, Religion, and Power in Celtic Ireland (Harper, 1989) is the foundational feminist-historical treatment. Erynn Rowan Laurie's A Circle of Stones (Eschaton, 1995) and Sharon Blackie's broader work develop her in contemporary practice.

The Cailleach — the Old Woman of the Celtic and Scottish tradition, the winter hag, the maker of mountains, the dark sister of Brigid. Welsh attestation is thinner than Scottish or Irish — the Cailleach is more directly a Scottish-Irish figure, with Welsh resonance through the broader Celtic substrate. Sharon Blackie's Hagitude: Reimagining the Second Half of Life (September Publishing, 2022) treats the Cailleach across European tradition.

The Mórrígan — Irish triple-goddess of war, sovereignty, and prophecy. One of the most powerful chthonic-warrior feminine figures in European tradition. Morpheus Ravenna's The Book of the Great Queen (Concrescent, 2015) is the major contemporary devotional-scholarly treatment.

The Welsh figures specifically

The Welsh feminine divine is its own substantive territory, distinct from the broader pan-Celtic register. The principal figures attested in the medieval Welsh corpus, primarily through the Mabinogion (Sioned Davies trans., Oxford 2007 — the standard contemporary English translation) and the Welsh Triads (Bromwich, Trioedd Ynys Prydein, University of Wales Press, 1961/2014, 4th ed. with Owen as co-editor):

Rhiannon — sovereignty goddess of the First and Third Branches of the Mabinogi. Her name reconstructs to Brittonic *Rīgantonā ("Great Queen") via the Proto-Celtic root *rīgan-. Generally accepted by Celtic scholars as inheriting from the Gaulish horse goddess Epona; Hutton skeptical of the direct connection. Mac Cana 1958 and McKenna 1980 develop the major scholarly reading of Rhiannon as Celtic sovereignty figure: marrying Pwyll, she ordains him legitimate king of the territory she personifies. The narrative arc across the First Branch — courtship, marriage, the lost child, the wrongful accusation, the seven years of public punishment, the eventual restoration — is the foundational Welsh material on suffering-and-restored sovereignty. Caitlín Matthews's King Arthur and the Goddess of the Land (Inner Traditions, 2002) treats her in the sovereignty-goddess frame substantively. Jhenah Telyndru's Rhiannon: Divine Queen of the Celtic Britons (Moon Books, 2018) is the dedicated contemporary devotional-scholarly volume; Telyndru holds an MA in Celtic Studies from the University of Wales, Trinity Saint David, and founded the Sisterhood of Avalon. For you given Mia's call: the Sisterhood of Avalon's Seeds of Devotion immersion focuses specifically on Rhiannon, Branwen, Arianrhod, Blodeuwedd, and Cerridwen. This is direct fit.

Branwen — Daughter of Llŷr, sister of Bran the Blessed, central to the Second Branch. Her marriage to Matholwch of Ireland and his betrayal sparks war between Britain and Ireland. Tragic figure; her name has been associated with white-bird imagery (bran = "raven" or "crow," wen = "white" or "blessed").

Arianrhod — "Silver Wheel," daughter of Dôn, mother of Lleu Llaw Gyffes. Connected to the moon and its phases. In the Fourth Branch she places three tynged (geis, prohibitions) on her son Lleu, which Gwydion circumvents through trickery. Sometimes read as a sovereignty figure, sometimes as initiatory "Terrible Mother." Her castle Caer Arianrhod corresponds to a real off-coast island visible at low tide on the Welsh coast.

Blodeuwedd — Created from the flowers of oak, broom, and meadowsweet to be a wife for Lleu when Arianrhod's tynged forbade marriage to any woman of a race living on earth. Her name means either "Flower Face" or "Owl" — the form she is changed into when she conspires against Lleu. Among the most theologically interesting figures in the Mabinogion: created without consent, develops desire and agency, attempts murder, is punished by transformation. Telyndru's Pagan Portals: Blodeuwedd: Welsh Goddess of Seasonal Sovereignty (Moon Books, 2021) treats her in contemporary devotional register.

Cerridwen — Owner of the Cauldron of Inspiration (Awen). The Hanes Taliesin tells of her brewing a year-and-a-day potion for her son Afagddu; the servant Gwion Bach steals the three drops; she pursues him through shape-shifting; she finally consumes him as a hen swallows wheat, conceives him, gives birth to him as the bard Taliesin. The most influential narrative in modern Welsh-tradition Pagan practice. Kristoffer Hughes's From the Cauldron Born (Llewellyn, 2012) organizes a year of ritual practice around the tale.

The Mesopotamian and Near Eastern

Inanna / Ishtar — the Sumerian and later Babylonian queen of heaven, goddess of love, war, and the descent. The Inanna descent-myth (preserved in cuneiform tablets from the third millennium BCE) is among the oldest written religious texts in the world and is the foundational text for the descended-feminine archetype across Western tradition. Diane Wolkstein and Samuel Noah Kramer's Inanna, Queen of Heaven and Earth (Harper, 1983) is the standard English translation with substantial commentary. Sylvia Brinton Perera's Descent to the Goddess reads the descent psychologically.

Lilith — the figure who has accumulated across Mesopotamian, Hebrew, and contemporary feminist tradition. From the wind-demoness of Sumerian incantation, through the rabbinic figure of Adam's first wife who refused subordination, to the contemporary feminist icon of women's autonomy and refusal. Judith Plaskow's The Coming of Lilith (Beacon, 2005) collects the Jewish feminist reclamation; Barbara Black Koltuv's The Book of Lilith (Nicolas-Hays, 1986) treats her psychologically.

The Christian and Jewish-Hermetic

The Black Madonna — the dark-skinned Marian images found across Mediterranean and European Catholic devotion (Czestochowa, Montserrat, Le Puy, hundreds of others). Long understood within feminist and Jungian scholarship as the Christian continuation of older underworld goddess cult — Demeter, Cybele, Isis. China Galland's Longing for Darkness: Tara and the Black Madonna (Penguin, 1990) is the foundational popular treatment. Lucia Chiavola Birnbaum's Black Madonnas (Northeastern, 1993) is the major academic treatment.

Mary Magdalene and the folk Catholic Marys — Mary Magdalene as the apostle to the apostles (recovered substantially by feminist scholarship in the last forty years), the various local Marys of folk devotion, the indigenous-syncretic Marys of Latin American and African Diaspora traditions (Guadalupe being the most widely venerated). Cynthia Bourgeault's The Meaning of Mary Magdalene (Shambhala, 2010) and Susan Haskins's Mary Magdalen: Myth and Metaphor (Riverhead, 1993) develop the Magdalene tradition. Sophie Strand's The Madonna Secret (Bear, 2023) does the contemporary mythopoetic work.

Sophia and the Shekhinah — the feminine divine wisdom in the Jewish and Christian-Hermetic traditions. Sophia (Greek for wisdom) appears in the Wisdom literature of the Hebrew Bible and is developed extensively in Gnostic, Hermetic, and Russian Orthodox theology. The Shekhinah is the feminine indwelling presence of God in Jewish mystical tradition, particularly Kabbalah. Caitlín Matthews's Sophia: Goddess of Wisdom, Bride of God (Quest, 2001) is the standard cross-traditional treatment. Raphael Patai's The Hebrew Goddess (Wayne State, 1990) remains foundational for the Jewish material.

The traditions named with cultural-engagement caveats

The feminine divine in indigenous and African Diaspora traditions deserves naming but cannot be entered through solo book-practice. Yemaya / Yemoja in the Yoruba and Lucumí traditions, Oshun, Oya, the various Iyami; the goddesses of Vodou (Erzulie in her many faces); the indigenous American goddesses across many traditions (the various Corn Mothers, Spider Woman, the Earth Mother in her countless local names); Kali, Durga, Sarasvati, Lakshmi, the broader Hindu feminine divine; Tara in her many forms across Tibetan Buddhism. These are real, living traditions held by living lineages with their own initiation requirements. Read about them as part of your education. Do not approach them as practice without lineage authorization. China Galland's Longing for Darkness models the appropriate stance for a Western practitioner — sustained study and reverent engagement without claiming initiation.

§ V.II sigil and image

Making as magic

Sigil work as practiced in serious witchcraft has a specific technical lineage and is one of the most direct vectors by which Mia's professional practice as a designer and maker is already adjacent to the work.

The technique now generically called sigil magic was developed by the British artist-occultist Austin Osman Spare (1886–1956) and articulated in The Book of Pleasure (1913). Spare's method was a deliberate attempt to bypass elaborate ceremonial apparatus in favor of a direct technique grounded in his own theory of the unconscious. The technique was systematized into modern chaos magic by Peter Carroll and Phil Hine in the 1980s and 1990s. Behind both lies the deeper lineage of the charactēres — the abstract glyphs of the Greek Magical Papyri, used in essentially the same way two millennia earlier.

The classical Sparean method: formulate desire as a sentence of intent. Strike out repeating letters. Recombine remaining letters into an abstract glyph. Charge through what Spare called the death posture — physiological exhaustion, sexual climax, sustained attention to the point of mental quietness. Forget the original intent; the sigil now operates without conscious interference. The forgetting is the point — the conscious mind's interference with the operation is what the technique works around.

For a designer-and-maker, the sigil practice converges with professional skill in a way that few other witchcraft practices do. The same eye that reads typography reads sigils; the same hand that designs commercial work makes the glyphs; the same skill in encoding meaning in form is the operative magical capacity. Over years of practice, the boundary between design work and sigil work will dissolve in productive ways. Your sigil archive becomes, eventually, an art practice that is also an operative magical practice.

→ the sigil practice and the design practice are the same eye, the same hand. do not separate them.

§ V.III tarot and divination

The years-long study

Tarot is a serious symbolic-philosophical system with a four-hundred-year European history and roughly a century of integration into Western occult practice. Treated seriously, it is a multi-decade discipline; treated casually, it produces the kind of generic "card of the day" reading that contemporary popular witchcraft trades in. The serious version asks for the long study.

The structure to know: the standard tarot deck has 78 cards — 22 Major Arcana (the named trumps, often read as a Hero's Journey or alchemical sequence), and 56 Minor Arcana divided into four suits (Wands, Cups, Swords, Pentacles), each with ten numbered cards and four court cards. The Rider-Waite-Smith deck (1909, illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith under A.E. Waite's direction) is the contemporary standard and the one most modern tarot literature is keyed to.

The Pamela Colman Smith recovery matters editorially. Smith was a Black-Jamaican-British artist who actually drew the deck; her contributions were historically minimized in favor of Waite. The deck is increasingly being called the Waite-Smith or Smith-Waite deck. Stuart Kaplan's Pamela Colman Smith: The Untold Story (US Games, 2018) is the major recovery of her life and work. The historiographical correction matters because Smith's specific iconographic decisions are what make the deck operative in the way it is — every card you pull is a Smith image first, with Waite's titling secondary.

The daily card pull (introduced in chapter 2 as part of the practice rhythm) deepens substantially over years. By the third year, you have pulled 800–1000 cards across the daily practice; the personal relationship with each of the 78 cards has begun developing. The book-knowledge is preliminary; the lived relationship is the practice. Pollack's Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom (Weiser, 1980/2007) is the canonical depth-text — read slowly, one card at a time, as a years-long study.

The three-card weekly pull (also introduced in chapter 2) develops different work. The discipline that builds divinatory accuracy is the review at the end of the week, not the pulling at the beginning. Over years, your own developed intuition about what a card means in her specific life builds — what the Tower means when it appears in her week, what the Three of Swords keeps surfacing for, how the Court Cards behave in her practice. This is the personal book of cards you build, distinct from any author's published interpretation.

What follows is a map, not a curriculum. Six tradition-clusters that Western magical and devotional practice contains are surveyed here, organized roughly by family — the Hellenic and chthonic stream first (closest to the document's center), then traditional witchcraft (Cochranian and Sabbatic), then cunning-folk and European folk-magical, then Wicca and modern witchcraft revivals, then ceremonial magic and the Hermetic tradition, then chaos magic and the eclectic. The Hellenic and chthonic traditions

The Greek and Greco-Roman religious traditions — including their late-Platonic theurgic developments and their continuing devotional revival in contemporary Hellenic polytheism — are among the most textually rich and continuously documented of the Western religious lineages. The chthonic stream within them (Hekate, Persephone, Demeter, the Eleusinian mysteries, the cult of the dead) is a particular emphasis. Contemporary practitioners often work in either the broader Hellenic devotional register or the more specifically chthonic register.

What distinguishes the tradition: a substantial primary-source base in Greek and Roman literature; the late-Platonic theurgic philosophical apparatus that gives it theological depth; a continuous devotional revival from the nineteenth century forward; and an emphasis on relationship with named, distinct deities rather than on abstract magical operation. The chthonic emphasis specifically takes death, the dead, and the underworld as central religious territory. Working with the dead is a developed devotional practice in this tradition, appropriately approached after years of broader practice.

Reading: Sarah Iles Johnston Restless Dead and Hekate Soteira; Christine Downing The Long Journey Home; Sylvia Brinton Perera Descent to the Goddess; Helene Foley The Homeric Hymn to Demeter; Charlene Spretnak Lost Goddesses of Early Greece; Ginette Paris Pagan Meditations and Pagan Grace; Sorita d'Este Hekate Liminal Rites; Walter Burkert Greek Religion as scholarly synthesis (read alongside the women-authored interpretive sources rather than as primary frame).

Traditional witchcraft — Cochranian and Sabbatic

Traditional witchcraft as a self-conscious contemporary current descends from Robert Cochrane (1931–1966) and the Clan of Tubal Cain, distinguished from Wicca by its emphasis on chthonic deity-work, the witches' sabbath as visionary state, spirit-work with the dead and with familiar spirits, and an aesthetic that draws from cunning-folk and folk-magical practice rather than from Wiccan ceremonial structure. The current was elaborated through the late twentieth century by Andrew Chumbley (1967–2004) and Daniel Schulke as the Sabbatic Craft — the most theologically and operatively developed contemporary traditional-witchcraft lineage.

What distinguishes the tradition: a darker theological register than Wicca, with chthonic and "left-hand" deities at the center; an emphasis on initiation, spirit-allies, and visionary practice; a serious operative magical tradition drawing on cunning-folk inheritance and ceremonial magic; a small-press literature (Scarlet Imprint, Three Hands Press, Xoanon) with substantial intellectual depth; and a generally austere aesthetic distinct from the popular witchcraft register. Some of the most serious contemporary witchcraft writing is in this stream.

Reading: Gemma Gary Traditional Witchcraft: A Cornish Book of Ways (Troy Books, 2008) — the most accessible entry from a working practitioner; Shani Oates Tubelo's Green Fire (Mandrake of Oxford, 2010) — Oates is the current Maid of the Clan of Tubal Cain, the most theologically substantive Cochranian text in print; Nigel Pearson Treading the Mill (Capall Bann, 2007); Peter Grey and Alkistis Dimech Apocalyptic Witchcraft (Scarlet Imprint, 2013) — Scarlet Imprint is built equally by Dimech, whose ritual choreography and visual practice are inseparable from the press's body of work; the archived writings of Sarah Anne Lawless (sarahannelawless.com, archived); the foundational male-authored Cochranian and Sabbatic texts (Cochrane, Chumbley) — read after the contemporary women practitioners who are continuing and developing the tradition.

Cunning-folk and European folk-magical traditions

The cunning-folk tradition — the documented historical practice of professional folk-magical practitioners across Britain and continental Europe — is the historical substrate from which much of contemporary traditional witchcraft descends. Distinct from both the witch-trial-era theological construct and the modern revived witchcraft, cunning-folk practice is what the practitioners actually did: healing, divination, breaking ill-wishing, charm-magic, herbal work, communication with the dead. It survived continuously into the early twentieth century and has been substantially recovered by contemporary scholarship (Wilby, Davies, Lecouteux).

The broader European folk-magical traditions — Italian stregheria, Slavic vedma traditions, Iberian and Mexican folk-Catholic magic, Irish folk practice, Norse and Germanic folk magic — each have their own distinct histories, primary sources, and contemporary practitioner communities. Engagement typically benefits from cultural and ancestral connection (Mia's Welsh matrilineal line opens specific traditions) but does not require it; many traditions have substantial published material in English.

Reading: Sabina Magliocco Witching Culture: Folklore and Neo-Paganism in America (Univ. of Pennsylvania, 2004); Emma Wilby Cunning Folk and Familiar Spirits (Sussex Academic, 2005) and The Visions of Isobel Gowdie (Sussex Academic, 2010); Mary-Grace Fahrun Italian Folk Magic (Weiser, 2018); Owen Davies Popular Magic: Cunning-folk in English History (Hambledon, 2003); Claude Lecouteux's continuing catalogue (The Tradition of Household Spirits, The Return of the Dead, The Book of Grimoires); Charles Leland Aradia, or the Gospel of the Witches (1899) — read with critical context as historical artifact alongside Magliocco's contemporary academic treatment.

Wicca and modern witchcraft revivals

Modern Wicca was constructed in the 1940s and 1950s by Gerald Gardner, drawing on Margaret Murray's (now-discredited) thesis of a surviving European witch-cult, ceremonial magic from the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, folk practice, and Gardner's own creative synthesis. The tradition has since branched substantially — Gardnerian, Alexandrian, Dianic, Reclaiming, eclectic, solitary — and has become the most widely practiced contemporary Western Pagan religion.

What distinguishes the tradition: a duotheistic theology centered on the God and Goddess; a structured liturgical calendar of eight sabbats and twelve to thirteen esbats; a circle-casting ritual structure derived from ceremonial magic; a strong emphasis on initiation, lineage transmission, and coven structure (in the traditional forms) or on personal eclectic practice (in the solitary forms); a generally celebratory, life-affirming theological orientation in contrast to the chthonic stream. Wicca is the most accessible entry into structured Western Pagan practice and has the largest contemporary literature, but the literature is uneven — much of the popular Wiccan publishing is the contemporary self-help register the document warned against early.

Ceremonial magic and the Hermetic tradition

Western ceremonial magic descends from a long lineage — the late-antique Greco-Egyptian magical papyri, medieval grimoiric tradition, Renaissance Hermeticism (Marsilio Ficino, Pico della Mirandola, Cornelius Agrippa), the Rosicrucian tradition, and the Victorian-era synthesis in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (1888–1903). Contemporary ceremonial magic largely descends from the Golden Dawn, with substantial branches through Aleister Crowley's Thelema and the various derivative orders (OTO, A∴A∴).

What distinguishes the tradition: a highly structured ritual apparatus (banishing rituals, invocations, evocations, planetary and elemental correspondences); substantial integration with Kabbalah, astrology, and alchemy; an initiatory grade system; and a generally cosmological and theurgic frame. Ceremonial magic and witchcraft have substantial historical overlap — modern Wicca borrowed extensively from Golden Dawn ritual structure — but they sit in distinguishable registers. The ceremonial tradition is more elaborate, more formal, more philosophically systematized; the witchcraft tradition is more direct, more practical, more spirit-relational.

Chaos magic and the eclectic tradition

Chaos magic is a deliberately syncretic, post-modern tradition developed in the late 1970s and 1980s primarily through Peter Carroll and Phil Hine, drawing on Austin Osman Spare's earlier work. Distinguished by its operational rather than devotional orientation — you adopts and discards belief systems pragmatically, evaluating each by what it produces rather than by what it commits to — chaos magic has been substantially influential on contemporary witchcraft and ceremonial practice without being a tradition most practitioners commit to as a primary identity.

What distinguishes the tradition: a strong emphasis on technique over theology; the use of "paradigm shift" as deliberate practice (working in different magical frameworks for different operations); sigil magic as central technique; a generally non-devotional, non-deity-relational orientation; and an irreverent, often deliberately transgressive aesthetic. Useful as a body of technique for any practitioner regardless of primary tradition; less useful as a sole devotional commitment because it does not develop the depth of relational practice that the other traditions specialize in. The witchcraft animism specifically

Chapter 4 introduced animism as foundational philosophical position and developed the land sit, plant introduction, and animal augury as practitioner-level work. Chapter 5 develops the witchcraft-specific animism — the familiar-spirit tradition, the developed plant-spirit relationships, the particular forms of animal communication that the traditional craft tradition documents.

The familiar spirit tradition — documented across European cunning-folk practice, treated extensively in Emma Wilby's Cunning Folk and Familiar Spirits — is animist relationship formalized into devotional and operative practice. Historical witches and cunning-folk worked with specific spirit-allies, often in animal form (the toad, the cat, the hare, the fox, the raven), with whom they had developed long-term reciprocal relationships. The familiar was not a "tool" or a "magical assistant" in the modern sense; it was a person across the threshold with whom the witch had a working relationship.

Plant-spirit work — the relationship with specific plants as persons with their own agency, intelligence, and willingness or refusal to participate in the witch's work — is documented throughout the European folk-magical tradition and has substantial contemporary practitioner literature. The witch does not simply "use" mugwort or yarrow or vervain; the witch is in relationship with these plant-persons, asks their participation, makes offerings, listens to their refusal. By year three, if you has been doing the plant introduction practice consistently has developed a small group of plant-allies — five, ten, fifteen — with whom she has actual ongoing relationships. This is your herbal. It is not learned from books; it is grown.

Animal communication, in the witchcraft register, runs from the most conservative — reading the appearance of certain animals as omen or message, in the long tradition of augury documented from Etruscan and Roman religion through European folk practice — to the more substantive: developed working relationships with specific animal-persons. The contemporary practitioner-quality material exists but requires careful selection. Beyerl's The Master Book of Herbalism and the broader plant-spirit-medicine literature (Eliot Cowan's Plant Spirit Medicine, 1995) develop the practice; the appropriation considerations from chapter 4 apply.

For your San Diego context specifically: the chaparral and coastal sage scrub plant communities offer their own substantial witchcraft-traditional working ground. Black sage, white sage (sourced ethically), California sagebrush, yerba santa, mugwort (which is both a European tradition plant and has California-native varieties), yarrow, all the native salvias. Over years, you build her own working relationship with the plants of her actual land — the European-witchcraft-traditional plants alongside the California-native plants, with the appropriate care about appropriation and ethical sourcing throughout.

§ V.VI soul-memory · with explicit cptsd bracketing

Past lives and the long inventory — held with care

This section requires explicit framing before substantive engagement. For a structurally-dissociative body — and you carry CPTSD, which the contemporary clinical literature increasingly understands as a structural-dissociation condition (van der Hart, Nijenhuis, Steele, The Haunted Self, Norton 2006; Janina Fisher, Healing the Fragmented Selves of Trauma Survivors, Routledge 2017) — the patterns that surface in soul-memory inventory may not be past-life material in any sense the popular literature presumes. You cannot reliably distinguish these alone. The discernment between recurring patterns that are inheritance, recurring patterns that are dissociative parts, recurring patterns that are intergenerational trauma surfacing, and recurring patterns that are past-life material in any meaningful sense is work that requires sustained relationship with a practitioner trained in trauma-aware parts work — IFS (Schwartz), Sensorimotor Psychotherapy (Ogden), or the structural-dissociation framework (Fisher, van der Hart). Your shortlist built in chapter 2 is the first place this work happens.

This bracketing is not an argument against engaging the work. It is an argument for engaging the work with practitioner support, not in the grimoire alone. The pull inventory and dream attention practices developed below are appropriate for a CPTSD practitioner working with practitioner support; they are not appropriate as solo book-practice for a body that has not been substantively held in relationship with a trauma-aware clinician. The framework Foor's stages two through four require — practitioner support — applies equally here. Soul-memory work and ancestral excavation work belong in the same bracketed register.

With that bracketing held: the frameworks for what is popularly called past-life work are more numerous and more historically contested than the popular literature suggests. A serious engagement requires distinguishing between substantially different traditions — the Indic doctrine of samsara, the Western esoteric reincarnation literature, the clinical-empirical research at the University of Virginia, and the witchcraft framings of soul-memory and karmic continuity. Each makes different metaphysical claims and yields different practices.

The clinical-empirical literature is substantially more rigorous than is commonly recognized. Ian Stevenson, professor of psychiatry at the University of Virginia, spent forty years documenting cases of children reporting past-life memories — over 2,500 cases catalogued with substantial methodological care. His successor Jim Tucker continues the work. Whatever the metaphysics, the corpus is serious. Tom Shroder's Old Souls (Simon & Schuster, 1999) is the journalistic introduction; Stevenson's Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation (Univ. of Virginia, 1966/1974) is the foundational academic text — methodologically conservative; Stevenson does not conclude reincarnation, only that the cases suggest it as one possible explanation. Plato's Republic Book 10 (the Myth of Er) is the foundational Western philosophical text on the soul's choice of subsequent incarnations.

The pull inventory practice (introduced as soul-memory entry in chapter 4 — flagged then as work that "begins in the first year" but should be understood now as work that you brings to her clinician alongside her own attention, not work she does in isolation) develops across years. By year three, patterns may be visible — recurring places, periods, languages, configurations. The pattern is the data, regardless of metaphysical interpretation. Whether you read the patterns as past lives in the literal sense, as ancestral memory, as dissociative-trauma material the body has been holding, as archetypal-imaginative material — the discernment is the work, and the discernment requires the clinical relationship.

The dream attention practice — recording dreams from waning crescent and balsamic moon phases that present as past-life material — accumulates similarly. Specific historical periods, places, configurations recur. You build a reading file for the historical periods, places, and cultures that recur; reads the histories; looks at the images; visits if possible. And she brings the recurring material to her practitioner, who can help her distinguish what is metabolizable through study and pilgrimage from what is metabolizable only through clinical work. This is the metabolizing work, regardless of metaphysical interpretation. The CPTSD body's particular structural needs are part of the metabolizing.

§ V.VII ancestral work

The lineages, recovered honestly

Ancestral veneration is among the most widely attested ritual practices in human cultures and one of the few areas where contemporary witchcraft engages with a continuous, cross-cultural, and well-documented practice. The serious literature is correspondingly substantial.

For solo practice in a Western context without an intact lineage tradition, the contemporary methodology is largely Daniel Foor's. Foor is a clinical psychologist (PsyD) trained both academically and through apprenticeship in Yoruba ancestral practice with Malidoma Patrice Somé. His framework is structured, conservative, trauma-aware, and explicitly designed for solo work. The Ancestral Medicine network (ancestralmedicine.org) now has nearly 300 trained practitioners globally; you directory permits filtering by specialization including settler-colonial inheritance, racialized intergenerational trauma, and slavery-and-enslaver inheritance specifically. The serious version of ancestral work begins with reading Ancestral Medicine: Rituals for Personal and Family Healing (Bear & Co., 2017).

Foor's methodology proceeds in stages. Stage one: relationship with one well ancestor — at least three generations back, beyond living memory of immediate trauma, who feels bright and resourced when sat with. Stage one is solo-appropriate. Stages two through four work the broader lineage and require Foor-trained practitioner support; they are not appropriate for solo book-practice. Your lineage configuration — descended from both the enslaved and (through her father's matrilineal Dutch ancestor) the enslaver — is precisely the kind of complex inheritance configuration the Foor methodology stages two through four are designed to engage with practitioner support. The work is real; the support is necessary.

The genealogical research dimension runs alongside. Across years, you build a record of her matrilineal line as far back as she can trace — names, dates, locations, occupations, what they survived. Ancestry, FamilySearch, family records, oral history. Yoruba traditional religion and the orisha tradition. Yoruba religious-magical tradition is one of the substantive world religions, practiced today by tens of millions of people in West Africa (primarily Nigeria and Benin) and its substantial Diasporic descendants in the Americas. The orisha (the deities) — Olódùmarè (supreme being), Èṣù-Elegba (trickster, gatekeeper, divinatory orisha), Ọbatala (creation, wisdom, white-cloth orisha), Yemoja (sea, motherhood, primal-water orisha), Òṣun (sweet water, love, prosperity, beauty), Ọya (winds, storms, the river Niger, transformation, the dead), Ṣàngó (thunder, lightning, kingship, fire, justice), Ogún (iron, war, technology, the road), Babalú-Ayé (illness, healing, smallpox-and-AIDS specifically in contemporary Diaspora), the substantial broader pantheon — are not metaphor and are not pagan-pantheon-survey material. They are religious figures within active religion. Substantive engagement requires initiation through the religion's apparatus, typically through one of the lineage-traditions in the Diaspora (Lukumí/Santería in Cuba and the United States, Candomblé in Brazil, the various Yoruba-tradition initiatory lineages in West Africa, the contemporary North American Yoruba reconstructionist movement). The accessible non-initiate engagement is: reading substantively about the religion from primary-voice authoritative sources (Wande Abimbola, Awo Fa'lokun Fatunmbi, Bolaji Idowu, the substantial Yoruba-scholarly literature); cultural-historical literacy on the Yoruba religious-philosophical tradition; finding teachers through legitimate channels (the Yoruba diasporic religious communities have ways of accepting interested students; the path to formal initiation typically takes years of preparatory engagement). The Yoruba practitioner-communities in the United States are substantial — particularly in New York, Miami, Los Angeles, and the Bay Area; finding teachers requires sustained relational work, not aesthetic engagement. For your lineage specifically: if Yoruba ancestry is documented through the genealogical research, the path to initiation can be substantively direct; if the lineage is documented as West African but not specifically Yoruba, the appropriate engagement is with the actual ancestral tradition rather than borrowed Yoruba practice.
