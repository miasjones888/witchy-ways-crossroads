## I. Position — Two Interlocked Late-Practice Questions

The handoff document, v10's framing, and the chapter-outline architecture all point to two questions that come up in late practice and that the document needs to engage honestly.

The first: how does the practitioner teach. Not whether she should teach, not whether she has earned the right (she has, eventually, by virtue of sustained practice across decades). How. The published literature on the kind of teaching the document points toward — being kept aside as resource for specific practitioners who eventually find their way to her, rather than running workshops or seeking certification — is thin. The literature on workshop-leader teaching is substantial and not directly relevant. The literature on initiated-tradition transmission within specific lineages is substantive but is bounded by lineage. The cross-tradition contemporary literature on the kept-aside model specifically is sparse.

The second: how does the practitioner continue the work of repair across decades. The work of repair has been developed in the Continental Hermetic-Masonic deliverable as the response to the violence at the origin of the Hermetic-Masonic and West African Diaspora streams in the keyed reader's body. The cross-stream consolidation — how all three lineage streams contribute to a sustained repair practice over decades — is what this deliverable adds.

Both questions are about what the practitioner gives back. Late practice produces capacity that early practice did not have; the question is what she does with it. The traditions converge on the answer: she teaches by being available, and she repairs by sustaining the practice of repair across years until the practice itself becomes a kind of repair the world receives.

## II. The Kinds of Teaching the Document Points Toward

The contemporary American spirituality marketplace is heavy with workshop-leader teaching. The model: a teacher develops a methodology, builds a brand, runs workshops and intensives, certifies graduates, scales reach. This is the model of the contemporary American Pagan workshop circuit (PantheaCon-and-successors), the somatic-trauma certification networks, the contemporary witchcraft publishing-and-teaching economy. It has its uses and is not without integrity in many cases.

The document points toward something different. The model: an experienced practitioner is kept aside as a resource for specific other practitioners who, over years, find their way to her by some combination of recognition, recommendation, and encounter. She does not seek students; she does not run workshops; she does not certify. She is available to the people who ask. Her teaching happens in particular conversations, in extended correspondence, in occasional formal apprenticeship that is itself rare. The model is older than the contemporary American workshop tradition and is closer to how teaching happened in lineage traditions across most of human history — from master to apprentice through intimate meetings, slowly, with the time necessary for each lesson to mature.

This model has thin published literature for several reasons. Practitioners who operate in this register tend not to publish about their teaching method (they are working with specific people in specific contexts, not building public-facing curriculum). The contemporary publishing economy rewards workshop-leader teaching and underweights kept-aside teaching. The specific question of late-practice transmission within hybrid-lineage contemporary practice (the keyed reader's situation) does not have a developed published literature; the chapter writing will participate in articulating it.

The closest contemporary published reference: **Parker J. Palmer, *The Courage to Teach: Exploring the Inner Landscape of a Teacher's Life* (Jossey-Bass, 1998; 10th anniversary edition 2007).** Palmer's argument: good teaching cannot be reduced to technique, is rooted in the identity and integrity of the teacher, and is fundamentally about guiding students on an inner journey toward more truthful ways of seeing and being in the world. Palmer's broader catalogue (*Let Your Life Speak*, 2000; *A Hidden Wholeness*, 2004; *On the Brink of Everything*, 2018) develops the elder-as-teacher frame across the arc of a life. Palmer was given the Shalem Institute's Contemplative Voices Award in 2017 for his contributions to contemplative understanding, living, and leadership. The Center for Courage & Renewal continues his teaching tradition.

Palmer is the closest contemporary cross-tradition reference but is not specific to the keyed reader's lineage configuration. The lineage-specific teaching models, developed below by stream, contribute additional material.

## III. Welsh Tradition Transmission — The Bardic Apprenticeship Model

The Welsh bardic tradition holds one of the most documented teaching-and-transmission frameworks in the European folk-religious landscape. The structure provides a working model for what kept-aside lineage transmission looks like in continuous practice over centuries.

### The pencerdd

The *pencerdd* (literally "chief of song" or "chief of craft") was the master poet — the bard who had won the bardic chair in competition. At the top of the bardic order, the pencerdd's duty was to sing the praise of God, the ruler, and the ruler's family. The pencerdd was the chief poet of a specific court or region; the role was earned through demonstrated mastery, not appointed.

The pencerdd was also the principal teacher. Welsh bards were instructed in the traditional manner by a pencerdd — literally a "song-master" or "poetry-master," but perhaps best translated as "bardic teacher" or "chief poet." The connections between teachers and pupils formed bardic lineages extending back through centuries, anchoring the living practitioner in the greatness of the past.

### The nine-year apprenticeship

The professionalism of the Welsh poetic tradition was sustained by a Guild of Poets — also called the Order of Bards — with its own rule book emphasizing the making of poetry as craft. Under its rules, poets undertook a nine-year apprenticeship to become fully qualified. The apprenticeship was not workshop attendance; it was sustained relationship with a master poet across years, with progressive study of meters, traditional materials, the bardic grammars, the genealogies of the patron families, and the elaborate metrical apparatus of strict-meter Welsh verse.

The medieval Welsh text *Gramadegau'r Penceirddiaid* ("the grammars of the chief poets") was the curricular spine. From the 14th century forward, the collection was handed down through the bardic tradition, with each generation revising and adapting it. The bardic triads in the Gramadegau'r Penceirddiaid were used to instruct apprentice bards. The text functioned both as instructional material and as living document — the master poet's own working knowledge transmitted in continuous tradition.

### The continuing tradition

Welsh poetic tradition continues into the contemporary period. Literature Wales runs the Pencerdd program — a contemporary formal recognition of master poets. The strict-meter Welsh verse tradition (cynghanedd) continues; contemporary Welsh-language bards still compete at the National Eisteddfod for the bardic chair.

For contemporary Welsh-tradition Pagan and reconstructionist practice, the bardic apprenticeship model is one of the few continuous-tradition transmission frameworks accessible. The Anglesey Druid Order's three-grade structure (Executive/Priests, Officially Trained Members/Awenyddion, Open Members) operates on this model in adapted form. OBOD's Bardic-Ovate-Druid grade structure operates on this model. Sisterhood of Avalon's Ninefold Path of the Priestess (33-month immersion) operates on this model.

### What this contributes to the keyed reader's teaching practice

The Welsh tradition contributes the model of slow, oral, personal, lineage-rooted transmission across years. The pencerdd does not run workshops; the pencerdd takes specific apprentices and teaches them across the time required for mastery. The transmission is structured (the bardic grammars provide curricular spine) and personal (the master-apprentice relationship is the operative unit). The apprenticeship's nine-year duration is honest about the time required.

For a Welsh-tradition late-practice practitioner: she becomes available as pencerdd to the practitioners who eventually present themselves to her. She does not seek; she is sought. Her teaching happens in the long-form relationship with specific others, with the curricular material her own developed practice has produced (the grimoire, the sigil archive, the personal book of cards, the reading log) as substrate.

## IV. West African Diaspora Transmission Models

The Black American and broader African Diaspora spiritual tradition holds multiple transmission models, several of which converge on the kept-aside elder model the document points toward.

### The church mother

The church mother is the most documented contemporary Black American spiritual transmission role. The title — given to older women within many Black churches — is the seed of leadership and eldership. Church mothers are the custodians of tradition, bearers of wisdom, and embodiments of spiritual guidance within their congregations. Their function combines spiritual mentorship, counseling, and the oral transmission of cultural meaning across generations.

Specifically: church mothers serve as spiritual mentors to younger members, drawing on deep understanding of scripture combined with life experience. They participate in fictive kinship relationships within the church community — "Mother" denotes spiritual-and-moral stature transcending biological relationship. They are active in community outreach and social justice. They are keepers of tradition through storytelling, hymn singing, and adherence to customs. The transmission happens largely through presence, conversation, modeling, and the oral tradition rather than through formal teaching.

The church mother model is the closest existing American reference to the kept-aside elder model the document points toward. The role is recognized rather than appointed, earned rather than self-claimed, embedded in community rather than in institution. For a practitioner with Black ancestry whose practice eventually places her in this kind of role within her communities, the church mother is the available model.

### Conjure and Hoodoo transmission within family lines

Hoodoo and conjure transmission has historically operated within family lines. Hoodoo Sen Moise's account (raised by his grandfather, taught from age four, learning to "open the door to power, wisdom, and service" before formal childhood) is representative of how the tradition has been transmitted in continuous Black American practice. The teaching is intimate, sustained, intergenerational, and embedded in daily life rather than in dedicated instructional contexts.

Stephanie Rose Bird, Hoodoo Sen Moise, Sherry Shone, and other contemporary Black practitioner-authors all reflect this transmission model in their own backgrounds — the tradition was given to them by elders in their families and communities. Their public teaching (books, courses, festivals like Beaucoup Hoodoo Fest) is supplementary to the family-lineage transmission, not replacement of it.

### The praise house tradition

The Gullah Geechee praise house — the small intimate community-prayer space distinct from the larger Sunday church service — is another transmission infrastructure. In the praise house, only members could take part in the sacred ring shout circle dance; adults taught the tradition to their children within the praise house space across generations. The tradition was preserved in continuous transmission specifically because the praise house was small, community-bounded, and inaccessible to outsiders.

For a contemporary practitioner with Gullah Geechee ancestral connection: the praise house is the model of intimate community-bounded transmission space. Substantive engagement requires direct relationship with continuing Gullah Geechee community where the praise house tradition still operates.

### The role of the older Black woman in religious community

Across African Diaspora traditions broadly — Lucumí (the iyalorisha as priestess and teacher), Vodou (the manbo as priestess and community leader), Hoodoo (the family elder as keeper of practice), the Black church (the church mother) — the role of the older Black woman as keeper of tradition and teacher of subsequent generations is foundational. The transmission happens through long sustained presence in community, with teaching given to those who come ready to receive it rather than sought out and recruited.

For the keyed reader: as a half-Black woman with documented Carolinas paternal ancestry, the available model is the elder-in-Black-spiritual-community position — earned over years, recognized by community rather than self-claimed, the work of continuous presence. Whether her actual community engagement places her in this role is for time and relationship to determine; the model exists.

### What this contributes to the keyed reader's teaching practice

The Black American and broader African Diaspora traditions contribute several specific elements: the church mother as the elder-resource model in continuous American practice; the family-line conjure transmission as model for intergenerational teaching; the praise house as model of intimate community-bounded transmission space; and the broader figure of the older Black woman as keeper of tradition. The transmission happens through presence and relationship, not through workshops or certifications. The teaching is given to those who come; the teacher does not seek.

## V. Continental Hermetic-Masonic Transmission Models

The Western Hermetic-Masonic tradition holds the most elaborated formal transmission apparatus among the three streams — initiated structure with progressive grades, formal master-to-apprentice transmission, ritual investiture, and substantial documentary tradition.

### Initiated transmission

Within Hermetic-Masonic streams, transmission happens through initiation. The initiated practitioner has been formally taken into the lineage; the transmission is bounded by the initiation. The structure: progressive initiations into higher grades, with each grade representing a more substantive engagement with the tradition's apparatus. The Memphis-Misraim 90-92 degrees, the Strict Observance / Rectified Scottish Rite degree progression, the Martinist progression through Élu Cohen working — these are formalized progressive transmissions that take years to complete.

The teaching happens in two registers within initiated bodies. Formal: the rituals themselves, the documents of the lineage, the curriculum of the working. Personal: the master-apprentice relationship within the lodge, with experienced members guiding initiates through the questions that mature each stage. The contemporary Western esotericism literature describes this as "introducing the full complexity of symbolism gradually" — like teaching an apprentice to swim before throwing them into the sea.

### The progression from learner to teacher

Within Hermetic-Masonic initiated bodies, the progression from learner to teacher happens in stages. The early grades are receiving (the initiate is being taught). Middle grades are integrating (the practitioner is consolidating). Higher grades involve transmitting (the practitioner has entered the position of teaching others within the lineage). The position is institutional — earned through grade progression and recognition by the body — rather than self-claimed.

For a Co-Masonic or Continental Hermetic-Masonic practitioner over decades: she progresses through grades; eventually she is in the position of teaching within the lineage; the teaching is bounded by initiation and operates within the apparatus of the body. This is closer to the lineage-specific transmission model than to the cross-tradition kept-aside model the broader document points toward.

### Non-initiated Hermetic study and teaching

Outside formal initiation, the Hermetic tradition supports significant non-initiated study. The academic Western Esotericism field (Faivre, Hanegraaff, McIntosh) is one continuing infrastructure. The non-initiated practitioner-scholar tradition (Crowley, Fortune, Regardie, Greer, Bardon) is another. The contemporary non-initiated Hermetic teacher-figures (Hermetic Academy, Hermetic.com, various adept-training programs) offer non-initiated transmission of varying quality.

For the keyed reader: substantial Hermetic teaching is accessible without formal initiation through academic and non-initiated practitioner channels. Whether she eventually pursues Co-Masonic or related initiation is a separate question; the non-initiated study can run in parallel and is itself substantive.

### Adept training

Franz Bardon's *Initiation into Hermetics: The Path of the True Adept* (1956 in German, 1962 in English; multiple editions) is the major non-initiated systematic Hermetic training curriculum in print. Bardon develops the four-elements framework into staged exercises providing detailed training across the arcanum of magic, from basic thought-control techniques through advanced astral teachings. The curriculum is severe (eight months minimum to complete the first degree of training; multi-year for advanced work) and is one of the few complete adept-training curricula accessible to the non-initiated practitioner.

For the keyed reader, Bardon is one option among several; not specifically Continental but Hermetic broadly. The relevance to teaching practice: Bardon's curriculum is a model of how a non-initiated practitioner could, after sustained practice, develop her own working teaching apparatus that draws from her actual practice rather than from any borrowed system.

### What this contributes to the keyed reader's teaching practice

The Continental Hermetic-Masonic tradition contributes the model of progressive grade transmission within initiated bodies (relevant if the keyed reader pursues Co-Masonic or related initiation) and the non-initiated adept-training model (Bardon, the academic Western Esotericism field) for sustained study without formal initiation. The transmission within initiated bodies is bounded by lineage; the non-initiated transmission is bounded by curriculum quality.

The Hermetic stream's specific contribution to the kept-aside elder model: the principle that transmission is gradual and personal even within formal apparatus, that introducing too much too quickly damages the apprentice, that the master's role is "guiding through questions rather than answers, awakening curiosity, weaving a network of trust that provides the time necessary for each lesson to mature." This is the master-apprentice principle that the tradition holds in formalized institutional form and that the kept-aside elder model holds in non-institutional form.

## VI. Contemporary Cross-Tradition Teacher-Figures

Beyond the lineage-specific models, several contemporary practitioner-figures operate in registers relevant to the keyed reader's eventual teaching practice. These are not models to imitate but reference points for how late-practice teaching has actually been done in contemporary American context.

**Daniel Foor — Ancestral Medicine network and practitioner training.** Foor's network has trained nearly 300 practitioners globally as of 2025. The training is multi-year (the certified Practitioner Training was developed over two decades), trauma-aware, ethics-bound (practitioners agree to the Ancestral Healing Practitioner Code of Ethics), and produces practitioners who maintain certification through Ancestral Medicine. The model is hybrid — institutional enough to maintain quality and ethics, personal enough to support actual transmission. The keyed reader, if she eventually moves into teaching, could follow this model (developing her own apprentice-track for specific people over years) or could sit alongside it (referring those she works with into the Foor network where appropriate).

**Resmaa Menakem — Cultural Somatics Institute.** Menakem's work has produced a training apparatus for somatic abolitionism and Cultural Somatics — eLearning courses, in-person immersive workshops, organizational training. The model is more institutional than Foor's; it operates at the scale of cultural movement-building rather than individual practitioner transmission. Relevant as reference for community-and-cultural teaching, less directly as model for individual elder-apprentice transmission.

**Stephen Jenkinson — Orphan Wisdom School.** Jenkinson's school operates as small-cohort residential teaching with selected participants over multi-year periods. Substantial direct transmission across the relationship between Jenkinson and his students; explicitly working in the elder-as-teacher register. *Die Wise* and *Come of Age* are the published apparatus; the school is the actual transmission. Relevant model for the kept-aside elder operating in small-cohort sustained relationship.

**Roshi Joan Halifax — Upaya Zen Center.** Halifax's institution operates as Zen Buddhist training combined with chaplaincy and end-of-life care training. Substantial individual teacher-student relationships; institutional and lineage-rooted. Relevant model for institutional teaching with lineage transmission.

**Parker Palmer — Center for Courage & Renewal.** Palmer's institution operates retreats, facilitator training, and the broader Courage Work movement. More movement-focused than individual-elder; relevant as cross-tradition reference for contemplative-teaching frame.

For the keyed reader: she does not need to operate as any of these. Each is a model, and her own working will be its own developed shape. The relevance is that contemporary American practice has produced multiple sustained models of late-practice teaching that are not the workshop-leader-and-certification model; the kept-aside elder model has contemporary precedent.

## VII. The Work of Repair as Sustained Practice — Cross-Stream Consolidation

The work of repair was substantively developed in the Continental Hermetic-Masonic deliverable, with focus on the Menakem framework, the Foor framework, and the Schulman frame. This section consolidates across all three streams.

### The frame, briefly

The work of repair as the document develops it is sustained ritual practice rather than therapeutic concept. It engages what the practitioner inherits — including violence at the origin, intergenerational trauma, the specific configuration the keyed reader carries. The work is not finite (repair is not completed); the work distinguishes the historical from the personal; the work names violence directly rather than allegorizing or softening it; the work distinguishes what is repairable from what is not (large-scale institutional violence is not repairable by individual practice; the practitioner's own relationship to the inheritance is repairable, slowly, over years); the work proceeds by stages with practitioner support; the work is sustained.

### What each stream contributes

**Welsh stream contributions to repair.** The elegiac form holds suffering without dissolving it — the bardic elegy and keening tradition give shape to grief that would otherwise be unbearable, and the practice of holding that shape across years is itself a form of repair. The Welsh holy well tradition holds the principle that water at sacred sites carries healing power across centuries — sustained pilgrimage to a holy well is sustained repair practice. The Welsh contemplative-mystical tradition (Augustine Baker, the apophatic-prayer tradition) holds the principle that interior contemplative work over years is its own slow repair of the practitioner's relationship with what she has inherited. The matrilineal Welsh inheritance for the keyed reader is unentangled (not entwined with the violence configuration of her father's line); the repair work in the Welsh stream is recovery work in its more straightforward form — finding what was lost, recovering Welsh-language access, building sustained relationship with the tradition's living teachers (Hughes, Telyndru, Sisterhood of Avalon).

**West African Diaspora stream contributions to repair.** The spiritual bath and uncrossing rituals are repair apparatus directly engaged with releasing accumulated harm. The ring shout is community repair through embodied collective ritual — the practice does not eliminate the trauma but allows the community to bear it together across the threshold. The ancestor work specifically engages the work of repair across generations — the keyed reader's enslaved ancestors, the violence they survived, the inheritance carried forward. Foor's stage-two-through-four methodology specifically addresses the ancestral wounds that sustained practice can engage with practitioner support. The honest acknowledgment within Black tradition: the repair is collective, takes generations, and the individual practitioner's work is participation in something much larger than herself. Resmaa Menakem's somatic abolitionism is the contemporary articulation of this: sustained body-based practice across years, with specific somatic techniques rooted in African Diasporic practice (humming, rocking, breathing, vagal regulation through community resonance), as continuing work that no completion can ever fully accomplish.

**Continental Hermetic-Masonic stream contributions to repair.** The alchemical nigredo holds the practitioner's suffering as material for transformation rather than as pathology to be eliminated; this is theological scaffolding for sustained repair work. The Reintegration framework holds the cosmic-scale work as larger than individual repair — the practitioner's repair participates in the broader human and cosmic movement of return. The theurgic purification practices provide ritual structure for the staged work. The specific work of repair given the violence at the origin of the Hermetic-Masonic stream in the keyed reader's line: the Dutch ancestor's violence is named directly, not softened; the apparatus is implicated in the violence, not separable from it; the practitioner takes from the inheritance what is useful, refuses what is harmful, makes reparation through the practice itself.

### The contemporary clinical apparatus as substrate

Already developed: Levine, Ogden, Menakem, Foor, Schwartz, Fisher, Porges, Dana, Schulman, Weller, Walker. The substrate for the repair work the lineage traditions hold in religious-ritual form. The clinical apparatus is necessary, not optional, for the keyed reader's CPTSD configuration; the lineage practices supplement but do not substitute. The integration: clinical practitioner relationship for the body work; lineage practices for the meaning-making and ritual scaffolding; community engagement for the collective dimension; sustained practice for the years required.

### The integration question

How do all three streams' repair apparatus integrate for a practitioner whose body holds all three inheritances simultaneously, with two of them entwined through the same violence?

The provisional answer: the streams contribute distinct material that sits in relation rather than blending. The Welsh stream is engaged for what Welsh repair work specifically does (the elegy, the well, the matrilineal recovery). The Diasporic stream is engaged for what Diasporic repair work specifically does (the bath, the ring shout, the ancestor work, somatic abolitionism). The Hermetic-Masonic stream is engaged for what Hermetic-Masonic repair work specifically does (the alchemical scaffolding, the Reintegration framing, the theurgic purification). The contemporary clinical apparatus runs through all of it as substrate. The keyed reader's actual repair practice over decades becomes the integrated working that no advance theory can produce.

The honest acknowledgment: the integration is sustained practice, not synthesis. The streams remain distinct; the practitioner becomes the body in which they coexist.

## VIII. Teaching and Repair as the Same Late Practice in Different Registers

The two questions — how does the practitioner teach, how does she continue the work of repair — converge in late practice. They are the same work in different registers.

The teaching is itself repair work. When the keyed reader, late in practice, transmits to a younger practitioner what she has learned from her three-stream inheritance, she is participating in the broader work of repair — the recovery of what was lost, the transmission of what was preserved, the naming of what was taken. Her teaching is not separate from her repair practice; it is one of the practice's late-stage productions. Welsh tradition transmission specifically operates as repair of what colonial-suppression and assimilation have taken; West African Diaspora transmission operates as continuing the lineage work that enslavement attempted to break; Hermetic-Masonic transmission within the keyed reader's practice operates as honest engagement with the inheritance that includes its violence rather than scrubbing it clean. The transmission is itself the work.

The repair is itself teaching. The keyed reader's sustained practice across decades — the ancestor work, the body work, the lineage-recovery work — produces a body that has metabolized inheritance, a practitioner who is a different kind of person than the one who started. This is what the people who eventually come to her as students are coming to. They are not coming for techniques (techniques can be found anywhere); they are coming for the presence of someone who has done the work. Her presence is itself her teaching. The repair across decades is what she has to give.

For chapter writing in Ch 6: the closing chapter holds these as one. The acceptance work of late practice (already developed in the death practice deliverable), the transmission practice of late practice (developed here), and the sustained repair practice of late practice (consolidated here across streams) are not three separate Ch 6 sections. They are the same late practice in different registers. The chapter prose can move among them honestly without artificially separating.

## IX. The Practical Shape of Late-Practice Teaching

What it actually looks like, drawing on the tradition models above and the contemporary references.

**The shortlist.** The practitioner builds, over years, a working file of practitioners she has encountered, vetted, or come into relationship with. Some are prospective apprentices (people who have begun to come to her with questions, who are doing serious work, who might over time develop into actual teaching relationship). Some are colleagues (other late-practice practitioners with whom she sustains exchange). Some are students who have moved on (people she taught at one stage who continue their own development). The shortlist is itself part of the practice — maintained, updated, kept aside for the moments when teaching becomes possible.

**The kept-aside availability.** The practitioner does not seek students. She does not run workshops; she does not certify; she does not build a brand. She is available in the practical sense — reachable, willing to respond to specific inquiries, willing to enter sustained correspondence with practitioners who present themselves seriously. The discipline is the not-seeking; the practice is the availability.

**The recognition work.** When a practitioner does come to her with serious questions, the keyed reader's late-practice work includes the discernment of whether this person is one she can teach, whether the relationship is productive, whether her apparatus is what this practitioner needs. The recognition is not always yes; sometimes the right move is to refer, sometimes to decline, sometimes to engage briefly without entering long-form teaching relationship. The discipline of the recognition is the work of the elder.

**The actual teaching.** Once a teaching relationship has formed, what the keyed reader transmits is not a curriculum but her own developed practice — the grimoire, the sigil archive, the personal book of cards, the reading log, the ancestral records, the accumulated working knowledge of decades. The transmission happens in conversation, in extended correspondence, in occasional in-person work, in the slow modeling of how the practice has been done. The Welsh nine-year apprenticeship is the operative-time order of magnitude; the relationship may be longer or shorter, but the transmission is sustained over years rather than packaged into events.

**The transmission of inheritance specifically.** For the keyed reader's three-stream configuration: what she transmits is not just her own practice but the recovered inheritance she has been working with across decades. Welsh material recovered from the matrilineal line, Diasporic material recovered through community engagement and Foor's apparatus, Hermetic-Masonic material engaged through whatever pathway she has actually pursued — these become inheritance she can offer to specific practitioners who eventually have standing for them. The transmission is itself the work of repair: what was taken from her ancestors becomes available to those who follow.

**The ending.** The teacher's death is also the teacher's last teaching. The practitioner-students who have been with her over years receive the death as the working's culmination; the transmission of what continues after her is itself part of the practice. This is the convergence with the death practice already developed in Ch 6.

## X. The Practical Shape of the Work of Repair as Sustained Practice

What it actually looks like over decades.

**The daily practice.** The candle, the grimoire, the daily card pull, the somatic anchors. The repair work is woven through; it is not a separate practice. The body work that supports the practitioner's capacity to bear what she carries; the writing practice that holds what surfaces; the cards that surface what the body and mind have not yet seen consciously. Daily practice is the substrate; the repair work develops within it.

**The weekly practice.** The longer working that v10 § VI develops. Weekly direct engagement with specific repair material — an ancestor with whom the relationship needs sustained tending, a body experience that needs pendulation work, a piece of lineage research that needs digestion. Weekly is where the work happens that the daily practice cannot hold.

**The seasonal practice.** Each turning of the wheel surfaces different material. Samhain (fire watch peak in San Diego, monarchs arriving) brings the dead forward; Imbolc brings new material the wet-season abundance has surfaced; Beltane brings what the practitioner is releasing as the dry season opens; Mabon brings what the year has produced. The seasonal practice integrates the repair work across the year's rhythm.

**The yearly practice.** At Samhain or the practitioner's chosen yearly threshold: the year's repair work is reviewed in the recognition record. What has the year produced. What ancestor has come forward. What lineage material has been recovered. What inheritance has been refused. What relationship has been deepened or ended. The yearly review is the practice's accounting of itself.

**The body as ongoing site.** Repair work happens in the body across years. Some of it is excavation work that requires practitioner support (Foor stages 2-4, somatic-experiencing or sensorimotor-psychotherapy or IFS work with appropriate clinicians). Some of it is regulation work that the practitioner does on her own. Some is integrative work that happens in community (ring shout, Welsh group ritual, Co-Masonic working). The body holds what the mind cannot fully process; the body is also where the integration eventually happens.

**The relationships across the threshold.** The ancestor work continues across decades. The keyed reader's relationship with specific ancestors deepens; some ancestors who began as challenging become resourced over time; some take longer than expected; some never fully open. The Foor stage-by-stage methodology is the spine; the lineage traditions deepen the work; the keyed reader's actual relationship with her specific dead becomes the developed apparatus.

**The naming and refusing.** Across decades, the practitioner names what was carried, refuses what was harmful, transforms what can be transformed. The Hermetic-Masonic apparatus inherited through violence is engaged honestly — the violence is named, the apparatus is not romanticized; what is useful is kept; what is harmful is refused; the practitioner's work over years is the transformation of what can be transformed. The Welsh inheritance is recovered honestly. The Diasporic inheritance is engaged honestly with the recognition that the work is collective and intergenerational rather than individual.

**The sufficient-not-finite frame.** The repair is never completed. After thirty years of practice, there is still repair work to do. The acceptance is not resignation; it is the practitioner's settled relationship with the work as her sustained life rather than as project. The honest acknowledgment: the trauma is never gone; what changes is the practitioner's capacity to bear and metabolize what is carried. This is what sustained practice across decades produces — not the elimination of inheritance but the practitioner who has become someone who can hold it.
