# The San Diego Bioregional Wheel
## Research deliverable for 2.3 — feeds Ch 2 (apparatus building, where the wheel is built) and is referenced in Ch 3 (where the wheel runs)

This document develops the bioregional adaptation of the wheel of the year for San Diego / coastal Mediterranean / chaparral. v10 § V names the question and points at it; this document does the research work v10 only points at. It is a reference deliverable, not chapter prose — chapter prose will draw on it.

The work has three parts. First, the macro shape of the San Diego year — what's actually happening climatically, ecologically, phenologically, and how that differs from the Northern European agricultural cycle the eight conventional stations were designed for. Second, the eight stations mapped honestly to San Diego — for each, the conventional date and meaning, what's actually present here at that turning, the bioregional anchors that let the observance land, and an assessment of whether the station holds or feels imported. Third, the framing for Kumeyaay seasonal knowledge — present and acknowledged as cultural-historical context, with the appropriate boundary that substantive engagement with Kumeyaay tradition requires direct relationship to the Kumeyaay community, not borrowing from book-research.

A summary at the end gives the tabulated quick reference, names what's settled vs. contested, and identifies what would benefit from local primary research (a year of personal phenological observation produces the most accurate version of this).

## I. The Macro Shape of the San Diego Year

San Diego is a borderline arid Mediterranean climate, USDA Zone 9, with under twelve inches of average annual rainfall, no frost, and what is meaningfully a two-season year — wet and dry — rather than the four-season year the conventional wheel assumes. The wet season runs roughly December through March; the dry season July through October; April-May and November are transitional. This pattern is the inverse of what the conventional eight-station wheel was designed around: the British and Northern European agricultural cycle has a wet summer growing season followed by autumn harvest and winter dormancy. San Diego's growing season is winter and spring; summer is dormancy for most native chaparral and coastal sage scrub plants; autumn is fire watch.

The two-season honest framing matters because it's the foundation everything else rests on. Several conventional sabbats — Imbolc as "first stirring of spring," Beltane as "great fire festival of the height of summer," Lughnasadh as "first harvest" — were designed for an agricultural cycle that does not exist here. They can still be observed, but observed as imported gestures unless the practitioner does the work of finding what locally is actually happening at that turning of the year.

The major climatic and phenological features of San Diego's year:

**The wet season (December through March).** This is when the hills green up, creeks and rivers run, native plants begin their annual cycle. Most native bloom is winter and early spring. California poppies and other wildflower bloom typically peaks late February through April. The growing season for vegetables and ornamentals is centered here.

**The dry season (May through October).** Native chaparral plants enter dormancy or summer dormancy ("aestivation"); leaves curl and drop; fire fuel accumulates. Most coastal sage scrub plants finish flowering by May.

**June Gloom (and May Gray).** The marine layer that sits over the coast through May and June, producing overcast cool mornings and afternoons. May (59% sunny days) and June (58%) are the cloudiest months of the year on the coast. Inland and desert areas escape this; coastal and near-coastal San Diego does not.

**Santa Ana wind season (September through April, peak October through December).** Hot, dry easterly winds from the desert that push the marine layer offshore, drop relative humidity to single digits, and create the most dangerous fire weather of the year. October and November are the highest-risk fire months because vegetation has accumulated fuel through the long dry summer and the rains have not yet arrived. This is the period when "the wheel" in Northern Europe is thinning toward Samhain and turning toward winter; in San Diego this is when fire watch is at its peak.

**Gray whale migration.** Southbound past the San Diego coast roughly mid-November through early February (passing on the way to Baja calving lagoons); northbound roughly mid-February through May (returning to Alaska feeding grounds with new calves, the latest of whom pass through April-May).

**Monarch butterfly overwintering.** Western monarchs cluster on the California coast from mid-October through mid-February, peak November through January. Pacific Grove is the most documented site; there are smaller overwintering sites along the Southern California coast.

**Coast live oak acorn fall.** Late summer through autumn — September and October are the standard harvest window. The acorn was the principal food staple for at least twelve California Native American groups including the Kumeyaay, who are the indigenous people of San Diego County.

**California native bloom phenology, generally.** Black sage (*Salvia mellifera*) flowers March-May; California poppies February-April; California fuchsia flowers in autumn (uncommon among natives); coastal sage scrub generally has something flowering every month, but the wet-season concentration is heavy and the dry-season pattern is sparse. Recent research documents that climate change has advanced phenophase timing across most California native plants, including in San Diego.

This is the substrate. The wheel that gets observed in San Diego sits on top of a year that is structurally different from the year the wheel was designed for. Some stations land easily on the local substrate; some require translation; a few require honest acknowledgment that they import meaning the local climate doesn't carry.

## II. The Eight Stations Mapped to San Diego

For each station: the conventional name, conventional date, conventional Northern European meaning, and then what's actually happening in San Diego at that time. The honest assessment of fit.

### Samhain — October 31 / November 1

*Conventional meaning*: Celtic new year, festival of the dead, thinning of the veil between worlds, entry into the dark half of the year, the agricultural-pastoral threshold between abundance and dormancy.

*What's happening in San Diego*: This is fire watch peak. October and November are the highest-risk fire months — Santa Ana winds, dry vegetation, no rainfall yet. The dry season is at its very end. Trees are not turning color in any meaningful way (San Diego has limited deciduous tree population beyond planted ornamentals). The first rains have not yet arrived, though they are imminent. Daylight is decreasing toward the winter solstice. Coast live oaks are dropping the last of the acorn harvest. Monarch butterflies are arriving at overwintering sites. Gray whale southbound migration is beginning. The Kumeyaay calendar continues from its New Year at the fall equinox; in their reckoning this is the heart of the late autumn moon-cycle leading to the winter solstice.

*Honest fit assessment*: Samhain holds, but for reasons different from the Celtic agricultural-pastoral. Where the European Samhain is the end of harvest and the entry into the dark half, the San Diego Samhain is the end of the year's most dangerous fire window and the moment just before the rains arrive — the threshold between the parched and the renewed. The thinning-of-the-veil and festival-of-the-dead theological substance translates fully; the agricultural framing does not, but the threshold framing does, in different terms. **A San Diego Samhain centers the fire ecology and the imminent return of water — what survived the dry, what is about to be reborn by rain — alongside the dead.**

### Yule / Winter Solstice — December 20-23

*Conventional meaning*: longest night, rebirth of the sun, deepest moment of the dark half of the year, vigil through the longest night.

*What's happening in San Diego*: This is the heart of the wet season. Rain is reliably arriving. Hills are greening. Native plants are resuming their growth cycle. Daylight is at minimum but the temperature is cool rather than genuinely cold (San Diego average winter low is around 9°C / 48°F; frost is rare on the coast). Gray whales are passing southbound in peak numbers. Monarchs are clustered at peak overwintering. The Kumeyaay observe the winter solstice as Hilyati — the most important date on their calendar, the moment when the return of the sun is honored.

*Honest fit assessment*: Yule holds. The longest-night / rebirth-of-the-sun theology translates fully. The local layer that San Diego adds is that this is also the height of the wet season — the rebirth of the sun coincides with the renewal of water. **A San Diego Yule honors both the return of light and the active wet-season rains. The Kumeyaay reading of the winter solstice as the year's most important date is consonant with the European Yule observance.**

### Imbolc — February 1 / 2

*Conventional meaning*: first stirring of spring, festival of Brigid in the Irish tradition, lengthening of light beginning to be perceptible, lambing season, candles and purification.

*What's happening in San Diego*: Wet season continues. Hills are deeply green. California poppies are beginning to bloom — peak bloom is late February through April, so Imbolc catches the leading edge. Black sage is beginning to bloom (peak March-May). Native bulbs (Dichelostemma, Calochortus) are stirring. Gray whale southbound migration is ending; the first northbound whales are passing. Monarchs are still clustered but mating activity is increasing in late February. Daylight is noticeably increasing. There is no actual lambing on the agricultural-pastoral schedule in most of San Diego County (sheep and goat herding is a small part of regional agriculture).

*Honest fit assessment*: Imbolc translates fully but in a different register. Where the European Imbolc is the first pale stirring of growth in the deep cold, the San Diego Imbolc is the active early flowering of the wildflower season, the deep green of the hills, the leading edge of the wet-season abundance. **A San Diego Imbolc centers the actively unfolding native wildflower bloom and the hill-green abundance of the wet season — the festival of light being kept by Brigid translates as the festival of water-fed life actively returning.** The candle work and purification translate without modification.

### Spring Equinox / Ostara — March 19-22

*Conventional meaning*: balance of light and dark with light increasing, beginning of true spring, balance, growth, fertility.

*What's happening in San Diego*: Peak wildflower bloom. California poppies, black sage, coast live oak flowering, native lupines, all the chaparral spring bloom. Gray whale northbound migration is in active middle (March is one of the peak viewing months). Hills are at maximum greenness. Final rains of the wet season are typically falling. Monarch butterflies are beginning northern migration after late-February mating.

*Honest fit assessment*: Ostara translates fully. The balance-of-light-and-dark astronomical reality is universal; the spring growth and bloom theology is precisely fit to what San Diego is doing at this moment. **A San Diego Ostara centers the peak native wildflower bloom and the gray whale northbound migration. Of the eight stations, this is one of the cleanest fits to local reality.**

### Beltane — May 1

*Conventional meaning*: the great fire festival, beginning of summer in the Celtic agricultural-pastoral year, fertility and the union of masculine and feminine principles, vitality, height of light half of the year, May Queen and May King, the bonfire.

*What's happening in San Diego*: Wet season is ending. Last spring bloom is finishing — black sage is at peak, California poppies are tapering. May Gray begins (the marine layer establishes; cloudy mornings become routine). The transition to dry season is underway. Gray whale northbound migration continues with the late mothers-and-calves group. The hills will green their last for the year and begin the slow shift toward summer brown-out.

*Honest fit assessment*: Beltane lands awkwardly in San Diego. Two specific tensions. First, the great-fire-festival framing is operatively dangerous — May is the start of the year's drying-out, and ritual fires are a real fire-danger consideration in San Diego County. Second, the height-of-light-half-of-the-year framing belongs at midsummer, not May 1, in any climate — Beltane is the start of summer in the Celtic frame because Celtic summer begins early. In San Diego the equivalent moment of "the season opens" lands more honestly at the beginning of the dry season proper, in late May through June. **A San Diego Beltane centers the transition from wet to dry season and the establishment of the marine layer (May Gray as a real local atmospheric event), with the fire framing softened or transposed — symbolic fire (candle, hearth) rather than bonfire is the more honest local move. The fertility and vitality theology translates; the great-fire-festival framing is one of the stations that requires translation.**

### Summer Solstice / Litha — June 19-22

*Conventional meaning*: longest day, height of the sun, deepest moment of the light half of the year, fullness, abundance, recognition that from this moment the light begins to decrease.

*What's happening in San Diego*: Peak June Gloom. The marine layer is at its most established; coastal mornings and afternoons are routinely overcast. Inland is bright and hot. Native chaparral has finished flowering and is entering summer dormancy. Coastal sage scrub plants are dropping leaves in their summer-deciduous mode. Daylight is at maximum but on the coast the sun is often hidden. Days are long but the felt experience is cooler and grayer than the calendar suggests.

*Honest fit assessment*: Litha lands strangely in coastal San Diego. The astronomical reality (longest day, peak sun) is universal, but the felt experience is dampened by the marine layer in a way that doesn't match the Northern European Litha experience of high sun, full warmth, peak bloom. Inland San Diego (and the desert east of the mountains) experiences a more conventional Litha — bright, hot, full sun. Coastal San Diego experiences something more ambiguous — long days under gray light, with a particular meditative quality that the Northern European Litha doesn't carry. **A coastal San Diego Litha honors both the astronomical peak-of-light AND the marine-layer-tempered local experience of that peak — the "longest day under gray light" rather than "longest day under full sun." This is one of the stations where the bioregional gap is most visible; some practitioners may find that the inland-vs-coastal distinction matters more than the date itself.**

### Lughnasadh / Lammas — August 1

*Conventional meaning*: first harvest festival, named for the Irish god Lugh or for the Anglo-Saxon Christian loaf-mass, beginning of harvest, first cutting of grain, recognition of what is being reaped, early descent into the dark half.

*What's happening in San Diego*: Mid-dry-season. Hills are brown. Most native plants are in summer dormancy. Marine layer continues but is less reliable as the high-pressure system intensifies. Days are still long but starting to shorten perceptibly. Coast live oak acorns are beginning to fill out (the acorn harvest is approaching but not yet at peak — that comes in September-October). The agricultural cycle of Mediterranean California has summer crops at peak (tomatoes, melons, summer fruit), but the native landscape is deep in dormancy.

*Honest fit assessment*: Lughnasadh translates only partially. The first-harvest framing fits the cultivated agricultural year (gardeners are harvesting summer fruit and vegetables in early August) but doesn't fit the native chaparral ecology, which is at its driest. The grain-and-bread framing is borrowed from a culture that doesn't grow wheat as a native crop here. **A San Diego Lughnasadh can land in two registers: as the cultivated-garden first-harvest moment (consistent with summer-fruit abundance) or as the marker of the year's deepest dormancy (the chaparral at its driest, the recognition of what survives drought). The latter framing is more bioregionally honest. The Coast live oak acorn maturation, peaking later, is the closest thing the native landscape has to a "first harvest," and it lands closer to Mabon than Lughnasadh.**

### Mabon / Autumn Equinox — September 19-23

*Conventional meaning*: balance of light and dark with darkness increasing, second harvest festival, entry into autumn proper, balance, gratitude.

*What's happening in San Diego*: This is the **Kumeyaay New Year** (the fall equinox marks the beginning of the Kumeyaay calendar, the Kli'a 'Emul or beginning of the acorn harvest). Coast live oak acorn drop is at peak. Marine layer is breaking down; clear bright days return. Santa Ana winds may begin (October is the peak Santa Ana month, but late September is possible). Fire risk increases sharply through the next two months. Daylight is balanced (~12 hours each). Native chaparral is in deepest dormancy before the autumn rains.

*Honest fit assessment*: Mabon lands well in San Diego, with a particular weight that the Northern European Mabon doesn't carry. The acorn harvest framing — central to the indigenous ecology of California and to the Kumeyaay specifically — gives Mabon a content that the conventional wheel only gestures at. **A San Diego Mabon centers the acorn harvest, the entry into fire watch, and the bioregional acknowledgment that the Kumeyaay observe this date as their New Year. It is one of the stations where the bioregional adaptation actually deepens the observance rather than thinning it, because the local indigenous tradition independently identifies this date as the year's pivot.**

## III. Bioregional Additions Beyond the Eight Stations

Some practitioners working bioregionally in California have added stations or shifted emphasis to capture local turnings the conventional eight don't mark. The relevant precedents:

**Mark Green (Atheopaganism, coastal Northern California) added Riverain** — a station at the height of the wet season (February in his frame), marking the moment when creeks and rivers run high and the hills are at peak green. This is the moment when San Diego's wet-season abundance is most visible and felt. Green's argument is that the conventional wheel's "deepest dark" framing at Yule misses the operational fact that in Mediterranean California the wet-season abundance is actively unfolding through the winter — it is not a season of dormancy, it is a season of activity. *Riverain* names this directly. The keyed reader could observe Riverain at a date appropriate to San Diego (likely mid-to-late February, when the hills are at peak green and the wildflower bloom is leading edge — though this overlaps Imbolc, so the practitioner may choose to fold Riverain into Imbolc as San Diego's particular flavoring).

**Fire watch as a marked period.** October and November as the fire-watch peak deserve specific ritual attention in San Diego. Some practitioners observe the period between Mabon and Samhain as a particular contemplative attention to fire ecology — what the dry season has produced, what is at risk, what has been received from the sun. This is not a single-night observance but a sustained attention through six to eight weeks. Could be ritualized as a daily candle practice through the period (consistent with the chapter's general candle apparatus).

**The first rain.** The single most felt event of the San Diego year is the arrival of the first significant autumn rain — typically late October through early December, but variable year to year. Most practitioners working bioregionally in arid Mediterranean climates observe the first rain as its own event, separate from the eight stations, marked as a specific small ritual when it arrives. This is a moveable feast set by the climate rather than the calendar, and it is one of the most honest local additions a San Diego practitioner can make.

**The marine layer establishment and breakdown.** May Gray and the breakdown of the marine layer in late September are felt atmospheric events that have no conventional sabbat equivalent. Some practitioners mark these as transitional observances — the marine layer's establishment at Beltane and breakdown at Mabon — without making them new stations.

## IV. The Kumeyaay Calendar — How to Engage with Boundaries Held

The Kumeyaay are the indigenous people of San Diego County. The Kumeyaay calendar (Matt'aam) consists of thirteen lunar months and four seasons demarcated by the equinoxes and solstices. The Kumeyaay New Year is at the fall equinox (September 21 or thereabouts), marking the beginning of the acorn harvest (Kli'a 'Emul). The winter solstice (Hilyati) is the most important date on the Kumeyaay calendar, observed as the celebration of the return of the sun. The names of the months repeat after six months and follow lunar phases, with adjustments made by the appearance of certain constellations.

The Kumeyaay maintain sophisticated traditional ecological knowledge, including prescribed burnings to promote transitional plant species, rock-drop water management to create wetlands, and astronomical timing for plant harvest and burns.

**Boundary held**: substantive engagement with Kumeyaay traditional knowledge and ritual cycle requires direct relationship to the Kumeyaay community. The Kumeyaay nation includes multiple bands (Viejas, Sycuan, Barona, Campo, La Posta, Manzanita, Mesa Grande, San Pasqual, Iipay Nation of Santa Ysabel, Inaja-Cosmit, Jamul, Ewiiaapaayp, and others) with their own governance and cultural practices. Public-facing cultural-historical resources include Museum of Us (San Diego), the Kumeyaay Studies program at Cuyamaca College, the Sycuan Cultural Resource Center, and tribal museums. These are appropriate entry points for cultural-historical learning. They are not appropriate substitutes for the kind of teacher-student relationship that substantive Kumeyaay practice requires.

**What's appropriate for a non-Kumeyaay practitioner doing bioregional work**: Knowing whose land you live on. Knowing the basic outline of the Kumeyaay calendar. Knowing that the Kumeyaay observe the fall equinox as New Year and the winter solstice as Hilyati. Acknowledging this in the practitioner's own observances ("I am observing Mabon, which is also the Kumeyaay New Year"). Honoring the acorn harvest as the central indigenous-ecological event of late summer / early autumn that the conventional wheel only gestures at. Visiting Kumeyaay public resources to learn cultural-historical context.

**What's not appropriate**: Borrowing Kumeyaay ritual structure, deity names, or specific traditional practices into solo book-practice. Using "Kumeyaay-inspired" framings as decoration on a non-Kumeyaay practice. Treating the calendar information available in public sources as license to do Kumeyaay-tradition work without a Kumeyaay teacher.

This is the same boundary that v10 § XVI and § XIX hold for other initiation-required traditions (Yoruba/Lucumí, Vodou, etc.). The framing is consistent.

## VI. What's Settled vs. What's Open

**Settled** (well-attested, multiple-source confidence):
- San Diego climate pattern (Mediterranean, two-season, wet winter / dry summer)
- Marine layer / June Gloom timing (May-June primary)
- Santa Ana wind pattern (September-April, October-December peak)
- Fire watch peak (October-November)
- Gray whale migration windows (south Nov-Feb, north mid-Feb through May)
- Monarch overwintering (mid-Oct to mid-Feb, peak Nov-Jan)
- California poppy bloom (late Feb to late April)
- Coast live oak acorn drop (September-October)
- Kumeyaay calendar basics (13 lunar months, 4 seasons, fall equinox = New Year, winter solstice = Hilyati)

**Less settled** (variable, year-dependent, or contested):
- Specific first-rain date (genuinely variable; recent years have included some of the driest on record)
- Climate-change-driven phenological shifts (well-documented in California broadly; specifics for San Diego still evolving)
- Whether Riverain is widely-adopted as a station or remains Mark Green's personal practice (latter is more accurate)
- Whether the inland-vs-coastal distinction matters more than the date for Litha (probably yes, but practitioner-dependent)
- Specific timing of monarch population health (population has been in significant decline; a 2026 update on overwintering numbers is worth checking annually)

**What would benefit from local primary research** (a year of personal observation):
- Specific phenology of the practitioner's actual neighborhood (different parts of San Diego County have meaningfully different microclimates — coastal, inland valley, mountain, desert)
- The practitioner's own first-rain marker year by year
- The practitioner's personal observation of the marine layer establishment and breakdown each year
- Local plant phenology (which native plants in which exact locations bloom when, in the practitioner's specific area)
- Local bird migration timing (gray whale is well-documented; songbird migration is more variable)
- Building a personal "what was happening when" record over years that becomes more accurate than any reference document

The chapter should explicitly invite this — the wheel is genuinely built by the practitioner over the first several years of practice, with this document as starting reference rather than as fixed truth.
