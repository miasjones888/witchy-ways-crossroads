The Deeper Years

If lineage commitment ever happens, it will not happen in the early years and it will not happen by decision. It will happen by recognition. Years three to ten are the spiral — same territories returned to at greater depth, with the question of commitment becoming a real question rather than a deferred one.

· · ·

The chapter develops what years three through ten produce. The spiral structure is named honestly: same territories, different depths. The wheel that was an introductory frame in chapter 2 is now your calendar; the lunar architecture is mature; the daily candle has been lit thousands of times; the grimoire has accumulated into multiple volumes. The body work that was regulation only in chapter 2 has earned the right to do excavation work, with practitioner support. Lineage commitment becomes a real question — solo practice continues, or it becomes joining a tradition. Teachers and community become possible to find. The work of repair with the violent ancestral inheritance becomes a sustained practice rather than a question.

§ VI.I commitment by recognition

What recognition looks like, when it comes

If lineage commitment ever happens for you, it will not happen by decision. It will happen by recognition. The literature describes the eventual recognition consistently across traditions. It is not an enthusiastic decision; it is a noticing. You notice that she has been returning to one set of texts more than others. That one deity has been showing up across her dreams, her reading, her attention, in ways that are no longer plausibly coincidence. That one tradition's practices have been the ones that consistently produce real effect rather than just producing the experience of practicing. That when she sits with the question of what she actually believes, one frame holds and the others fall away.

The recognition is usually accompanied by something like reluctance. You often does not want to commit, because commitment narrows the broad exploration that has been the work of the early years. There is a real loss in commitment — the other traditions become permanently the not-chosen, and you becomes someone who has narrowed. This reluctance is appropriate and is not a sign that the recognition is wrong. The practitioners who report no reluctance generally report later that they committed too early.

When the recognition holds and the commitment is made, the relationship to the practice changes. The early-years breadth is replaced by depth. The grimoire develops differently — material specific to whichever tradition or deity has claimed her accumulates in a way that other material no longer does (Hekatean primary sources for one practitioner, the Mórrígan corpus for another, the Welsh-Druidic primary literature for a third, the Yoruba odu-corpus for a fourth, the Hermetic primary tradition for a fifth, the goddess-spirituality lineage for a sixth) — or the lunar work becomes specifically the structured-work of whichever tradition holds her rather than personal observation. The reading list narrows. The community of other practitioners becomes possible to find — most lineages have at least informal networks. The practice deepens substantially as a result of the narrowing.

The recognition is usually accompanied by something like reluctance. This is appropriate and is not a sign that the recognition is wrong.

If commitment never comes, that is also a real practitioner mode. Some practitioners never arrive at a single-lineage commitment. They develop instead what could be called an eclectic-grounded practice — sustained engagement with foundational practices, periodic deep engagement with specific traditions, devotional relationships with several figures across several traditions, ongoing reading across the field. This is not a failure of commitment; it is a defensible position. Some of the most accomplished contemporary practitioners hold it.

The distinguishing feature between a serious eclectic practice and the popular eclecticism the document warned against early is the depth of the foundational work. The serious eclectic practitioner has engaged the major traditions seriously, has built the foundational practices over years, has developed the metaphysical and practical sensibility that comes only from sustained work — and from that ground has chosen, deliberately, to remain plural rather than narrowing.

build · the recognition record

Long-form attention to what is emerging

Across the years of broad practice, maintain a section in your grimoire (or its own short notebook) for what could be called the recognition record. Quarterly, in a single sitting, write briefly:

  • What I have been reading most consistently this quarter
  • What practices have produced real effect rather than just the experience of practicing
  • What deities, figures, or traditions have shown up across my attention without me seeking them
  • What I currently believe, in two or three sentences (this echoes the metaphysical journal in chapter 4 but kept short here)
  • What I notice has fallen away — what was a pull a year ago that no longer is

After three or five years of these entries, the pattern will be visible to you in a way it cannot be in any single quarter. The eventual recognition, if it comes, will be the noticing of the pattern that has been there for years. The record makes the noticing possible.

§ VI.II on belief — at depth

Three positions a thoughtful practitioner can hold

Chapter 4 held the question of belief lightly — the metaphysical journal as practice, the invitation not to resolve in advance, the awareness that the question would arrive across years. By the deeper years, the question has arrived. You have lived enough relationship with the deities, the dead, the chthonic powers, the land to have actual questions about what those relationships are. The substantive philosophical apparatus — Edward Butler, Iamblichus, Hillman, von Franz, Strand — now serves rather than overwhelms because you are reading philosophy about something that has happened to her experientially.

The honest position: most serious practitioners drift between several metaphysical positions over the arc of a practice. The drift is not failure of belief; it is the practice working on the believer. The deities and entities engaged through ritual become more real over time, in a sense that does not map neatly onto the literal-versus-metaphorical distinction the modern Western mind defaults to. The position you hold at the start of the practice is rarely the position you hold at the end. The position you hold in any given week may differ from the one you held the week before. What stays consistent is the practice — the candle lit, the offering made, the deity addressed, the dead named. The metaphysics develops alongside.

Three positions a thoughtful practitioner can hold, with the writers and traditions that develop each.

The deities act on you; the practice produces effects; the relationship is real. What to call this becomes a question you can defer indefinitely while continuing to practice.

Position one — literal devotional polytheism. The deities are real, ontologically distinct persons with their own agency, intelligence, and existence independent of human belief in them. Whichever specific deities a particular practitioner has come into relationship with — Hekate, the Mórrígan, Rhiannon, Brigid, Yemoja, Inanna, Freyja, the Black Madonna, any of the figures the practice has opened toward — are real entities. The dead are real, continuing persons. The relationship is the same kind as relationship between two living humans: real, mutual, ethically substantive, capable of being damaged by neglect or strengthened by attention. The principal contemporary philosophical defender is Edward Butler, whose Essays on a Polytheistic Philosophy of Religion (Phaidra Editions, 2012) is the most rigorous contemporary defense of the position. The broader contemporary devotional polytheist movement — Galina Krasskova, Morpheus Ravenna, John Beckett — works in this register.

Position two — theurgic-Platonic. The deities are real but in a more philosophically nuanced sense — as the highest principles of reality accessible through ritual engagement, ontologically real but not reducible to the kind of personal-agential entity the literal position holds. This is the position of late Platonic theurgy — Iamblichus, Proclus, Damascius. The principal contemporary defender is Algis Uždavinys; Gregory Shaw's Theurgy and the Soul: The Neoplatonism of Iamblichus (Penn State, 1995) is the standard scholarly treatment. Sallustius's On the Gods and the World (4th c. CE) is the shortest accessible primary source.

Position three — psycho-magical / depth-archetypal. The deities and entities engaged through ritual are real in a phenomenological and psychological sense — archetypal realities, real patterns of human experience that act on the psyche through ritual engagement, capable of producing genuine effects without committing to literal ontological independence. Developed most extensively by C.G. Jung, James Hillman, Marie-Louise von Franz, and the broader archetypal-psychology tradition. Sophie Strand's contemporary work develops the depth-archetypal-witchcraft register substantively.

What practitioners across the serious literature actually report: the question of metaphysics is held lightly while the practice is engaged seriously. Over years of practice, the relationship with the deity becomes operative regardless of your metaphysical position. The deity acts on you; the practice produces effects; the relationship is real. Whether to call this real in the ontologically-independent sense, the theurgic-Platonic sense, or the depth-archetypal sense becomes a question you can defer indefinitely. By the deeper years, you have often arrived at her own working synthesis — drawing from each position what serves and refusing what doesn't, which is you-philosophical mode the long-practice tradition operates in.

→ by the deeper years the question has arrived. now the philosophy serves. reading butler at year one is reading at the wrong stage.
→ three positions; each coherent. none correct. the drift is the practice working.

§ VI.V teachers and community

When solo work asks for company

Solo practice is the right shape for the early years and may remain the right shape indefinitely. But there are kinds of work that solo practice cannot hold, and there are points in a developing practice when finding actual teachers, practitioners, or community becomes part of the next move.

The discipline of solo practice that the rest of this document develops is real and not lesser. Most contemporary serious practitioners spend substantial portions of their practicing lives working alone, with reading and writing and the slow accumulation of the grimoire as their primary companions. But certain kinds of work — initiation-required traditions, lineage-transmitted material, deep ancestral repair where the lineage is complex or the inheritance is heavy, devotional work with figures whose tradition has living human carriers, and the simple human need to not be the only person doing this — eventually ask for company.

Vetting principles

The contemporary spiritual marketplace contains both serious teachers and exploitative operations. Some general principles for distinguishing: legitimate teachers and traditions generally do not charge substantial sums for entry-level engagement, do not promise rapid initiation or rapid spiritual development, do not isolate practitioners from outside contact or other teachers, do not require sexual contact for spiritual progress, are open about their own teachers and lineage, and are accountable to communities beyond their immediate students. The opposite of any of these is a substantial warning sign.

For trauma-informed work specifically: any practitioner who claims to do trauma work should be able to name their training (Somatic Experiencing, Sensorimotor Psychotherapy, NARM, EMDR, IFS, etc.), should hold appropriate credentials, and should be willing to discuss the limits of their competence. Anyone claiming to do trauma work without specific training and credentialing should be approached with substantial skepticism.

§ VI.VI the work of repair

Sustained practice across decades

The work of repair as the document develops it is sustained ritual practice rather than therapeutic concept. It engages what you inherits — including the violence at the origin of your lineage configuration. The work is not finite; the work distinguishes the historical from the personal; the work names violence directly rather than allegorizing or softening it; the work distinguishes what is repairable from what is not (large-scale institutional violence is not repairable by individual practice; your own relationship to the inheritance is repairable, slowly, over years); the work proceeds by stages with practitioner support; the work is sustained.

The principal contemporary frameworks. Resmaa Menakem, My Grandmother's Hands (2017) and The Quaking of America (2022) — somatic abolitionism, the distinction between clean pain (productive suffering that fosters growth) and dirty pain (suffering that stems from avoidance), specific somatic practices grounded in African Diasporic substrate. Daniel Foor, Ancestral Medicine (2017) — methodology specifically for descendants of those who enslaved others; the four-stage framework with practitioner support required for stages two through four. Sarah Schulman, Conflict Is Not Abuse (Arsenal Pulp, 2016) — the boundaries-of-repair frame, distinguishing what is repairable (relationship work) from what is not (large-scale institutional and historical violence).

The lineage-tradition contributions. The Welsh stream contributes the elegiac form (suffering held without dissolving), the holy well tradition (sustained healing site), the contemplative-mystical tradition (interior work over years). The West African Diaspora stream contributes the spiritual baths and uncrossing rituals (direct repair apparatus), the ring shout (community embodied repair), the ancestor work specifically (trauma metabolized across generations). The Continental Hermetic-Masonic stream contributes the alchemical nigredo as theological frame for suffering as transformative dissolution rather than pathology, the Reintegration framework as cosmic-scale work, the theurgic purification practices as staged ritual structure.

The practical shape of repair as sustained practice for you: it begins in the body, not the head. It distinguishes the historical from the personal — what her ancestors did and what was done to her ancestors is not her personal moral burden, but the inheritance is in her body. It names the violence directly: the Dutch ancestor raped one of her enslaved ancestors. The practice does not soften this. It distinguishes what is repairable from what is not — the original violence and the centuries of slavery are not repairable; your own relationship to the inheritance is, slowly. The practice has practitioner support; this is not solo work. The practice is not finite — sustained relationship to the inheritance that develops over time, not a project with a completion date.

§ VI.IX trance and the journey

Automatic writing, deeper scrying, the hedge-crossing

The operative-witchcraft tradition is fundamentally trance-based. The witch's flight to the Sabbath, the hedge-crossing, the journey to the meeting in the green, the descent into the underworld — these are trance-technologies, not metaphor. The contemporary witchcraft register has substantially under-developed the trance dimension; the document has handled devotional ecstasy (the candle-and-altar work, the lunar-architecture work, the reading-and-attention work) but not technique-of-ecstasy as developed practice. By years three to ten, if your work has been operative-witchcraft for substantial time can engage trance-technology as an explicit dimension of the practice. This section is the framework.

The trance tradition's scholarly foundations. Carlo Ginzburg's The Night Battles: Witchcraft and Agrarian Cults in the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Centuries (1966; English 1983) is the foundational scholarly text — the documented Friulian benandanti tradition of trance-traveling witches who fought for the harvest in spectral combat. Ecstasies: Deciphering the Witches' Sabbath (1989; English 1991) is the substantial cross-European synthesis of the trance-tradition underneath the witch-trial record. Eva Pócs's Between the Living and the Dead: A Perspective on Witches and Seers in the Early Modern Age (Central European University Press, 1998) treats the Hungarian táltos and shamanic-witch tradition substantively. Emma Wilby's Cunning Folk and Familiar Spirits and The Visions of Isobel Gowdie (already cited) develop the British trance-tradition. Ronald Hutton's Shamans: Siberian Spirituality and the Western Imagination (Hambledon, 2001) and The Witch: A History of Fear, from Ancient Times to the Present (Yale, 2017) provide the critical historiography of the trance-shamanism category specifically. The contemporary practitioner-trance literature: Andrew D. Chumbley's Azoëtia (Xoanon, 1992/2002) is the foundational small-press grimoire of the contemporary trance-tradition in the Cultus Sabbati register; Lee Morgan's A Deed Without a Name: Unearthing the Legacy of Traditional Witchcraft (Moon Books, 2012) and Sounds of Infinity (Mandrake, 2018) are the principal contemporary practitioner-author texts on traditional witch trance. Robin Artisson's The Witching Way of the Hollow Hill (self-published, 2006) and the broader catalogue treat the American traditional-witchcraft trance-tradition. Rae Beth's Hedge Witch: A Guide to Solitary Witchcraft (Robert Hale, 1990) names the hedge-witchcraft register specifically — "hedge" is the trance-crossing between worlds.

Automatic writing as a specific trance-technology. The practice is small to introduce: a fixed time, a fixed surface, a brief invocation, a hand on the page, no editing. The training is in repeating it across months until the threshold-crossing becomes reliable. The contemporary practitioner-handbook is Donald Tyson's Communing with the Spirits: The Magical Practice of Necromancy (Llewellyn, 2007). The literary-historical apparatus: W. B. Yeats's A Vision (1925; revised 1937; the Connie Hood Macmillan critical edition is the standard) — the most consequential modern literary engagement with automatic writing; the system came through his wife George Yeats's mediumship across years of sustained practice. C.G. Jung's The Red Book / Liber Novus (Norton, 2009; Sonu Shamdasani edition) — active imagination as the psychological-clinical relative of automatic writing; the documented primary-source of Jung's own decade of sustained engagement with imaginal material. Hilma af Klint's notebooks — the spiritualist-medium Swedish painter (1862-1944) whose entire body of work came through mediumistic dictation; the Hilma af Klint: Notes and Methods edition (University of Chicago, 2018, Christine Burgin edition) is the substantial primary-source documentation of her practice. André Breton's Manifestoes of Surrealism (1924; 1930) — the surrealist deployment of automatic writing as creative method; the substantial twentieth-century literary tradition descending from this. Jane Roberts / Seth material — controversial and substantial corpus; the foundational text is The Seth Material (Prentice-Hall, 1970); read with awareness of the substantive contemporary criticism of the channeling-tradition Roberts inaugurated.

The deeper scrying that opens at depth. The water-bowl and black-mirror practice from chapter 3 § III.II matures across years into substantive scrying-engagement. The Enochian scrying tradition that John Dee and Edward Kelley developed across the 1580s is the principal Western practitioner-scrying primary-source corpus; Joseph Peterson's edition of John Dee's Five Books of Mystery (Weiser, 2003) is the standard scholarly-practitioner edition. The PGM scrying procedures (PGM IV.222-260, V.1-53, IV.930-1114, VII.250-254, and VII.319-334) become readable as your scrying matures. The lecanomantic and lychnomantic (lamp-scrying) traditions in the Hellenistic primary literature open here. The hedge-witchcraft and traditional-witchcraft trance-scrying — the witch's mirror used not for divination but as threshold-crossing apparatus — is the operative-trance scrying that Chumbley, Morgan, Artisson develop in their respective small-press traditions.

The hedge-crossing as developed practice. The hedge-witchcraft register specifically names trance-traveling — going across the hedge, crossing into the other-realm — as the central witch-tradition technology. Rae Beth's Hedge Witch is you-introduction; the more substantive contemporary treatments are in Morgan, Chumbley, Artisson, and the broader small-press traditional-witchcraft literature. The hedge-crossing is not journey-work in the indigenous-shamanic register (which the contemporary practice has substantially appropriated and which serious witchcraft practitioners are rigorous about not borrowing); it is the European-tradition equivalent — substantively documented in the witch-trial record (Ginzburg's benandanti, Pócs's táltos, Wilby's Gowdie), accessible through the contemporary practitioner-tradition that has reconstructed the apparatus. The substantive year-engagement with hedge-crossing develops across years three to seven for if your work opens that direction; it requires substantial preparatory operative-and-trance work and is not material for the year-one or year-three practitioner. Substantial caveat: trance-work that opens you to substantive other-realm contact also opens you to substantive psychological and psychiatric vulnerability. The CPTSD bracketing from chapter 5 § V.VI applies with even greater weight here. Trance-and-journey work for a body with substantial trauma history requires practitioner support — both somatic-trauma-aware practitioner support and ideally an experienced-witchcraft-practitioner mentor who can hold the working alongside.

§ VI.X grimoire tradition at depth

The medieval and early-modern primary literature in working engagement

Chapter 3 § III.VI established the grimoire-tradition as territory and named the principal texts. This section is where if your work has matured operatively can engage the primary texts as actual working-references. The depth-engagement is years three to ten and beyond.

The substantive working-engagement with grimoires has structure. Read scholarly editions — the Peterson editions for the Solomonic and Honorian material, the Tyson edition of Agrippa, the Greer-Warnock and Attrell-Porreca editions of the Picatrix, Kieckhefer's Forbidden Rites for the Munich Manual. The Mathers-tradition translations (his Greater Key of Solomon and Goetia) are foundational but should be read as historical artifacts rather than as the most reliable contemporary access; the contemporary scholarly editions are substantively better. Engage one grimoire at a time; if you reads broadly across the grimoire literature without engaging any one substantively develops thin knowledge. Working from grimoires requires substantial preparatory practice, ethical clarity, and ideally direct engagement with practitioners working the same tradition; the grimoires assume an apparatus (planetary timing, ritual purification, specific tool-construction, the operative-magical lineage that the contemporary practice has substantially lost) that the contemporary practitioner has to reconstruct or adapt deliberately. Aaron Leitch's Secrets of the Magickal Grimoires (already cited) is the principal contemporary practitioner-text on the engagement-protocol.

The principal grimoires for substantive depth-engagement, organized by tradition. The Hellenistic / late-antique stratum: the Greek Magical Papyri (Betz edition) at depth — specific procedures (PGM IV.475-829 the Mithras Liturgy, PGM XII the substantial talismanic material, PGM IV the Hekate hymn for practitioners whose Hellenic-chthonic work has opened) become engageable. The medieval Hermetic-Solomonic stratum: Agrippa's Three Books Books II and III at depth (the celestial and ceremonial-religious magic); the Picatrix at depth; the Sworn Book of Honorius (Peterson edition, Ibis Press, 2016); the Book of Abramelin (Dehn edition); the Munich Manual through Kieckhefer; the various recensions of the Key of Solomon. The early-modern French and English stratum: the Lesser Key / Goetia (Peterson edition); the Heptameron; the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century French grimoires (the Grimoire of Pope Honorius, the Black Pullet, the Grand Grimoire — Peterson and others have edited contemporary editions). The Iberian and Latin American stratum: the Books of St. Cyprian tradition through José Leitão's and Humberto Maggi's editions (Hadean Press); the substantial Portuguese-and-Spanish-and-Brazilian Cyprian-tradition material. The contemporary practitioner-grimoire stratum: Chumbley's Azoëtia and One: The Grimoire of the Golden Toad; Schulke's catalogue (already cited); Stratton-Kent's Encyclopaedia Goetica trilogy; Sara Mastros's Sorcery of Solomon and Orphic Hymns Grimoire; Jake Stratton-Kent's broader catalogue; the Scarlet Imprint catalogue as a whole.

The Goetic tradition specifically. The seventy-two demons of the Lesser Key are the most-engaged primary-source corpus in the contemporary grimoiric-magical practitioner literature. Substantial caveat: the demonological tradition is the most ethically contested grimoire-tradition; engagement requires the appropriate framework, substantial protective apparatus, and ideally direct teacher-engagement. The contemporary practitioner-Goetic literature includes substantial work by Frater Acher, Aaron Leitch, Jason Miller, and others; the territory is well-developed in contemporary practitioner-publishing.

§ VI.XI spirit work distinct from ancestor

Genius loci, fae, named dead, saints

The ancestor work from chapter 5 § V.VII develops the relationships with your own bloodline-dead. The familiar work from chapter 4 § IV.VII develops the relationships with specific animal-spirit presences. This section names the broader spirit-work territory that opens at depth — the relationships with non-bloodline spirits you may come into engagement with across the deeper years. Four principal categories: the genius loci (the spirits of specific places), the fae (the otherworld-tradition presences), the named dead beyond ancestors (specific historical figures, the dead you have known but who are not bloodline), and the saints (the operative-religious figures whose territories were named in chapter 3 § III.IV).

Genius loci — the spirits of places. The Roman tradition is primary: the genius loci as the resident spiritual presence of a specific location, deserving offering, capable of relationship, sometimes named, sometimes recognized only as the place's character. Pliny the Elder, Ovid's Fasti, Servius's commentary on Virgil document the substantial Roman primary apparatus. R.J. Stewart's Earth Light (1990) and Power Within the Land (1992) develop the contemporary practitioner-apparatus for engaging land-spirits in the British and Celtic traditions. Belden Lane's The Solace of Fierce Landscapes (Oxford, 1998) and Backcountry Reckonings (Oxford, 2014) develop the contemplative-geography register. The contemporary practitioner's land-sit practice from chapter 4 § IV.V matures into substantive genius-loci relationship across years; by year five to seven, you have substantial relationship with at least one specific land-spirit, knows its name (sometimes given, sometimes constructed by you in collaboration with the place), makes offerings, asks before working with the land's resources.

The fae — the otherworld-tradition presences. The Welsh Tylwyth Teg, the Irish Aos Sí, the Scottish Sìth, the Cornish Pobel Vean, the Manx Sleih Veggey, the Continental fairy traditions, the broader European otherworld inhabitants. Substantial caveat: the contemporary popular fairy-and-fae literature is largely New Age inflation that bears little relation to the historical and folk-traditional figures; the serious engagement is substantively different. § VI.XII operative herbalism at depth · the poison path

The witching herbs and the baneful tradition with substantial cautions

The herbalism-and-witch's-garden work from chapter 4 § IV.VI develops across years two and three; by year three to five, if your practice has matured into operative herbalism can begin substantive engagement with the poison path — the European witching-herb tradition the historical witch trials repeatedly named, the flying-ointment apparatus, the operative-and-medicinal use of plants that contemporary herbalism otherwise treats only as toxic. This section is at depth. Substantial cautions apply.

The principal witching herbs, with their primary working applications and their toxicity classifications. Mandragora officinarum (mandrake) — root used in flying ointments and as protective amulet; toxic at moderate dose; the Mediterranean European mandrake distinguished from the American May-apple (Podophyllum peltatum) which is sometimes mistakenly labeled mandrake. Atropa belladonna (deadly nightshade, belladonna) — leaf and berry used in flying ointments and visionary work; can kill at small dose; the principal active compounds (atropine, scopolamine, hyoscyamine) are pharmacologically significant. Hyoscyamus niger (henbane, also other hyoscyamus species) — leaf and seed used in flying ointments; powerful tropane alkaloids; substantial toxicity. Datura stramonium and other datura species (jimson weed, thorn apple) — leaf, flower, seed; the most powerful and most dangerous of the principal witching herbs; can kill at small dose; the visionary effects are substantial but the toxicity is real and the dose-response is highly variable. Aconitum napellus (monkshood, wolfsbane, aconite) — root and leaf; can kill on skin contact at sufficient quantity; one of the most poisonous plants in Europe; used historically in flying ointments and as poison. Conium maculatum (poison hemlock) — entire plant; can kill at small dose; the plant of Socrates's death; used historically in operative-magical work primarily for binding-and-killing applications. Artemisia absinthium (wormwood) — leaf; less toxic than the others but accumulates with chronic use; foundational in absinthe and in the western witching-herb tradition. Mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris), yarrow (Achillea millefolium), vervain (Verbena officinalis), rue (Ruta graveolens) — the broader witching-herb apparatus, less acutely toxic but operatively significant.

The flying-ointment tradition. The historical witch-trial record contains substantial documentation of unguents made from baneful-plant material that produced substantial visionary and out-of-body experience. The contemporary reconstruction is well-developed in the small-press literature: Coby Michael's The Poison Path Herbal develops the contemporary apparatus in accessible practitioner-form; Sarah Anne Lawless's older work (with the controversy noted) was substantively important; Daniel A. Schulke's Veneficium is the small-press grimoiric depth-engagement; Thomas Hatsis's The Witches' Ointment: The Secret History of Psychedelic Magic (Park Street, 2015) is the substantial contemporary historical-and-practitioner study. Substantial caveat: flying-ointment work is genuinely dangerous; the baneful plants involved can kill; the dose-response is highly individual and variable; engagement requires substantive study, careful sourcing, ethical clarity about why you are undertaking the work, and ideally direct teaching from an experienced practitioner. The contemporary harm-reduction principles for psychedelic-assisted work (sitter present, low-and-slow dose, prepared environment, integration support) apply with even greater weight given the toxicity of the plants involved.

Sourcing and ethics. The baneful plants are not freely available at most herb-suppliers; some require specific cultivation; some are restricted in some jurisdictions. Harold Roth's Alchemy Works (alchemy-works.com) is the principal contemporary North American mail-order source for ethically-grown witching-herb seeds and plants. The wildcrafting ethics from § IV.VI apply with particular weight: never harvest from threatened populations, ask before taking, leave offerings, take far less than you might want. Some practitioners cultivate their own poison-garden across years; this is the more substantive practitioner-relationship and the principal recommendation for serious engagement. The legal-jurisdictional question varies — many of the witching herbs are legal to grow but illegal to sell as medicinal or psychoactive products; you should know her local jurisdiction.
