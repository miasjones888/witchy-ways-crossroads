## I. Position — Why Death Practice is the Central Work of Late Practice

The conventional contemporary American assumption is that death is something that happens at the end of life, to be deferred and managed when it arrives. The contemplative-religious traditions across most of human history have held a different position: death is the constitutive horizon against which a life is lived, and developing the capacity to die well is one of the central works of mature practice. Stephen Jenkinson — who directed palliative care at Mount Sinai Hospital of Toronto for years before founding the Orphan Wisdom School in 2010 — names this as the recovery of "death literacy" in cultures that have lost it. The Buddhist Five Remembrances tradition, the Christian *memento mori* literature, the *Ars Moriendi* tradition of late medieval Europe, the Sufi practice of dying-before-you-die, and most indigenous and traditional religious practices treat death as ongoing practice rather than as terminal event.

For the keyed reader's practice as v10 develops it — Hekatean, chthonic, Sabbatic-traditional, animist, ancestral — death practice is not an addition but a deepening of what is already present. The chthonic stream in v10 is already death-adjacent (Hekate as goddess of the dead, Persephone as queen of the underworld, the Eleusinian mysteries as initiation into the mystery of death). The ancestral work in v10 § XVII is already death practice in one register (relationship with the dead). The grief work in v10 § VII via Weller is already death practice. What Ch 6 develops is what these become when the practitioner herself is approaching the threshold — the daily contact deepening into sustained practice with one's own dying as the subject.

The three streams contribute distinct material. The Welsh stream brings the wake, the keening, the sin-eater, the funeral procession with its specific ritual structure, the Welsh folk-Christian death-and-resurrection theology. The West African Diaspora stream brings the sit-up wake of the Gullah Geechee, the body-washing, the bottle tree, the burial society, the second-line procession, the African-derived theology of the soul "crossing over" to the ancestors. The Continental Hermetic-Masonic stream brings the alchemical death-and-rebirth (the nigredo dissolution, the rubedo rebirth), the Masonic third-degree raising (Hiram Abiff), the Martinist Reintegration. The traditions converge on the proposition that death is not the end of practice but its arrival.

## II. Welsh Death Practice

The Welsh death tradition preserved substantial pre-Christian and folk-Christian material across the early modern and modern periods, much of it documented in the early-tourist literature and the 19th-20th century folk-life scholarship.

### The wake (gwylnos)

The Welsh wake — *gwylnos* or *wylnos* — was the night-vigil held over the body between death and burial. Family members and friends took turns holding vigil; the house was never empty until the day of the funeral. Dialectical variations included *onglos* (Llanwenog, Cardiganshire), *winglos* (north Pembrokeshire), and *wynglos* (Llŷn peninsula). In some regions the wake was held every night with food and drink; the proceedings were respectful rather than celebratory.

Under the influence of the Methodist Movement in the 19th century, the *gwylnos* became more reverential — closer to a prayer meeting. A recognized singer would be invited to sing an elegy over the body, an echo of the elegiac poetry composed by the court bards of the medieval Welsh princes.

### Keening

Keening — the ritual lament for the dead, performed traditionally by women — is more thoroughly documented in Irish than in Welsh tradition (where the Methodist influence partially suppressed it earlier than in rural Ireland). The *gwylnos*-elegy practice is the Welsh continuation, with the bardic elegy as its sustained form. Contemporary Welsh-tradition death practice for the keyed reader can recover this through the Welsh-language poetry tradition (engaging the *Llyfr Aneirin* for *Y Gododdin*'s extended lament for the fallen, the medieval Welsh elegies, and the contemporary Welsh-language poetic tradition's continuation of elegiac form).

### The sin-eater

The sin-eater — *bwytäwr pechod* in Welsh — was a folk practitioner whose ritual function was to eat ceremonial bread and drink ale placed on or near the corpse, thereby taking on the deceased's unconfessed sins and freeing the soul "from walking after they were dead." Documented across Wales, Welsh-border English counties, the West Country, parts of Scotland, and (less commonly) Ireland. The practice was most active from at least the 17th century into the 19th, with the last documented Welsh sin-eaters operating in the late 19th century.

The sin-eater was a marginal social figure — abhorred as ritually unclean, living apart from the community, often impoverished. Aubrey's 17th-century account: a loaf of bread was given to the sin-eater over the corpse, with a maple bowl of beer and sixpence; the sin-eater drank, ate, took the sixpence, and "took upon himself all the sins of the defunct." The practice has substantial Christian-folk theological apparatus — the bread and beer echoing the Eucharist, the sins literally consumed — but it operates in tension with orthodox Christian teaching, which is why the sin-eater was simultaneously called for and stigmatized.

For the keyed reader, the sin-eater tradition is one of the more distinctive Welsh death-practice elements. Contemporary Welsh-tradition recovery work does not require literal sin-eating; the practitioner can engage the tradition by understanding its ritual logic — the willingness to take on weight that is being released by the dying — and by adapting elements (specific ritual food at the wake, specific ritual presence with the body in its dying hours).

### The funeral procession

Welsh funeral procession customs preserved specific ritual structure. At each cross-way between the house and the church, the bier was laid down, the company knelt, and the Lord's Prayer was repeated. The procession often involved substantial walking (rural Welsh churches were sometimes distant from outlying farmhouses), with the ritual stops marking the journey out of ordinary geography into the church's threshold space.

### The folk-Christian theological substrate

Welsh death practice operated within a Welsh folk-Christian frame that was distinct from English or American mainstream Protestantism. The strong presence of the *anadl* (breath-as-soul-wind) framing in Welsh mystical theology, the attention to the dying breath as the moment of the soul's departure, the specific Welsh-language prayers and elegies, the continuity of pre-Christian elements (the marking of cross-ways, the night-vigil, the sin-eater) within nominally Christian practice — all of this constitutes a distinctive Welsh approach to death that the keyed reader can recover.

For Ch 6 chapter writing: the Welsh stream contributes the *gwylnos* (the night-vigil-with-the-body), the elegy (sustained bardic-poetic engagement with the death), the sin-eater logic (willingness to take on weight in service of release), the cross-way ritual (marking thresholds in the journey out), and the *anadl* framing (breath as soul-wind, the dying breath as the soul's departure).

## III. West African Diaspora Death Practice

The Black American death tradition, with substantial West and Central African inheritance preserved through enslavement and continuing into contemporary practice, is one of the most ritually developed living death-practice traditions in the contemporary American religious landscape.

### Gullah Geechee specifically

The Gullah Geechee burial and funerary tradition preserves substantial Africa-derived practice that was lost in mainland Black American populations. Specific elements:

**The sit-up.** As a community member is dying, friends, family, guests, and members of the burial society gather around the dying person to "help them cross." The dying person is not left alone; the community holds vigil through the dying process. When the last breath is drawn, everyone present gives a loud shriek as notification that another soul has "done crossed ober" into the spirit world.

**The body-washing.** Specific community members are sent to wash and dress the body. Elders perform this function for those of the same gender as the deceased (women preparing women, men preparing men). The body is dressed in a white sheet and placed in a casket made traditionally from four pieces of wood and covered with black cloth inside. The pots used to wash the body are placed on top of the deceased's grave — a continuing practice in Gullah Geechee burial grounds.

**Grave goods.** The Gullah Geechee tradition continues the West African practice of placing specific objects on graves — often the last objects used by the deceased, broken pottery, blue glass, the body-washing pots. The breaking of the objects is intentional; broken things accompany the spirit to the world of the ancestors.

**Bottle trees.** The blue bottle tree — often a leafless tree with cobalt-blue glass bottles placed on its branches — appears in Gullah Geechee yards as both protective practice (the bottles trap evil spirits) and memorialization (the tree honors loved ones who have passed). The tradition is a direct West Central African inheritance brought through enslavement.

**The burial society.** The community-organized burial society is a distinctive Gullah Geechee institution that provided economic and ritual support for proper funerary practice in the period when insurance companies refused to sell policies to formerly enslaved Black families. The burial societies organized the wake, the body preparation, the procession, the burial, and the ongoing memorialization. Many continue in continuous operation in Gullah Geechee communities.

For the keyed reader with Carolinas paternal ancestry: the Gullah Geechee specifics are the most directly inheritance-relevant if her line is Sea Island-and-Lowcountry-rooted. Penn Center on St. Helena Island and the Gullah/Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor Commission preserve the tradition in living form.

### New Orleans second-line jazz funeral

The New Orleans Black American funeral tradition is one of the most visible continuing African-derived death practices in contemporary America. The tradition has West African origins — the second-line dance form derives from West African circle dances, where children danced in a second circle around the main circle of adults; the dances came to New Orleans through enslavement and were incorporated into funeral processions.

The structure is two-line: the **first or main line** consists of musicians (brass band), funeral directors, family, and friends of the dead. The **second line** is the crowd marching behind — mourners and revelers who join the procession. The procession marches solemnly to the grave; after burial, the music shifts and the second line dances and celebrates on the route away from the grave. The ritual logic: the body is honored in solemnity at the grave; the soul is celebrated as it departs to join the ancestors.

The historical development: as slavery was abolished, Black New Orleans funerary practice became elaborate, supported by Social Aid and Pleasure Clubs that provided dues-paying members with brass bands for funerals. By the early 20th century the funerals had become forums for the new music being developed in Black New Orleans — jazz — eventually becoming the jazz funeral tradition. The Black Men of Labor and similar contemporary social aid organizations continue the tradition with substantial cultural pride and ritual seriousness.

For the keyed reader, the second-line tradition is geographically Louisiana-specific (New Orleans and adjacent communities) but theologically representative of broader Black American Christian-syncretic death practice — celebrating the soul's departure to the ancestors while honoring the body in solemnity.

### Ancestor veneration as ongoing death practice

In the West African Diaspora register, death practice does not end at burial. The relationship with the deceased continues through ancestor veneration as ongoing devotional practice. The Daniel Foor methodology developed in v10 § XVII engages this directly — relationship with the well-resourced ancestors who have crossed over, sustained ritual contact, food and drink offerings, conversation. The Stephanie Rose Bird tradition develops it within the hoodoo/conjure register specifically. The Mallorie Vaudoise approach (*Honoring Your Ancestors*, 2019, named in v10 § III on altar work) treats it as the apparatus for sustained relationship with the dead.

For Ch 6 chapter writing: the West African Diaspora stream contributes the sit-up (community vigil through the dying process), the body-washing as community ritual, the grave goods and bottle trees as continuing relationship with the deceased, the burial society as community infrastructure for proper ritual, the second-line procession as celebration of the soul's departure, the sustained ancestor veneration as relationship-after-death. The frame: death is community work, not solo; the soul does not depart, the soul transitions; the relationship continues across the threshold.

## IV. Continental Hermetic-Masonic Death Practice

The Western Hermetic-Masonic stream's death apparatus is distinctively allegorical-initiatic rather than mortuary-folk. The death practice operates as ritual symbolism of the practitioner's transformation in life, with the bodily death framed as the culmination of an initiatic process that has been ongoing throughout the practitioner's mature work.

### Alchemical death-and-rebirth

The classical alchemical work proceeds through stages, the central of which is the *nigredo* — the blackening, the calcination and putrefaction in which the old form must be destroyed before the new can emerge. The *Emerald Tablet* of Hermes Trismegistus and the *Atalanta Fugiens* of Michael Maier and the broader alchemical corpus work this image extensively: true transformation requires the death of the old self before the new can come into being.

The four-stage alchemical sequence — *nigredo* (blackening), *albedo* (whitening), *citrinitas* (yellowing), *rubedo* (reddening) — is read by the Hermetic tradition as both the chemical process of transmutation and the spiritual process of the practitioner's transformation. The death of the *nigredo* is the death of attachment, illusion, false self; the eventual *rubedo* is the rebirth into the perfected state. The death is not a catastrophe but a necessity.

For the Hermetic practitioner, this means that death practice is built into the apparatus from the beginning. Every working that involves serious internal transformation participates in the alchemical death-and-rebirth structure. The bodily death at the end of life is the culminating instance of a process that has been ongoing across the practice; the practitioner who has been doing serious alchemical work has been dying-and-being-reborn repeatedly across years.

### The Masonic third degree raising — Hiram Abiff

The central death-and-rebirth ritual of mainstream Anglo-American Freemasonry is the third-degree raising, which dramatizes the legend of Hiram Abiff. Hiram is presented as the chief architect of King Solomon's Temple. Three fellowcraft masons ambush him as he leaves the building, demanding the secrets of a master mason. He refuses; they kill him; they bury his body in a shallow grave marked by a sprig of acacia. The body is found, the murderers brought to justice, and Hiram is "raised" by King Solomon — though the original word of the master mason is lost and replaced with a substitute.

The third-degree candidate participates in the legend ritually: enacts the death of Hiram, is raised in ritual analogue to Hiram's raising. The ritual operates as initiation into the mystery of death and rebirth, with the candidate symbolically dying as the old self and being raised into the new. The substitute word that replaces the lost original is itself part of the teaching — what is lost in death cannot be fully recovered; what is found is something new, related but not identical.

For Continental Hermetic-Masonic streams (which the keyed reader's inheritance more directly is), the third-degree raising is the entry to a much more elaborate death-and-rebirth apparatus. The high degrees of Memphis-Misraim, the Rectified Scottish Rite, and the Martinist working substantially extend the death-rebirth material — particularly the Egyptian-Hermetic register of Memphis-Misraim, which works the Egyptian Book of the Dead and the Osiris myth (the dismembered god restored by Isis) as central material.

### Martinist Reintegration

The Martinist tradition centers explicitly on the doctrine of the Fall and Reintegration. The original Adam fell from divine source into materiality; the work of the Christian mystic-esotericist is to participate in the process of return. Reintegration is achieved progressively through the practitioner's interior work, with the bodily death as the moment when the work either bears its full fruit or is incomplete and must be continued.

The Martinist apparatus engages death practice differently than the alchemical or Masonic streams: more interior, more contemplative, less ritualized. Saint-Martin's "Way of the Heart" emphasizes direct interior union with the divine, with the practitioner's death as the threshold across which Reintegration is completed (or revealed to have been completed in life).

For the keyed reader, the Continental Hermetic-Masonic stream contributes the alchemical death-and-rebirth as the structure of mature practice; the third-degree raising as the initiation into the mystery; the Reintegration framework as the orientation that locates the practitioner's death within a larger process of return. The frame: death is the structure of practice itself, not a discrete event; the practitioner has been dying across years; the bodily death is the culmination, not the catastrophe.

## V. Contemporary Contemplative Death Practice

The contemporary infrastructure for death practice as a contemplative discipline has matured substantially in the last twenty-five years, with the death-doula movement, the death-positive movement, and the contemplative end-of-life-care literature becoming widely available.

### Stephen Jenkinson

Stephen Jenkinson (born 1954) is the Canadian writer, teacher, and grief-literacy advocate who directed palliative care at Mount Sinai Hospital of Toronto before founding the **Orphan Wisdom School** with his wife Nathalie Roy in 2010. His major works:

- ***Die Wise: A Manifesto for Sanity and Soul*** (North Atlantic Books, 2015). Jenkinson's history, explication, and exploration of his approach to dying — drawn from his years in what he calls "the death trade" plus what he has learned from indigenous peoples. Critical of dominant Western cultural denials around death; teaches the skills of dying that have to be learned through living deeply. Foreword by Dr. Martin Shaw. The single most substantive contemporary text on death practice.
- ***Come of Age: The Case for Elderhood in a Time of Trouble*** (North Atlantic Books, 2018). Argues for the necessity of true elderhood — being willing to be old, to be a steward, to face the mortality of the self and the world honestly.
- The Orphan Wisdom School continues as Jenkinson's teaching institution.

### Frank Ostaseski

Frank Ostaseski is an internationally respected Buddhist teacher, cofounder of the **Zen Hospice Project** (San Francisco, 1987-2018, the first Buddhist hospice in the United States), and founder of the **Metta Institute**. *The Five Invitations: Discovering What Death Can Teach Us About Living Fully* (Flatiron, 2017) distills his decades of bedside experience with the dying into five contemplative invitations: don't wait, welcome everything (push away nothing), bring your whole self to the experience, find a place of rest in the middle of things, cultivate "don't know" mind. The book is the major contemporary contemplative practitioner-text on dying as practice.

### Joan Halifax (Roshi)

Roshi Joan Halifax, PhD, is a Buddhist teacher, founder and head teacher of **Upaya Zen Center** in Santa Fe, New Mexico, and was formerly an anthropologist at Columbia University and the University of Miami School of Medicine. She founded the **Project on Being with Dying** in 1994, which has trained hundreds of healthcare professionals in contemplative care of the dying. *Being with Dying: Cultivating Compassion and Fearlessness in the Presence of Death* (Shambhala, 2008; reissued 2024) is the central practitioner-text. Halifax and Ostaseski have collaborated on retreats and training programs over decades.

### Sallie Tisdale

Sallie Tisdale's *Advice for Future Corpses (and Those Who Love Them): A Practical Perspective on Death and Dying* (Touchstone, 2018) is a working hospice nurse's substantively practical, religiously omnivorous, beautifully-written guide to dying. Less explicitly Buddhist than Ostaseski or Halifax; more direct on the bodily-medical realities. A useful complement to the contemplative texts.

### Stephen Levine

Stephen Levine (1937-2016) was a poet, teacher, and author whose work pioneered the contemporary contemplative engagement with death. *A Year to Live: How to Live This Year as If It Were Your Last* (Three Rivers Press, 1997) — already named in v10 § VIII — proposes a year-long practice of treating each day as if it were the last in one's life, with specific contemplative exercises and meditations. The Levine approach is more contemplative-poetic than Jenkinson's *Die Wise* and less institutional than Halifax's; useful as a practitioner-direct text.

### The death-doula and death-positive movements

The contemporary death-doula movement — bringing companion-care to the dying outside institutional medical contexts — has grown substantially since approximately 2015. The **National End-of-Life Doula Alliance (NEDA)** maintains a directory of trained doulas. The **Death-Positive movement** (centered on Caitlin Doughty and the Order of the Good Death) advocates cultural reckoning with death and direct engagement with the bodily realities of dying. *Smoke Gets in Your Eyes* (W. W. Norton, 2014) and *From Here to Eternity* (W. W. Norton, 2017) by Doughty are the major popular texts.

For the keyed reader, the contemporary contemplative death practice infrastructure provides supportive apparatus that supplements the lineage-tradition material. Jenkinson's *Die Wise* is the recommended single-volume entry; Ostaseski's *Five Invitations* is the most directly contemplative-practitioner text; Halifax's *Being with Dying* is the major institutional-training text; Tisdale's *Advice for Future Corpses* is the most practical-bodily text.

## VI. Integration Across the Three Streams

The keyed reader's inheritance carries three distinct death-practice traditions, each with its own ritual structure, theological substrate, and practitioner apparatus. The integration question — how a single practitioner honors all three without flattening any — is the most theologically interesting question Ch 6 generates.

The traditions are not equivalent. Welsh death practice operates in a folk-Christian frame with substantial pre-Christian substrate; the *gwylnos* and the elegy and the sin-eater are folk-popular practices, not initiatic. West African Diaspora death practice operates in a Black American Christian-syncretic frame with substantial West African continuity; the sit-up and the second-line and the bottle tree and the burial society are community practices grounded in continuing African theology of the soul's transition. Continental Hermetic-Masonic death practice operates in an initiatic-allegorical frame; the third-degree raising and the alchemical work and Reintegration are esoteric ritual practices for the initiated practitioner specifically.

The integration question proceeds through several layers.

**At the level of personal dying practice.** The contemporary contemplative apparatus (Jenkinson, Ostaseski, Halifax, Tisdale, Levine) provides the substrate for the practitioner's own daily contemplative engagement with mortality. The lineage-stream specifics deepen this substrate without replacing it. The keyed reader's daily practice can hold the *anadl* framing (breath as soul-wind, drawn from Welsh) and the alchemical death-and-rebirth structure (drawn from Hermetic) and the sustained ancestor relationship (drawn from West African Diaspora) all simultaneously, because these are different registers of the same fundamental work.

**At the level of community ritual.** The streams contribute different ritual material. The wake structure (Welsh *gwylnos*) and the sit-up structure (Gullah Geechee) are similar but not identical — both involve the night-vigil with the body, but the Welsh version is more reverent-prayer-meeting and the Gullah Geechee version more participatory-community-celebratory. The procession structures differ: Welsh is the ritualized cross-way-stops procession with the Lord's Prayer; New Orleans is the two-line jazz funeral with the shift from solemn-march to celebration. The integration: at the keyed reader's eventual death, the community rituals can draw from both traditions, with the specific shape determined by what community is actually present and what rituals are alive in it.

**At the level of esoteric ritual.** The Continental Hermetic-Masonic third-degree raising and Reintegration framework are specifically initiatic; they apply to the keyed reader if she has undertaken Co-Masonic or related initiation. Without initiation, the apparatus remains as conceptual substrate (the alchemical death-rebirth as a way of understanding the practice) rather than as ritual reality.

**At the level of ancestral relationship.** All three streams develop sustained relationship with the dead. The keyed reader's death practice means she eventually becomes one of the ancestors. The Welsh stream contributes the elegiac form (her death will be sung in some way). The West African Diaspora stream contributes the ongoing relationship (she will be a presence at altars across years). The Hermetic-Masonic stream contributes the initiatic transmission (whatever she has been able to transmit will continue without her).

**The hard question.** How does the keyed reader honor all three streams when the streams enter through different points and at different angles to her life? The Welsh stream is matrilineal, unentangled, recovery work in its more straightforward form. The West African Diaspora and Hermetic-Masonic streams are entwined through the violence at their origin. The contemporary contemplative apparatus is not lineage but supplementary practice. The integration cannot be neat; it cannot be doctrinally settled. The keyed reader's death practice, as it develops over decades, becomes its own working answer to the integration question — a working that no advance theory can resolve.

## VII. The Shape of Late-Practice Death Work

What death practice looks like as it actually unfolds across decades, drawing on the lineage streams and the contemporary contemplative apparatus together.

**The daily practice deepens.** The candle, the grimoire, the daily card pull — practices the reader has been doing for years — become explicitly death-engaged in late practice. The candle held with awareness of what it is doing in the body — the small thermal-attentional field that the dying body responds to. The grimoire entries holding the body's changes as data, not failure. The card pull holding the question of what is being prepared for, not just the question of the day.

**The wake practice for others.** As the practitioner ages, more people in her life die. The practitioner becomes, by virtue of long practice, the person who can hold space at deathbeds — for parents, for siblings, for friends, for community members. The Welsh *gwylnos* and the Gullah Geechee sit-up are both apparatus for this work; the Ostaseski-Halifax bedside-vigil training is the contemporary supplement. The practitioner becomes, slowly, a kind of priest of dying without ceremonial appointment.

**The will-and-instructions practice.** The practical work of preparing one's own death — the legal documents, the practical instructions, the funeral and memorial intentions, the ancestral altar provisions, the disposition of the grimoire and the practitioner's accumulated working tools. This is sustained over years, revised, returned to. The will-and-instructions document becomes part of the grimoire's late-practice apparatus.

**The body's awareness practice.** The practitioner's own body, in late practice, begins teaching her about dying. The accumulated minor illnesses, the slowdowns, the new aches, the new limits. The body practice (drawn from Levine, Ogden, Foor's regulation work, the contemplative practitioners) becomes attention to what the body is communicating about its trajectory toward dying. This is not morbid; it is intimate. The body becomes, late in practice, the primary teacher of death.

**The conversation with the ancestors specifically about what is coming.** The Foor-trained ancestral practice in late practice opens explicitly to the conversation about the practitioner's own coming death. The well-resourced ancestors who have already crossed are available as teachers and companions for the work of preparing. This is its own deep practice that the contemporary contemplative-death literature does not directly engage; it requires the lineage-tradition apparatus.

**The transmission practice.** Late in practice, the question of whether and how to teach others arises. The keyed reader's accumulated grimoires, sigil archive, tarot working library, reading log, ancestral records — these are inheritance the practitioner can transmit to others. Not as workshops, not as certifications, but as being kept-aside as a resource for specific other practitioners who eventually find their way to her. The transmission is part of dying practice because it is the practitioner's preparation of what continues after her.

**The acceptance work.** Across decades the practice produces, slowly, a kind of acceptance of death that the early years could not have produced and that the practitioner cannot reach by deciding to. It is the cumulative effect of the daily practice, the wake practice for others, the body's teaching, the ancestral conversation, the transmission work. The acceptance is not resignation; it is the practitioner's settled relationship with her own mortality after years of active engagement with it.

**The final practice.** The dying itself, when it comes, is the practitioner's last working. The practitioner has been preparing the apparatus across decades; the apparatus does its work. The Welsh stream contributes the *anadl*-attention to the dying breath; the West African Diaspora stream contributes the community presence in the sit-up; the Continental Hermetic-Masonic stream contributes the alchemical death-and-rebirth structure as the framework the practitioner is working within; the contemporary contemplative apparatus contributes the moment-by-moment attention to the dying process. The death itself is the working's culmination, not its catastrophe.

## VIII. What Remains Unaddressed

Several research dimensions remain open within death practice across the three streams:

- **Specific Welsh-tradition contemporary death-practice teachers.** Hughes (Anglesey Druid Order) and Telyndru (Sisterhood of Avalon) have written substantially on Welsh tradition broadly but less specifically on death practice as a developed discipline. Whether contemporary Welsh-tradition death-doulas, contemporary Welsh-tradition deathbed practitioners exist as identifiable figures was not surfaced. Direct outreach to Anglesey Druid Order or Sisterhood of Avalon for current death-practice resources is the next step.
- **Specific Gullah Geechee contemporary death-practice teachers and burial societies still in active operation.** The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor Commission and Penn Center are the cultural-historical institutions; the spiritual-practitioner-specific contemporary lineage is community-based and not as accessible through web research. Direct community engagement is the substantive answer.
- **Whether Co-Masonic and Continental Hermetic-Masonic bodies have developed contemporary death-practice apparatus** beyond the historical third-degree raising. The traditional rituals are in place; whether contemporary practitioners have developed sustained late-practice death work specifically is unclear. Le Droit Humain American Federation contact would surface this.
- **The integration question for mixed-inheritance practitioners specifically.** Published literature does not engage substantively with practitioners whose inheritance carries three distinct death-practice traditions. The chapter writing may participate in developing this rather than citing it.
- **The bodily-medical realities of dying for the keyed reader's specific configuration.** Beyond the contemplative apparatus, there is the actual medical-bodily-decisional work of late life — advance directives, palliative care vs. hospice decisions, MAID (Medical Aid in Dying) where legal, Black American medical-system inequities specifically, the particular configurations of how late life unfolds for women of color in the contemporary American medical system. These are practical research dimensions Ch 6 may engage rather than the document handling here.
- **Death as community practice in contemporary diaspora contexts.** The Welsh diaspora, the Black diaspora, the New Orleans community — for a practitioner not living in the immediate community where the lineage is rooted, what does community death practice look like? This is real and the answer is partly improvisational.

## IX. Notes for Chapter 6 Writing

**Death practice is not a Ch 6 add-on; it is the chapter's spine.** The chapter is short (estimated 2,500-3,500 words; possibly extending to 4,500 if death practice is given full development). Death practice as developed here is the central material; the related Ch 6 territories (the long view of practice, teaching others, the closing return to Ch 1 / v10 § I orientation) sit around it.

**The three streams contribute distinct material; the chapter prose should keep them distinct rather than blending them.** The Welsh stream has *gwylnos*, elegy, sin-eater, cross-way procession, *anadl*. The West African Diaspora stream has sit-up, body-washing, bottle trees, burial society, second-line, sustained ancestor relationship. The Continental Hermetic-Masonic stream has alchemical death-rebirth, third-degree raising, Reintegration. Each stream's specifics matter; flattening them into a generic "Celtic-African-Hermetic" register would lose what makes each one substantive.

**The contemporary contemplative apparatus is supplementary, not primary, for the keyed reader.** Jenkinson, Ostaseski, Halifax, Tisdale provide substrate for the daily practice; the lineage-tradition specifics are what the practitioner inherits. **The specific configuration matters at the very end.** The keyed reader's death is not a generic death.
