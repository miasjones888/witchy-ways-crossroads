§ I

Position — before anything

The early years of a serious magical and devotional practice are about exploration across traditions and the building of practitioner foundations — not about choosing a lineage. Lineage commitment, if it ever comes, is the work of years three, five, ten. This document is structured for the wide entry.

What this document is not: a curriculum in any one tradition. It is not a book of Hekatean devotion, though Hekate is treated. It is not a manual of traditional witchcraft, though that lineage is surveyed. It is not a guide to ceremonial magic or to Wicca or to chaos magic, though all three are mapped. It is a wider entry — a map of the territory of Western magical and devotional practice, with the foundational practices a serious practitioner builds across the early years regardless of which lineage, if any, eventually claims them.

The discipline of the early years has a shape. You read broadly across traditions, not deeply within one. You build the foundational practices — attention, observation, reading, the grimoire, divination, somatic ground — that any serious practice in any lineage requires. You notice what calls and what does not, without rushing to commitment. You let the question of what you actually believe develop alongside the practices, rather than trying to resolve it in advance. After enough years, if it happens, a lineage emerges as the one your practice has actually been growing toward — recognized rather than chosen. Until then, the work is broad.

The traditions surveyed in § XVI are real, documented streams with primary sources, scholarly literature, and contemporary practitioner communities. They sit in a different register from the popular witchcraft visible across contemporary social media, which is largely a contemporary self-help genre dressed in witchcraft vocabulary. The distinction matters because what you read and engage in the early years shapes the practitioner you become. The serious literature is not harder to access than the popular; it just requires knowing it exists.

This document presumes you will read alongside it. The reading lists in each section are not optional — they are the apparatus the practice rests on. Begin with §§ II–VI — the grimoire, altar work, ritual structure, the wheel of the year, and the practice rhythm — which are the foundational instrument, literacy, and scaffolding everything else fills. The inner-foundation practices in §§ VII–XV (body, belief, soul-memory, historical witch, feminine divine, animism, lunar, sigil, tarot) are what the practitioner builds across years before any survey of traditions makes sense as more than reading. The survey of traditions itself, ancestral work, the question of lineage commitment, and the eventual finding of teachers and community (§§ XVI–XIX) sit later — for orientation when the practitioner foundations have been built.

One further orientation worth naming directly. The canonical scholarship on Western magical and devotional traditions has been produced overwhelmingly by men, and the contemporary practitioner literature is similarly male-weighted in what gets cited and republished. This is not because women have not been doing the work — they have, continuously and substantially, particularly across the second-wave feminist goddess movement, the women-of-color spiritual lineages, the contemporary recovery of pre-patriarchal religious history, and the lived devotional traditions that have always been carried disproportionately by women. It is because the citation patterns of the field have systematically underweighted women's contributions. This document attempts to correct for that across its reading lists. Foundational women writers — Starhawk, Margot Adler, Sharon Blackie, Charlene Spretnak, Carol P. Christ, Sylvia Brinton Perera, Christine Downing, Rachel Pollack, Clarissa Pinkola Estés, Audre Lorde, adrienne maree brown, Robin Wall Kimmerer, Sophie Strand — are placed where they belong, which is at the foundations rather than at the margins.

Your reading log — the spine of everything

Before any practice, begin a reading log. A physical notebook or a single dedicated digital document. Every text you read in this stream gets logged: title, author, dates read, the three or four ideas that struck you, the questions it raised, the further texts it sent you to. This is the apparatus that turns reading into a developing scholarship.

The reading log will become, over years, the most useful single resource you build. It is the index of your own developing thought, and it will be the record that shows you, eventually, which tradition your practice has actually been growing toward. Begin it before anything else.

§ II

The grimoire — your central instrument

Before the reading widens and the traditions arrive, set up the instrument that will hold the work. The grimoire is the single most important resource you build, and it should exist before you read another page of this document. Every section that follows will fill it.

The grimoire is not a journal in any conventional sense. It is the working notebook of a developing practice — the place where daily card pulls accumulate, where dreams and signs are recorded, where workings are documented, where the made things are pressed in, where the correspondences you build for yourself are kept, where translations and fragments of primary sources you encounter are copied by hand, where reading notes and questions live, where the names of the dead and the ancestors are kept, where every relationship the practice opens with eventually gets its own page or section. Without it, the practice never accumulates. With it, even sparse and interrupted years of practice produce, eventually, a substantial document of an actual life of attention.

The historical literature on grimoires is itself a significant scholarly territory. The contemporary practitioner's grimoire descends from the medieval and early modern grimoire tradition — the Key of Solomon, the Picatrix, the Heptameron, the various ceremonial books — but adapted for personal rather than transmitted use. It is your developing book of practice, reference, and record, and over years it becomes a working instrument that no other document in your life resembles.

Physical, not digital. The act of writing by hand is itself part of the work; it slows you, it commits you, it leaves a trace that cannot be search-indexed or scrolled past. The aesthetic of the book matters — it should be a pleasure to open. Buy a notebook you genuinely like. The grimoire becomes, over years, an art object as much as a working text. Many practitioners find that they end up with a series of grimoires across decades — Volume I, Volume II, and so on — as the practice fills and outgrows individual books.

Build

The grimoire — begin this week

Before reading further into this document, do this:

  • Buy one physical notebook you find genuinely beautiful. Hardcover, sewn binding ideally. Size and ruling are personal — most practitioners eventually settle on something between A5 and B5, blank or dot-grid rather than lined.
  • Inscribe the first page with your name, the date you began, and (if you want) a short statement of intent — a dedication, a question you are entering with, a phrase that holds why you are beginning. This is your own personal opening rite for the book.
  • Reserve named sections from the start, even if some stay sparse for a long time. The accumulated registers across years of practice typically include: card pulls (daily and weekly, dated, with brief response), dreams and signs (anything that arrives sideways), working records (the date, moon phase, what was done, what arrived after), made things (sigils, sketches, pressed leaves, photographs printed small), correspondences (your own developed tables of plants, stones, days, deities, colors, hours, built up from sources over time), translations and fragments (copied by hand from primary sources that struck you), reading notes (separate from the reading log, the passages you wanted to keep, the questions they raised), ancestral material (names, dates, what was carried, what arrived in the work), devotional records (for any deity or figure you eventually come into relationship with).
  • Begin the first entry today. Even if it's only the date and a single sentence — I am beginning. The grimoire becomes real the moment it has its first entry.

The grimoire is the long arc. Begin small. Add as the practice asks. After five years it will be substantial; after ten, foundational. There is no rushing this and no falling behind.

§ III

Altar work — the locus of practice

An altar is the dedicated physical space where practice happens. It is not decoration and not aesthetic prop — it is the working surface that holds the relationships, materials, and acts of a developing practice. Before any rite, the altar is the where.

The altar in contemporary witchcraft and pagan practice descends from a long lineage of dedicated religious surfaces — the household shrines of Roman lararium tradition, the medieval Christian oratory, the indigenous and Diasporic ancestor altars of innumerable cultures, the ceremonial-magic altar of the Western Hermetic tradition, the seasonal altar of agricultural folk-religious practice. The contemporary witchcraft altar draws from many of these lineages without being any one of them. What is consistent across the lineages is the basic principle: a particular surface is set aside for sacred work, kept as the locus of a particular kind of attention, and developed over time into the place where the practice's relationships are tended.

What an altar is not: it is not an installation that has to be perfect before practice can begin. It is not a performance for others to see. It is not, primarily, an aesthetic object — though developed altars do have aesthetic coherence. It is not static; altars change as the practice changes. And it is not something requiring expensive or hard-to-find objects — most of the working altars of serious practitioners contain mostly objects of personal weight (found, gifted, inherited, made) rather than purchased ritual gear.

The basic working altar

The minimum working altar contains a few elements: a dedicated surface (a shelf, a small table, the top of a dresser), a candle, a vessel for water or offerings, and at least one object of personal weight. This is enough to begin and remains the core of the practice across years. More elaborate altars add elements — but elaboration is not maturity, and many of the most experienced practitioners maintain working altars that are striking in their simplicity.

The traditional elements that often appear on a witchcraft altar, drawn loosely from both Wiccan and traditional-craft conventions, include representations of the four classical elements (often: a candle for fire, incense or a feather for air, a bowl of water for water, a stone or salt for earth), one or two larger candles, a divination tool (usually a tarot deck), and any objects sacred to the practitioner's developing relationships (deity statues or images if any deity work is happening, ancestral photographs or named papers, found objects from the land, made objects from your own working). Different traditions will arrange these differently — Wiccan altars typically have specific positional conventions (the elements at the cardinal directions, the God and Goddess at center), traditional-craft altars are more variable, eclectic altars develop their own logic.

The siting of the altar matters more than is often noticed. A working altar should be in a place that is not heavily trafficked, not exposed to constant disruption, and ideally has some directional or environmental significance to the practitioner (facing east for sunrise, facing north for the chthonic, facing the windowed direction of most light, near a particular wall). It should be a place where you can sit comfortably for the duration of a working without interruption. If you live in shared space, the altar can be small and discreet — it does not need to be visible to others to be operative.

The kinds of altars a practice eventually has

Across years of practice, most practitioners end up with more than one altar. The configuration that often emerges:

The working altar — the primary altar of the daily and weekly practice. Where the daily candle is lit, the weekly card pull happens, the seasonal observances are marked. The center of the practice.

The ancestral altar — distinct from the working altar, dedicated specifically to the dead and to ancestral relationships. Photographs if you have them, named papers if you don't, an offering vessel kept fresh, a candle reserved for ancestral work alone. Typically sited differently than the working altar — many traditions hold that the ancestral altar should not be in the bedroom, should not face certain directions, and should be on a lower or separate surface from the working altar. The contemporary practitioner literature on this varies; Daniel Foor's Ancestral Medicine and Mallorie Vaudoise's Honoring Your Ancestors both treat the ancestral altar specifically and at length.

Deity altars — if and when relationship with a specific deity develops. Often distinct from the working altar, dedicated to one figure or a small constellation of figures. The conventions vary by tradition; the Hellenic devotional tradition has specific altar conventions, the Welsh Druidic tradition has others, the Yoruba Diasporic traditions have substantial and specific altar requirements that are part of what makes those traditions initiation-required.

The seasonal altar — sometimes maintained as a separate surface (often the working altar adapted seasonally, sometimes a distinct space), changes with the wheel of the year. The seasonal altar is where the eight-station observance becomes visible in the home: the symbols, colors, and offerings appropriate to each station are placed there and changed at each turning.

Beginners often try to set up multiple altars at once. The practitioner literature is consistent that one is enough at the start, and additional altars develop as the relationships they are for develop. Build the working altar first; let the others arrive as the practice asks for them.

Maintaining an altar

An altar requires maintenance. The candle is lit and extinguished; the water is changed regularly (stale water on an altar is generally considered to dishonor what is being addressed); the offerings are refreshed and the old ones disposed of appropriately (typically returned to the earth — buried, composted, poured onto a plant). Dust accumulates and is cleared with intention rather than absentmindedly. The altar is reset periodically — typically at the major seasonal turnings, at the dark moon, or whenever the configuration no longer feels alive.

The altar is also occasionally disassembled entirely and rebuilt — a major life transition, a substantial deepening of the practice, a year-end reset. This is itself a practice; the act of taking the altar apart, cleaning each object, sitting with what each represents, and rebuilding deliberately is a form of rite that develops over years.

The working altar — begin this week

  • Choose a surface. A shelf, a windowsill, a small table, the top of a dresser. It should be a place where you can sit or stand undisturbed, not heavily trafficked, ideally with some environmental significance to you.

§ IV

Ritual structure — what ritual is and how it works

Ritual is the structured action that the practice is built around. Before practicing rites, understand what ritual actually is, what it does, and how the basic structures across traditions function. The literacy comes before the doing.

Ritual in the broadest sense is patterned action performed with intention and attention, marking a particular kind of time and space as different from ordinary time and space. The anthropologist Catherine Bell, whose Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice (Oxford, 1992) is the standard contemporary scholarly treatment, defines ritual as the practice of differentiation — the act of making certain time, certain space, certain action, and certain attention distinct from the everyday flow. Ronald Grimes (The Craft of Ritual Studies, Oxford, 2014) extends this with attention to ritual as a craft that can be learned, refined, and practiced with skill.

What ritual does, operatively: it produces shifts in the practitioner that are difficult to produce by other means. The contemporary literature on ritual converges on several mechanisms — altered attention (the focused concentration of ritual differs from ordinary diffuse attention), embodied action (the physical performance of patterned movement engages the nervous system in ways that thought alone does not), structured time (the marked beginning, middle, and end of ritual creates a contained psychological space), and symbolic density (the layered meaning of ritual objects and actions communicates what direct statement cannot). Whether you take the operative effects of ritual as psychological-only, as theurgic-real, or as something between (the question § VIII develops), the practical fact is that ritual produces effects that cannot be produced by reading or thinking.

The basic ritual structure

Across most Western magical and devotional traditions, ritual has a recognizable three-part structure: an opening that marks the transition from ordinary to ritual time and space, a central work that is the actual purpose of the rite, and a closing that returns the practitioner to ordinary time and space.

The opening typically involves: physical preparation (cleansing, often with water; lighting candles; ritual dress if any), spatial demarcation (some form of casting circle, naming the directions, marking the working space as set apart), invocation or address (calling upon whatever is being addressed in the rite — deity, ancestors, spirits, the elements, your own deeper self), and the establishment of intent (often spoken aloud, sometimes written, sometimes simply held as focused attention).

The central work is the actual content of the rite — devotional address, divinatory operation, sigil charging, ancestor communication, seasonal observance, magical working, sustained presence. The shape of the central work varies entirely by what the rite is for. A devotional rite has a different shape than an operative magical working has a different shape than a divinatory consultation. The opening and closing structures are similar across rite types; the central work is what differs.

The closing typically involves: thanks given to whatever was addressed, release of the powers or presences invoked, dissolution of the spatial demarcation, grounding (the practitioner deliberately returning to ordinary embodied awareness — eating, touching the floor, moving), and the closing of the ritual time. Closings are routinely undervalued by beginners; the contemporary practitioner literature is consistent that ritual without proper closing leaves the practitioner ungrounded, the space unclosed, and the energies unbalanced. The closing matters as much as the opening.

The two main registers

Western contemporary practice contains two broad registers of ritual style, which it is worth knowing about as you develop your own approach.

The ceremonial register descends from the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and the broader Western ceremonial-magic tradition. Characterized by formal structure, specific scripted invocations (the Lesser Banishing Ritual of the Pentagram, the Middle Pillar exercise, the various pentagram and hexagram rituals), elaborate symbolic apparatus (Kabbalistic correspondences, planetary and elemental attributions, ceremonial implements), and a focus on precision in performance. Israel Regardie's The Golden Dawn (1937) is the foundational sourcebook; modern accessible introductions include Donald Michael Kraig's Modern Magick (1988/2010) and John Michael Greer's Circles of Power (1997). This register requires substantial commitment to learning the formal apparatus before becoming effective.

The direct or witchcraft register is more variable, less scripted, and more direct. Traditional witchcraft, much of contemporary Wicca after the formal-Gardnerian period, and most eclectic practice operate in this register. Rites are still structured, but the structure emerges from the work being done rather than from a formal apparatus. Less elaborate, more relational, more direct. Most contemporary witchcraft writing operates in this register; Starhawk's The Spiral Dance, the various practitioner-texts of the traditional craft, and most of what beginners encounter is in this style.

Most serious practitioners eventually develop a personal style that draws from both registers — the precision and structural literacy of the ceremonial tradition combined with the directness and adaptability of the witchcraft tradition. Neither register is more advanced than the other; they are different operating modes appropriate to different work.

The role of altered states

Most ritual produces some degree of altered consciousness — focused attention, mild trance, the suspension of ordinary cognitive chatter, occasionally deeper trance states. This is a feature of how ritual works, not a side effect. The contemporary literature on this — particularly Felicitas Goodman's Where the Spirits Ride the Wind (Indiana, 1990) and the broader literature on ritual trance — treats the altered state as integral to ritual efficacy.

The depth of altered state appropriate to a rite varies. Devotional rites typically involve mild focused attention; divinatory work involves a particular receptive state; operative magical working often involves more deliberate trance induction; certain visionary or oracular practices involve deep trance states that should not be attempted without preparation and (often) without a trusted presence. The trauma-informed considerations in § VII apply particularly here — for a practitioner with substantial trauma history, deep trance work without practitioner support carries real risk and is appropriately approached only with care.

Practice

The basic three-part rite — a working template

Per ritual · 20–60 minutes depending on central work

A simple template for ritual that scales from brief to elaborate, drawn from the direct/witchcraft register. Use as starting structure; adapt as practice develops.

Opening (3–5 minutes): Approach the altar. Light the candle. Take three slow breaths. Touch the floor or earth, then the heart. Speak aloud what the rite is for ("Tonight I am...", "I come to..."). The space is now ritual space.

Central work (variable): Whatever the rite is for. Devotional address, divinatory pull, sigil charging, sustained presence, written or spoken work, made offering. The shape of this varies by purpose; the opening and closing remain consistent.

Closing (3–5 minutes): Speak aloud what the rite has been ("This is done", "I am returning"). Take three slow breaths. Touch the floor or earth, then the heart. Extinguish the candle. Eat or drink something to ground. The space returns to ordinary space.

This template can hold a five-minute daily rite or a two-hour seasonal observance. The structure is the same; what fills the central work changes.

Your developing ritual library — a section in the grimoire

Across years of practice, develop a section in the grimoire dedicated to your own working rituals. What it accumulates:

§ V

The wheel of the year — the seasonal calendar of practice

The wheel of the year is the eight-station agricultural-solar calendar that contemporary paganism and witchcraft use to mark the year. Knowing what the stations are, where they come from historically, and what observance can look like at each is foundational to entering the seasonal-ritual life that runs underneath the rest of the practice.

An honest historical preamble: the wheel as commonly presented — the eight-fold cycle with the duotheistic Wiccan myth-narrative of the dying-and-reborn Sun God and the Goddess in her changing aspects — is largely a twentieth-century synthesis. It was assembled in the 1950s primarily by Ross Nichols (founder of the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids) and Gerald Gardner (the principal architect of modern Wicca), drawing together Celtic cross-quarter days that have older roots, Germanic and Norse solar quarter days that have older roots, agricultural folk traditions, and a constructed mythological narrative. The synthesis is not ancient; the components are partly ancient. Ronald Hutton's The Stations of the Sun: A History of the Ritual Year in Britain (Oxford, 1996) is the definitive scholarly history. Knowing this matters because the wheel as you encounter it in most contemporary witchcraft writing is presented as ancient continuous tradition, and it is not — it is a modern synthesis using older materials. This does not diminish its usefulness as contemporary religious framework; it does mean engaging with it honestly.

The eight stations

The four cross-quarter days are the older Celtic stations, marking roughly the midpoints between the solar quarter days. They are the agricultural-pastoral festivals of the pre-Christian Celtic world, with their roots traceable to the medieval Irish texts and earlier:

Samhain (October 31 / November 1) — the Celtic new year, the festival of the dead, the night when the veil between worlds is thinnest. The most theologically substantial of the cross-quarters in contemporary practice, particularly for chthonic and ancestral work. Traditional themes: death, the ancestors, divination, the recognition of what has passed, the entry into the dark half of the year. This is the pagan Halloween, but in serious practice it is substantially deeper than the popular treatment.

Imbolc (February 1 / 2) — the first stirring of spring, the festival of Brigid in the Irish tradition, the lengthening of light beginning to be perceptible. Themes: tentative new beginnings, purification, the dedication of the year ahead, the return of light, the lambing season in the agricultural calendar. Often observed with candles.

Beltane (May 1) — the great fire festival, the beginning of summer, the festival of fertility and the union of the masculine and feminine principles in the Wiccan duotheistic frame. Traditional themes: vitality, sexuality, the height of the light half of the year, the May Queen and May King, the bonfire. Counterpart to Samhain across the wheel.

Lughnasadh / Lammas (August 1) — the first harvest festival, named for the Irish god Lugh in the Celtic tradition or for the loaf-mass in the Anglo-Saxon Christian continuation. Themes: the beginning of harvest, the first cutting of the grain, the recognition of what is being reaped (literally and metaphorically), the early beginning of the descent into the dark half of the year.

The four quarter days are the solar stations — the solstices and equinoxes — drawn into the wheel from Germanic and Norse sources by the twentieth-century synthesizers. They are astronomically precise and shift slightly year to year:

Yule / Winter Solstice (December 20–23) — the longest night, the rebirth of the sun, the deepest moment of the dark half of the year. Themes: rebirth in darkness, the return of light from the deepest absence, the inner-turning of the year, the holding-vigil through the longest night. Theologically substantial; the contemporary Christmas tradition descends substantially from older solstice observance.

Ostara / Spring Equinox (March 19–22) — the balance of light and dark with light increasing, the beginning of true spring. Themes: balance, growth, fertility (the contemporary Easter eggs and rabbits descend from older spring-equinox fertility symbols), new life. Less theologically developed in most contemporary practice than the cross-quarters.

Litha / Summer Solstice (June 19–22) — the longest day, the height of the sun, the deepest moment of the light half of the year. Themes: fullness, abundance, the sun at its peak, the recognition that from this moment the light begins to decrease. Counterpart to Yule across the wheel.

Mabon / Autumn Equinox (September 19–23) — the balance of light and dark with darkness increasing, the second harvest festival, the entry into autumn proper. Themes: balance, gratitude, the harvest, the preparation for the dark half of the year.

Two framings of the wheel

The Wiccan duotheistic framing reads the wheel as the cyclical mythological narrative of the Goddess (in her Maiden, Mother, and Crone aspects through the year) and the God (in his birth at Yule, growth through spring, peak at Litha, sacrifice at Lammas, descent at Samhain, rebirth at Yule). The wheel is the year-long mystery cycle of the divine. This frame is theologically substantive and provides a coherent narrative arc to the year; many practitioners find it deeply meaningful and operate within it.

The agricultural-seasonal framing reads the wheel as the marking of the actual seasonal-agricultural-light shifts, without the specific Wiccan mythological narrative. The cross-quarters are the agricultural-pastoral festivals that mark planting, harvest, breeding, slaughter; the solar quarters mark the actual astronomical turnings of light. Practitioners working in this frame observe each station for what it is in the actual seasonal cycle of the place they live, with whatever local-specific observance is appropriate.

Most practitioners draw from both frames as feels alive. The wheel as personal-mythological-narrative is one register; the wheel as actual seasonal attention is another; the two can coexist and inform each other. There is no requirement to commit to one or the other.

Adapting the wheel to your geography

The wheel as it is conventionally presented assumes a temperate Northern Hemisphere climate similar to Britain or northern Europe. For a practitioner living elsewhere — and you live in San Diego, where the seasonal cycle is substantially different — the wheel as written does not match the actual seasons of your land. Some serious practitioners adapt the wheel to their actual climate (the Southern Hemisphere wheel inverts the dates entirely; desert and tropical wheels reorganize around local seasonal markers like wet/dry seasons rather than warm/cold; coastal Mediterranean climates like San Diego's have their own substantially different seasonal arc). The contemporary practitioner literature on this is developing — Sara Cleto and Brittany Warman's work, the broader land-based-paganism conversation, and writers like Lupa Greenwolf (The Tarot of Bones; Plant and Fungus Totems) treat the question of bioregional wheel-adaptation seriously. Worth investigating as the practice develops; worth knowing now that the wheel as given is not the only possibility.

Practice

The seasonal observance — scaled by capacity

Eight times yearly · ~ 30 minutes minimum, longer as practice develops

The minimum serious observance of each station, suitable for the early years: at the altar, on the day of the station (or the nearest weekend evening for practical reasons), light a candle of seasonally-appropriate color (orange/black for Samhain, white for Imbolc, red/green for Beltane, yellow/gold for Lammas, red/green/silver for Yule, pastels for Ostara, red/orange for Litha, brown/orange for Mabon — these are approximations; develop your own associations). Sit at the altar. Speak aloud what the station is — what is being marked. Pull three cards on the question "what is this turning asking of me." Record in the grimoire. Sit for as long as feels right. Close the rite.

Add observance elements as the practice develops: a particular meal cooked, a particular walk taken, a particular offering made, a longer rite worked, eventually full seasonal rituals constructed for each station. Begin with the minimum. Build over years.

§ VI

A practice rhythm — integrating the foundations

With the grimoire established (§ II), the altar built (§ III), ritual structure understood (§ IV), and the wheel of the year mapped (§ V), the practice rhythm integrates them into the daily, weekly, monthly, and seasonal scaffolding of an actual life of practice. What follows is the foundational rhythm. Begin with the smallest tier you will actually do.

The rhythm is scaled by capacity, not by ambition. None of this requires you to be well; all of it absorbs interruption without breaking. The discipline is consistency at whatever tier is sustainable, not heroism at a tier that collapses. Most practitioners spend the first year operating at the daily tier alone, and the practice still develops. Some spend years there, and the practice still develops. The depth comes from the return, not the volume.

Practice — Threshold

The first rite — done once

One sitting · 5–10 minutes

The threshold rite is the inaugural ritual that opens the practice. Performed once, at the working altar (built per § III), using the basic three-part rite structure (per § IV).

Approach the altar. Light the candle. Take three slow breaths. Touch the floor, then the heart. Speak aloud: this is the beginning of my practice. Sit for the length of ten breaths. Speak aloud: this is begun. Touch the floor, then the heart. Extinguish the candle. Write a single sentence in the grimoire: the date, and the act of having begun.

The locus is established. The threshold has been crossed. Everything else assumes this rite has been performed.

Practice — Daily

The minimum daily contact

Daily · ~ 60–120 seconds

Approach the altar. Light the candle. Take one breath. Pull one tarot card, record it in the grimoire by name and date with one sentence of response — what it brought up, not formal interpretation. Take one breath. Extinguish.

This is the baseline of the practice. Done consistently across a year, it produces a substantial divinatory record. Done across five years, it becomes the basis of your personal relationship with each card and your own developed sensibility for how the cards speak. Begin with this and add nothing else for as long as you need.

Practice — Weekly

The longer working

Weekly · 15–30 minutes

Once a week, perform a more substantial rite using the three-part structure (per § IV). One three-card divinatory pull on the week, recorded with brief interpretation, reviewed at week's end (the review is where divinatory accuracy actually builds). One made-thing offering placed on the altar — a sigil, a sketch, a poem, a found object, anything made on purpose. One outdoor walk without device, three observed elements recorded on return.

Practice — Lunar

The two poles

Twice monthly · 30–60 minutes each

Dark moon and full moon — twenty-four marked nights per year, the minimum lunar discipline. At each, a fuller ritual at the altar using the three-part structure.

Entry-level shape: at the dark moon, sit at the altar for longer than usual, write a letter to whatever you are addressing (a deity, the dead, a part of yourself), burn it or keep it. At the full moon, sit at the altar, pull three cards, ask one question, write what comes. Charge water, stones, or tools by leaving them in the moonlight overnight. § XIII (Lunar architecture) develops these substantially.

Practice — Seasonal

The eight stations

Eight times yearly · varies

The eight stations of the wheel of the year (per § V) — Samhain, Yule, Imbolc, Ostara, Beltane, Litha, Lammas, Mabon. Acknowledged minimally at first per the entry-level practice in § V, observed more substantively as developed practice grows. Mark them in the calendar a year in advance.

Practice — Re-entry

The protocol for return

Whenever needed

The practice will be interrupted, repeatedly, by the conditions of an embodied life. The protocol for return is identical to the threshold rite: approach the altar, light the candle, ten breaths, extinguish. Write the date and the word back in the grimoire. The practice resumes from there. There is no streak to break, no progress to lose, no apology owed.

The practitioner you become in ten years is built by the daily candle, the weekly card pull, the monthly lunar working, the eight seasonal observances, the slowly-accumulating grimoire, and the steadily-deepening reading. None of it is dramatic. All of it is the work. Whatever lineage your practice eventually grows toward, it grows from this foundational rhythm. The reading and exploration that follow in this document are what fills the practice with substance; the rhythm is what holds it.
§ VII

The body as ground — trauma-informed practice

A serious witchcraft practice and a serious relationship with the trauma-affected nervous system are not separate disciplines. The contemporary clinical literature has converged with the ritual literature in productive ways, and the practitioner working with both has substantial clinical material to draw on.

Two principles govern this section. The first: there is a difference between practices that regulate the nervous system and practices that open traumatic material. Regulation can be done alone; opening generally cannot, and should not be, regardless of how aesthetically continuous it would be with the rest of a practice. The second: the principle of titration — the discipline of brief contact with difficult material alternated with deliberate return to safety — is the methodological backbone of contemporary trauma work and applies fully to ritual practice.

Practice

The somatic anchors — layered into daily working

Daily · ~ 60–90 seconds each, layered into existing practice

Three regulation moves to layer underneath any ritual practice. Orienting: turn your head, let your eyes find five things in the room, name them silently. The long exhale: inhale through the nose four counts, exhale through the mouth eight, six rounds. Hand on heart: one hand on chest, one on belly, breathe into the lower hand for two minutes. Each takes seconds; together they regulate the nervous system enough to make ritual work safer.

Use orienting and the long exhale as the entry to any ritual. Use hand on heart whenever overwhelm arrives.

Practice

The pendulation principle — governing all opening work

Whenever working with difficult material · ongoing

From Levine's Somatic Experiencing. When you notice difficult sensation or affect, do not stay with it. Briefly contact it, then deliberately move attention to a place that feels neutral or okay. Then return briefly. Then leave again. Pendulate. This is the methodological backbone of contemporary trauma work and applies fully to ritual practice that opens difficult material.

Practice

Grief tending — the Weller-derived rite

Quarterly or as needed · ~ 30 minutes

Set the altar with water in a clear bowl, salt in a small dish, a candle. Sit. Light. Name what you are grieving — aloud or in writing. Let grief move if it wants; do not perform tears that aren't there. After however long you stay, dissolve a pinch of salt in the water. Pour onto the earth (a plant, the garden). The salt-water carries the grief out. Read Weller for the framework.

§ VIII

On belief — what to do with the metaphysics

If you are entering this for something to believe in, the question of what you actually believe will arrive. This section is for that arrival. It does not resolve the metaphysics. It maps the positions a serious practitioner can hold, names the writers who develop each, and frames engagement with the question as part of the practice rather than something to settle in advance.

The honest position: most serious witchcraft and Hekatean practitioners drift between several metaphysical positions over the arc of a practice. The drift is not failure of belief; it is the practice working on the believer. The deities and entities engaged through ritual become more real over time, in a sense that does not map neatly onto the literal-versus-metaphorical distinction the modern Western mind defaults to. The position you hold at the start of the practice is rarely the position you hold at the end. The position you hold in any given week may be different from the one you held the week before. What stays consistent is the practice itself — the candle lit, the offering made, the deity addressed, the dead named. The metaphysics develop alongside.

What follows is a map of three positions a thoughtful practitioner can hold, with the writers and traditions that develop each. None is correct. All are coherent. You will likely find yourself moving between them as the practice deepens.

Position one — literal devotional polytheism

The deities are real, ontologically distinct persons with their own agency, intelligence, and existence independent of human belief in them. Hekate is a real entity. The dead are real, continuing persons. The chthonic powers are real. The relationship between the practitioner and these realities is the same kind of relationship as the relationship between two living humans: real, mutual, ethically substantive, capable of being damaged by neglect or strengthened by attention.

This is the position held by most historical practitioners across most cultures and most periods, and it is the position held by a substantial portion of contemporary serious devotional polytheists. The principal contemporary philosophical defender of this position is Edward Butler, whose work on Platonic polytheism is the most rigorous contemporary defense of the position that the gods are real, distinct, ontologically primary entities. His Essays on a Polytheistic Philosophy of Religion (Phaidra Editions, 2012) is the foundational text; his ongoing work at henadology.wordpress.com continues the project. The broader contemporary devotional polytheist movement — writers like Galina Krasskova, John Beckett, Morpheus Ravenna — works in this register. From within this position, the question "but do you really believe Hekate is real?" is a category error; the question is whether you have entered the relationship.

Position two — theurgic-Platonic

The deities are real but in a more philosophically nuanced sense — as the highest principles of reality accessible through ritual engagement, ontologically real but not reducible to the kind of personal-agential entity the literal position holds. This is the position of late Platonic theurgy — Iamblichus, Proclus, Damascius — and of the contemporary Platonic theology that has developed from their work. The deities are real, but their reality is metaphysically prior to and richer than the personal-agential model can capture. They are accessed through ritual not because ritual flatters them but because ritual is the appropriate epistemic mode for that order of reality. Apophatic uncertainty about precise metaphysical status is held alongside genuine devotional engagement.

The principal contemporary defender is Algis Uždavinys, whose Philosophy as a Rite of Rebirth (Prometheus Trust, 2008) and Philosophy and Theurgy in Late Antiquity (Sophia Perennis, 2010) reconstruct the late Platonic theurgic position with substantial rigor. Gregory Shaw's Theurgy and the Soul: The Neoplatonism of Iamblichus (Penn State, 1995) is the standard scholarly treatment. For the broader Platonic philosophical context, Pierre Hadot's What Is Ancient Philosophy? (Harvard, 2002) is essential. Sallustius's brief late-antique treatise On the Gods and the World (4th century CE; Thomas Taylor's translation is the most-used) is the shortest and most accessible primary source for the position.

Position three — psycho-magical / depth-archetypal

The deities and entities engaged through ritual are real in a phenomenological and psychological sense — they are archetypal realities, real patterns of human experience that act on the psyche through ritual engagement, capable of producing genuine effects on perception, cognition, and life-trajectory, without committing to literal ontological independence from human consciousness. The chthonic deities are the real powers of the psychic underworld; they are encountered through ritual because ritual is the technology that brings the practitioner into contact with the strata of the psyche where they live.

This is the position developed most extensively by C.G. Jung and the Jungian tradition. James Hillman's The Soul's Code (Random House, 1996) and especially his Re-Visioning Psychology (Harper, 1975) are the foundational depth-psychological treatments. For the chthonic-archetypal specifically, Hillman's The Dream and the Underworld (Harper, 1979) is essential. Marie-Louise von Franz's work on the feminine archetypes — Animus and Anima in Fairy Tales (Inner City, 2002) and the broader catalogue — extends the framework. The contemporary practitioner-philosopher who most carefully develops this position in relationship to witchcraft specifically is Sophie Strand, whose The Flowering Wand (Inner Traditions, 2022) and The Madonna Secret (Bear, 2023) work this register seriously. From within this position, the question is not whether the deities are "really real" but whether the engagement is real — and the engagement is.

What practitioners actually do with the question

What practitioners across the serious literature actually report — Peter Grey, Sorita d'Este, Gemma Gary, Phil Hine, Jack Grayle, the writers of the Sabbatic-traditional current — is something like the following: the question of metaphysics is held lightly while the practice is engaged seriously. Over years of practice, the relationship with the deity becomes operative regardless of the practitioner's metaphysical position. The deity acts on the practitioner; the practice produces effects; the relationship is real. Whether to call this real in the ontologically-independent sense, the theurgic-Platonic sense, or the depth-archetypal sense becomes a question the practitioner can defer indefinitely while continuing to practice.

For someone looking for something to believe in: this is freeing rather than disappointing. You do not have to decide what you believe before you begin. You begin, and the belief develops alongside the practice, often in directions you did not anticipate. The practitioners who insist on resolving the metaphysics before practicing tend not to develop a practice. The practitioners who practice tend to develop a metaphysics — usually a richer and stranger one than they would have constructed in advance.

The metaphysical journal — a parallel record

Monthly · 30–60 minutes

§ IX

Soul-memory — past lives

The frameworks for what is popularly called past-life work are more numerous and more historically contested than the popular literature suggests. A serious engagement requires distinguishing between substantially different traditions — the Indic doctrine of samsara, the Western esoteric reincarnation literature, the clinical-empirical research at the University of Virginia, and the witchcraft framings of soul-memory and karmic continuity. Each makes different metaphysical claims and yields different practices.

The clinical-empirical literature is substantially more rigorous than is commonly recognized. Ian Stevenson, professor of psychiatry at the University of Virginia, spent forty years documenting cases of children reporting past-life memories — over 2,500 cases catalogued with substantial methodological care. His successor Jim Tucker continues the work. Whatever the metaphysics, the corpus is serious.

Dream attention — the longer-form recovery

§ X

The historical witch — which lineage

"Witch" in English names at least three distinguishable historical figures, and conflating them produces the muddle that contemporary popular witchcraft trades in. A serious practice begins by knowing which lineage you are working in.

The first is the cunning-folk tradition — professional folk-magical practitioners (cunning men and women, wise women, conjurers, pellars) documented continuously across Britain from the medieval period until the early twentieth century. They were paid for healing, divination, finding stolen goods, breaking ill-wishing. The second is the witch-trial witch — a theological construct produced by demonologists and inquisitors, projected onto often randomly accused individuals. The third is modern revived witchcraft, beginning in the 1940s through Gerald Gardner (Wicca) and Robert Cochrane (the Clan of Tubal Cain, source of contemporary traditional witchcraft).

Most contemporary popular witchcraft conflates all three. Most serious contemporary practice descends specifically from the Cochranian-Sabbatic stream, with substantial influence from cunning-folk inheritance and ceremonial magic.

§ XI

The feminine divine — a survey

The feminine divine across the world's religious traditions is plural, richly attested, and substantially more developed than the standard male-centered religious-historical canon presents. This section surveys figures across multiple traditions as orientation to the territory. Read as a map; notice what calls. None requires commitment.

The recovery of the feminine divine has been one of the central projects of women's spirituality and feminist religious scholarship across the last sixty years. The work of Carol P. Christ, Charlene Spretnak, Marija Gimbutas, Riane Eisler, Asphodel Long, Rosemary Radford Ruether, Elaine Pagels, and the broader goddess-spirituality movement has substantially recovered, reconstructed, and re-articulated traditions that the male-dominated academic religious studies of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries had marginalized or dismissed. The work continues across contemporary writers — Sharon Blackie, Sophie Strand, Mirabai Starr, Christena Cleveland, Rachel Pollack — and across the practitioner traditions that have always carried the lineages disproportionately.

The figures below are organized roughly by tradition. Each is accompanied by brief orientation and a small reading list for further engagement. The treatment is necessarily compressed; any one of these figures has substantial primary and scholarly literature.

The Greek and Mediterranean

Hekate — pre-Olympian goddess of crossroads, doorways, the dead, the dark moon, and witchcraft. Among the most extensively documented deities in the Greek world. Hesiod's Theogony 411–452 is the foundational primary text; Sarah Iles Johnston's Restless Dead (1999) and Hekate Soteira (1990) are the standard scholarly treatments; Sorita d'Este's Hekate Liminal Rites (2009) and the Circle for Hekate series (2017–) are the contemporary devotional texts.

Persephone — the descended queen of the underworld, the Kore who eats the pomegranate. Her cult at Eleusis was the principal mystery tradition of the Greek world. Christine Downing's edited anthology The Long Journey Home (1994) is the foundational women's-tradition collection on Persephone; Sylvia Brinton Perera's Descent to the Goddess: A Way of Initiation for Women (Inner City, 1981) reads the descent through the parallel Sumerian myth of Inanna and is one of the most influential women's-spirituality texts on the descended feminine. Helene Foley's translation of the Homeric Hymn to Demeter (Princeton, 1994) is the standard primary source.

Demeter — the great mother in her grief-stricken seeking, the goddess of grain and the cycles of life and death. The Eleusinian mother. Charlene Spretnak's Lost Goddesses of Early Greece (Beacon, 1981) recovers the pre-patriarchal Demeter mythology that classical Greek religion partially overwrote.

Artemis — the wild huntress, protector of women in childbirth, the unmarried virgin who chooses her own company. Jean Shinoda Bolen's Artemis: The Indomitable Spirit in Everywoman (Conari, 2014) treats her in the contemporary depth-psychological register; Ginette Paris's Pagan Meditations (Spring, 1986) is the major archetypal-psychological treatment.

Aphrodite — far more substantial than the popular reduction to "love goddess." Goddess of generative power, of beauty as religious experience, of the unmanageable. Paris's Pagan Grace (Spring, 1990) develops the feminist-archetypal treatment.

The Celtic and Northern European

Brigid — Irish goddess of poetry, smithcraft, healing, and sacred fire. Continues into Christian tradition as Saint Brigid of Kildare; the syncretic figure is one of the most living folk-devotional figures in contemporary Celtic spirituality. Mary Condren's The Serpent and the Goddess: Women, Religion, and Power in Celtic Ireland (Harper, 1989) is the foundational feminist-historical treatment; Erynn Rowan Laurie's A Circle of Stones (Eschaton, 1995) and Sharon Blackie's broader work develop her in contemporary practice.

The Cailleach — the Old Woman of the Celtic and Scottish tradition, the winter hag, the maker of mountains, the dark sister of Brigid. Increasingly central to contemporary Celtic women's spirituality as a figure of the elder, the crone, the long-lived feminine. Sharon Blackie's Hagitude: Reimagining the Second Half of Life (September Publishing, 2022) treats the Cailleach and related crone-figures across European tradition.

The Mórrígan — Irish triple-goddess of war, sovereignty, and prophecy. One of the most powerful chthonic-warrior feminine figures in European tradition. Morpheus Ravenna's The Book of the Great Queen (Concrescent, 2015) is the major contemporary devotional-scholarly treatment.

Freya, Frigg, the Disir — the Norse feminine divine, distinct figures often conflated in popular treatment. Diana Paxson's Essential Ásatrú (Citadel, 2006) provides accessible introduction; Kveldulf Gundarsson's Our Troth (the Troth) is the standard reconstructionist source.

The Mesopotamian and Near Eastern

Inanna / Ishtar — the Sumerian and later Babylonian queen of heaven, goddess of love, war, and the descent. The Inanna descent-myth (preserved in cuneiform tablets from the third millennium BCE) is among the oldest written religious texts in the world and is the foundational text for the descended-feminine archetype across Western tradition. Diane Wolkstein and Samuel Noah Kramer's Inanna, Queen of Heaven and Earth (Harper, 1983) is the standard English translation with substantial commentary; Sylvia Brinton Perera's Descent to the Goddess (cited above) reads the descent psychologically.

Lilith — the figure who has accumulated across Mesopotamian, Hebrew, and contemporary feminist tradition. From the wind-demoness of Sumerian incantation, through the rabbinic figure of Adam's first wife who refused subordination, to the contemporary feminist icon of women's autonomy and refusal. Judith Plaskow's The Coming of Lilith (Beacon, 2005) collects the Jewish feminist reclamation; Barbara Black Koltuv's The Book of Lilith (Nicolas-Hays, 1986) treats her psychologically.

The Christian and Jewish-Hermetic

The Black Madonna — the dark-skinned Marian images found across Mediterranean and European Catholic devotion (Czestochowa, Montserrat, Le Puy, hundreds of others). Long understood within feminist and Jungian scholarship as the Christian continuation of older underworld goddess cult — Demeter, Cybele, Isis. China Galland's Longing for Darkness: Tara and the Black Madonna (Penguin, 1990) is the foundational popular treatment; Lucia Chiavola Birnbaum's Black Madonnas: Feminism, Religion, and Politics in Italy (Northeastern, 1993) is the major academic treatment.

The Marys of folk Catholicism — Mary Magdalene as the apostle to the apostles (recovered substantially by feminist scholarship in the last forty years), the various local Marys of folk devotion, the indigenous-syncretic Marys of Latin American and African Diaspora traditions (Guadalupe being the most widely venerated). Cynthia Bourgeault's The Meaning of Mary Magdalene (Shambhala, 2010) and Susan Haskins's Mary Magdalen: Myth and Metaphor (Riverhead, 1993) develop the Magdalene tradition; Sophie Strand's The Madonna Secret (Bear, 2023) does the contemporary mythopoetic work.

Sophia and the Shekhinah — the feminine divine wisdom in the Jewish and Christian-Hermetic traditions. Sophia (Greek for wisdom) appears in the Wisdom literature of the Hebrew Bible and is developed extensively in Gnostic, Hermetic, and Russian Orthodox theology. The Shekhinah is the feminine indwelling presence of God in Jewish mystical tradition, particularly Kabbalah. Caitlín Matthews's Sophia: Goddess of Wisdom, Bride of God (Quest, 2001) is the standard cross-traditional treatment; for the Jewish material, Raphael Patai's The Hebrew Goddess (Wayne State, 1990) remains foundational.

The traditions named with cultural-engagement caveats

The feminine divine in indigenous and African Diaspora traditions deserves naming but cannot be entered through solo book-practice. Yemaya/Yemoja in the Yoruba and Lucumí traditions, Oshun, Oya, the various Iyami; the goddesses of Vodou (Erzulie in her many faces); the indigenous American goddesses across many traditions (the various Corn Mothers, Spider Woman, the Earth Mother in her countless local names); Kali, Durga, Sarasvati, Lakshmi, the broader Hindu feminine divine; Tara in her many forms across Tibetan Buddhism. These are real, living traditions held by living lineages with their own initiation requirements. Read about them as part of your education. Do not approach them as practice without lineage authorization. China Galland's Longing for Darkness models the appropriate stance for a Western practitioner — sustained study and reverent engagement without claiming initiation.

Devotional engagement — if and when a figure calls

§ XII

Animism and the living world — land, plant, animal, stone

Animism is the foundational philosophical position underneath every serious witchcraft practice — that the world is alive, that the non-human is populated by selves with whom relationship is possible and necessary. It is not decoration on the practice; it is the ground on which everything else stands.

The term animism was coined by the Victorian anthropologist E.B. Tylor in Primitive Culture (1871) as a category for what he considered "primitive" religion — the belief that natural objects and phenomena are inhabited by spirits or souls. Tylor's framing was condescending and evolutionist; he treated animism as the earliest stage of religion to be superseded by polytheism, then monotheism, then rational secularism. The contemporary philosophical and anthropological recovery of animism — particularly in the work of Graham Harvey, Eduardo Kohn, Bruno Latour, Anna Tsing, and the broader "ontological turn" in anthropology — has substantially reversed this evaluation, treating animism as a sophisticated philosophical position about the nature of personhood and relationship that the Western secular-rational tradition has impoverished itself by abandoning.

Graham Harvey's working definition, from Animism: Respecting the Living World (Columbia, 2005): animists are "people who recognize that the world is full of persons, only some of whom are human, and that life is always lived in relationship with others." The trees are persons. The rivers are persons. The dead are persons. The animals are persons. The land is a person, or many. The stones, depending on the tradition, may be persons. The relationships between human persons and these non-human persons are real, ongoing, ethically substantive, and the proper territory of religious life.

This is the ground on which every working in this document operates. The chthonic deities are real persons in this sense. The dead are persons. The plants you make offerings of are persons. The land you live on is a person, with whom you are in relationship whether or not you have noticed. The animal that crosses your path three times in a week is a person, possibly with something to say. The witch's traditional relationships with familiars, with plant-allies, with the spirits of land and place, are animist relationships — relationships between persons across kinds.

The witchcraft animism specifically

Within the witchcraft tradition, animism takes specific operative forms. The familiar spirit tradition — documented across European cunning-folk practice, treated extensively in Emma Wilby's Cunning Folk and Familiar Spirits — is animist relationship formalized into devotional and operative practice. Historical witches and cunning-folk worked with specific spirit-allies, often in animal form (the toad, the cat, the hare, the fox, the raven), with whom they had developed long-term reciprocal relationships. The familiar was not a "tool" or a "magical assistant" in the modern sense; it was a person across the threshold with whom the witch had a working relationship.

Plant-spirit work — the relationship with specific plants as persons with their own agency, intelligence, and willingness or refusal to participate in the witch's work — is documented throughout the European folk-magical tradition and has substantial contemporary practitioner literature. The witch does not simply "use" mugwort or yarrow or vervain; the witch is in relationship with these plant-persons, asks their participation, makes offerings, listens to their refusal. The same is true of stones, of bodies of water, of specific places on the land.

Animal communication, in the witchcraft register, runs from the most conservative — reading the appearance of certain animals as omen or message, in the long tradition of augury documented from Etruscan and Roman religion through European folk practice — to the more substantive: developed working relationships with specific animal-persons (often called familiars in the witchcraft register, power animals in the modern shamanic register, tutelary animals in the older anthropological vocabulary). The contemporary literature on this is uneven; the practitioner-quality material exists but requires careful selection.

Indigenous practice and the question of appropriation

A direct word: most of what is called "shamanism" or "animism" in contemporary Western spiritual marketplace is appropriated from indigenous traditions in ways that are both ethically troubling and practically corrupting. The witchcraft tradition has its own animist inheritance — European, well-documented, recoverable — and that is the inheritance to work in. Cunning-folk practice, the familiar tradition, the European folk-magical relationship with land and plant and animal, is your ground. You do not need to draw from Lakota, Yoruba, Amazonian, or Aboriginal Australian traditions to enter animist practice; the inheritance you have access to is sufficient and arguably more appropriate.

Where contemporary indigenous teachers have explicitly written for non-indigenous audiences and offered their teaching across cultures — Robin Wall Kimmerer, Tyson Yunkaporta, Robin Wall Kimmerer's broader work, Malidoma Somé in his ancestral work — engagement is appropriate and welcomed by the authors themselves. Where the material is being repackaged by non-indigenous teachers without lineage authorization, it is not.

The land you live on

One specific subcategory deserves mention: the relationship with the specific land you live on. In the European folk-magical tradition this was the relationship with the genius loci (the spirit of the place), the household spirits, the dead of the land, the wells and springs and stones of the immediate landscape. In the indigenous American context — relevant for you in California — there is a parallel tradition that you can engage with respect: learning whose land you actually live on (the original peoples whose territory it is and was), the names and stories of the specific places near you, the histories of what happened on this land before and after colonization.

Beginning a relationship with the specific land you live on is one of the foundational moves in serious witchcraft practice. It begins with naming — knowing what land this is, who lived here, what the place was called in older languages, what the rivers and hills and trees were named — and continues with sustained attention and small offerings over years.

Practice

The land sit — foundational animist practice

Weekly · 30–60 minutes · the same place each time

Choose one specific place outdoors that you can return to weekly. A particular tree in a park, a stretch of beach, a corner of a garden, a hillside — anywhere outdoors that you have reasonable access to. The same place each time, ideally for at least a year.

Sit there. Without phone. Without book. Without agenda. Notice what is alive there — the trees, the birds, the insects, the plants underfoot, the weather, the quality of the air. Over weeks and months, you will begin to notice the place rather than just inhabiting it. The plants will become specific plants you recognize. The birds will become specific species whose calls you learn. The light will become specific kinds of light at specific times of year.

This is not meditation. It is the establishment of relationship. Over a year, the place becomes someone you know, and you become someone known by the place. This is the foundation of the animist sensibility — not as belief but as developed perception.

Practice

Plant introduction — one plant at a time

One plant per season · ongoing practice over years

Choose one plant per season — three months — and learn it deeply. Read its botanical and folk-magical descriptions. Find it growing wild near you if possible; if not, grow it. Sit with it. Sketch it. Notice when it flowers, when it fruits, when it dies back. Make a small offering at its base — a cup of water, a strand of hair, a few drops of milk. Speak to it. Listen.

Begin with European witchcraft-traditional plants if the chthonic frame is the spine: mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris), yarrow (Achillea millefolium), vervain (Verbena officinalis), rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus), rue (Ruta graveolens), wormwood (Artemisia absinthium). These are the plants the European witch tradition most consistently named as allies. Build relationship with them slowly.

Over years, you build a small group of plant-allies — five, ten, fifteen — who you have actual ongoing relationships with. This is the practitioner's herbal. It is not learned from books; it is grown.

Practice

Animal attention — augury and the question of communication

Ongoing · as encounters arrive

Begin paying close attention to the animals that appear in your life — wild, domestic, briefly encountered, recurring. The crow that lands on your fence. The deer in the park. The dog you met. The hawk overhead. Record significant encounters in the grimoire: date, animal, context, what was happening in your life, what you noticed.

Over time, patterns may emerge: certain animals appearing at certain crossroads in your life, recurring species in dreams, specific encounters that feel weighted. The European tradition of augury — reading the appearance of animals as communication or omen — is one of the oldest documented witchcraft practices. The contemporary practitioner can take it up as developed observation rather than as a system imposed on experience.

The deeper version of animal communication — sustained working relationship with specific animal-persons — is its own developed practice. The conservative entry: pay attention, record, notice what arrives. Whether the relationships develop into something more is for the practice to reveal, not for you to decide in advance.

§ XIII

The lunar architecture

Lunar timekeeping is a calendrical discipline with deep roots in Mediterranean and European ritual practice. The serious version of working with the moon is not "moon vibes" but a sustained relationship with a second calendar that runs underneath the secular one — twelve to thirteen lunar months per solar year, with the new and full moons as the operative poles.

The technical distinction worth knowing: the dark moon, the new moon, and the first visible crescent are three different things. The Hekatean dark-moon working (the heneē kai nea, "the old and new") is performed on the night of complete invisibility, not at the astronomical moment of conjunction or at the first visible sliver. Most modern lunar calendars conflate these. The serious practice is to distinguish them.

Practice

Lunar observation — the prerequisite

One full lunar cycle · approximately 29.5 days · ~ 60 seconds nightly

Before any lunar working, observe the moon for one full cycle. Look at it, every night you can. Note the phase. Notice how the phase relates to the time of day and the position in the sky (waxing crescents are visible at dusk in the west; waning crescents are visible before dawn in the east; full moons rise at sunset and set at sunrise).

Most contemporary practitioners begin lunar work without this baseline observation, and the practice never grounds. Develop the eye first.

Practice

The two-pole working — the minimum lunar discipline

Twice monthly · the dark moon and the full moon · 30–60 minutes each

The minimum serious lunar practice is twenty-four marked nights per year — the dark moon (the Hekatean Deipnon, or any chthonic-banishing-purifying working you develop) and the full moon (esbat working: divinatory operation, charging of waters and tools, sustained altar contact, oracular work).

Develop your own structure for each over time, drawing from Pearson, d'Este, and your own working diary. The traditions differ; the discipline is consistency.

Build

Your lunar calendar — for the year ahead

Map every dark moon and every full moon for the next twelve months in your actual calendar. Use a serious astronomical source — timeanddate.com or a printed almanac — not a moon-phase app, which often imprecisely conflates dark and new moon. Set reminders the day before each.

Over time, build:

  • A wall-mounted lunar calendar or almanac for the year — Susan Baylies's lunar calendars, Llewellyn's Witches' Calendar, or your own constructed one
  • A lunar working journal — a separate notebook or section in your grimoire, with entries for each dark moon and full moon: phase, weather, what was worked, what arrived after
  • An ongoing record of correspondences you observe — what kinds of working go well at which phases, what kinds of dreams arrive, what your nervous system does. Over years, this becomes your own developed lunar lore.
Research thread

The menstrual-lunar relationship is one of the most contested topics in the literature. The popular claim that women's cycles align with the moon is supported by some studies (Cutler 1980, Helfrich-Förster et al. 2021) and contradicted by others (Komada et al. 2021). The "white moon" and "red moon" cycle frameworks descend from late twentieth-century goddess-spirituality writing (Vicki Noble, Demetra George) and are not historically attested. If this is part of your practice, the serious version is to track both cycles empirically over years and develop your own framework. The literature is suggestive, not prescriptive.

§ XIV

Sigil and image — making as magic

Sigil work as practiced in serious witchcraft has a specific technical lineage and is one of the most direct vectors by which your professional practice as a designer and maker is already adjacent to the work.

The technique now generically called sigil magic was developed by the British artist-occultist Austin Osman Spare (1886–1956) and articulated in The Book of Pleasure (1913). Spare's method was a deliberate attempt to bypass elaborate ceremonial apparatus in favor of a direct technique grounded in his own theory of the unconscious. The technique was systematized into modern chaos magic by Peter Carroll and Phil Hine in the 1980s and 1990s. Behind both lies the deeper lineage of the charactēres — the abstract glyphs of the Greek Magical Papyri, used in essentially the same way two millennia earlier.

Practice

Sigil construction — the Sparean method

One sigil per working session · ~ 30–45 minutes

The classical method, as Spare gave it: formulate desire as a sentence of intent. Strike out repeating letters. Recombine remaining letters into an abstract glyph. Charge through what Spare called the death posture — physiological exhaustion, sexual climax, sustained attention to the point of mental quietness. Forget the original intent; the sigil now operates without conscious interference.

Read Hine and Carroll for the procedural detail. Develop your own variations over time — graphical, sonic, photographic, gestural. Spare himself worked across many media; the method scales.

Build

Your sigil archive — a working art practice

Begin a dedicated archive of your own sigils. This is where your professional practice and your magical practice merge — you are already a designer and maker; the discipline is to bring intentionality to the making.

  • A physical sigil book — a sketchbook or large grimoire devoted to sigil work alone, with the constructed glyph, the date, the intent (sealed or written), the charging method, and (over time) the outcome
  • A library of personal charactēres — your own developed glyphs for recurring uses (protection, focus, dreaming, threshold-marking, divination)
  • A correspondence between your design practice and your sigil practice — the same eye, the same skill, the same commitment to encoding meaning in form. Over years, the boundary will dissolve in productive ways.
Research thread

Phil Baker's Lord of Strange Deaths (on Sax Rohmer, but tangentially relevant) and Gary Lachman's Turn Off Your Mind map portions of this territory. Worth following if the artist-occultist intersection is part of where this practice is going for you.

§ XV

Tarot and divination

Tarot is a serious symbolic-philosophical system with a four-hundred-year European history and roughly a century of integration into Western occult practice. Treated seriously, it is a multi-decade discipline; treated casually, it produces the kind of generic "card of the day" reading that contemporary popular witchcraft trades in. The serious version asks for the long study.

The structure to know: the standard tarot deck has 78 cards — 22 Major Arcana (the named trumps, often read as a Hero's Journey or alchemical sequence), and 56 Minor Arcana divided into four suits (Wands, Cups, Swords, Pentacles), each with ten numbered cards and four court cards. The Rider-Waite-Smith deck (1909, illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith under A.E. Waite's direction) is the contemporary standard and the one most modern tarot literature is keyed to. Begin there before exploring other decks.

Read
Core

The canonical depth-text on the Rider-Waite tarot. Treats the cards as a complete symbolic-philosophical system. Read slowly, one card at a time, as a years-long study. This is the single most useful tarot book in print.

Depth
The Tarot: History, Symbolism, and Divination Robert M. Place · Tarcher, 2005

The most rigorous historical scholarship on the actual provenance of tarot — a fifteenth-century Italian card game that became occultized only in the eighteenth century. Disabuses the practitioner of the Egyptian-origin myth and grounds the practice historically.

Depth

An entirely different reading — keyed to the older Marseille deck rather than Rider-Waite. Symbolic, esoteric, more demanding. Useful for widening the frame after grounding in Pollack.

Practice

The single-card daily pull — the foundational divinatory training

Daily · ~ 2–5 minutes

One card pulled daily, at a consistent time (morning or before bed). Recorded by name and date in a dedicated tarot journal with a single sentence of response — what the card brought up, not formal interpretation. At the end of each week, read back through the seven entries and note any pattern.

Over a year, this builds a substantial divinatory record. Over five years, it becomes the basis of your personal relationship with each card, which is the actual divinatory training. The book-knowledge is preliminary; the lived relationship is the practice.

Practice

The three-card weekly pull — pattern over time

Weekly · ~ 20 minutes

Three cards for the week ahead, in any structure that suits — past/present/future, situation/action/outcome, what to release/hold/anticipate. Recorded with a paragraph of interpretation. At the end of the week, read back and note what was true.

The crucial discipline is the review at the end of the week, not the pulling at the beginning. Divinatory accuracy is built in the looking-back.

Build

Your tarot working library — deck, journal, personal book of cards

The tools you build over years:

  • One primary working deck — Rider-Waite or a serious successor (the Smith-Waite Centennial, the Radiant Rider-Waite, the Sasuraibito, the Wild Unknown if more contemporary). Sleep with it, carry it, use it daily until it becomes yours.
  • A dedicated tarot journal — separate from the main grimoire — for daily pulls, weekly spreads, and reflective entries. This becomes your personal divinatory record.
  • A personal book of cards — over years, a hand-built reference where you record your own developed reading of each of the 78 cards: the imagery you notice, the personal meanings that have emerged, the situations where the card has shown up, the relationships between cards you have observed. This is your own tarot — distinct from any author's, built from your practice.
Research thread

The intersection of tarot with the broader Western esoteric tradition is substantial. The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (1888–1903) developed elaborate tarot correspondences keyed to Kabbalah, astrology, and ceremonial magic — most contemporary occult tarot descends from their work. Israel Regardie's The Golden Dawn (1937) is the foundational source; Lon Milo DuQuette's Understanding the Thoth Tarot is the standard contemporary treatment of Crowley's elaborate Thoth deck, which extends the Golden Dawn system. Worth following if the systematic-correspondential frame draws you.

§ XVI

A survey of traditions — the territory, briefly

What follows is a map, not a curriculum. Each of the traditions below is a real, documented stream with primary sources, scholarly literature, and contemporary practitioner communities. Read the surveys to know what exists. Notice what calls. Read further into what calls. Do not commit. The early years are for breadth.

The traditions are grouped roughly by family — the Hellenic and chthonic streams, the modern witchcraft revivals, the ceremonial and Hermetic currents, the European folk and cunning-craft traditions. Each survey gives a short orientation, the foundational texts to read if the tradition pulls, and brief notes on what distinguishes it from the others. The reading lists are starting points, not exhaustive.

A note on what is and is not surveyed: the document focuses on Western magical and devotional traditions because those are the ones a contemporary Western practitioner can responsibly enter through reading and solo practice. Eastern traditions (Buddhist tantra, Hindu tantric and devotional practice, Daoist internal alchemy) are real and substantial but typically require sustained engagement with living teachers, language acquisition, and substantial cultural immersion that solo book-practice cannot substitute for. They are mentioned in the closing notes only. Indigenous, African Diasporic, and other initiation-required traditions (Yoruba and Lucumí, Vodou, Santería, traditional curanderismo, Native American practice) are similarly named only in closing — they require initiation through their living lineages and are not appropriate for solo practice from books, regardless of how the popular spirituality marketplace treats them.

The Hellenic and chthonic traditions

The Greek and Greco-Roman religious traditions — including their late-Platonic theurgic developments and their continuing devotional revival in contemporary Hellenic polytheism — are among the most textually rich and continuously documented of the Western religious lineages. The chthonic stream within them (Hekate, Persephone, Demeter, the Eleusinian mysteries, the cult of the dead) is a particular emphasis within the broader Hellenic tradition. Contemporary practitioners often work in either the broader Hellenic devotional register (multiple deities, seasonal festivals based on the Athenian calendar) or the more specifically chthonic register (focused on the underworld deities and the dead).

What distinguishes the tradition: a substantial primary-source base in Greek (and Roman) literature; the late-Platonic theurgic philosophical apparatus that gives it theological depth; a continuous devotional revival from the nineteenth century forward; and an emphasis on relationship with named, distinct deities rather than on abstract magical operation. The chthonic emphasis specifically takes death, the dead, and the underworld as central religious territory rather than as marginal. Working with the dead is a developed devotional practice in this tradition rather than a casual undertaking; it is appropriately approached after years of broader practice rather than as an entry point.

Wicca and the modern witchcraft revivals

Modern Wicca was constructed in the 1940s and 1950s by Gerald Gardner, drawing on Margaret Murray's (now-discredited) thesis of a surviving European witch-cult, ceremonial magic from the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, folk practice, and Gardner's own creative synthesis. The tradition has since branched substantially — Gardnerian, Alexandrian, Dianic, Reclaiming, eclectic, solitary — and has become the most widely practiced contemporary Western pagan religion.

What distinguishes the tradition: a duotheistic theology centered on the God and Goddess; a structured liturgical calendar of eight sabbats and twelve to thirteen esbats; a circle-casting ritual structure derived from ceremonial magic; a strong emphasis on initiation, lineage transmission, and coven structure (in the traditional forms) or on personal eclectic practice (in the solitary forms); a generally celebratory, life-affirming theological orientation in contrast to the chthonic stream. Wicca is the most accessible entry into structured Western pagan practice and has the largest contemporary literature, but the literature is uneven — much of the popular Wiccan publishing is the contemporary self-help register the document warned against.

Traditional witchcraft — the Cochranian and Sabbatic streams

Traditional witchcraft as a self-conscious contemporary current descends from Robert Cochrane (1931–1966) and the Clan of Tubal Cain, distinguished from Wicca by its emphasis on chthonic deity-work, the witches' sabbath as visionary state, spirit-work with the dead and with familiar spirits, and an aesthetic that draws from cunning-folk and folk-magical practice rather than from Wiccan ceremonial structure. The current was elaborated through the late twentieth century by Andrew Chumbley (1967–2004) and Daniel Schulke as the Sabbatic Craft — the most theologically and operatively developed contemporary traditional-witchcraft lineage.

What distinguishes the tradition: a darker theological register than Wicca, with chthonic and "left-hand" deities at the center; an emphasis on initiation, spirit-allies, and visionary practice; a serious operative magical tradition drawing on cunning-folk inheritance and ceremonial magic; a small-press literature (Scarlet Imprint, Three Hands Press, Xoanon) with substantial intellectual depth; and a generally austere aesthetic that distinguishes it sharply from the popular witchcraft register. Some of the most serious contemporary witchcraft writing is in this stream.

The cunning-folk and European folk-magical traditions

The cunning-folk tradition — the documented historical practice of professional folk-magical practitioners across Britain and continental Europe — is the historical substrate from which much of contemporary traditional witchcraft descends. Distinct from both the witch-trial-era theological construct and the modern revived witchcraft, cunning-folk practice is what the practitioners actually did: healing, divination, breaking ill-wishing, charm-magic, herbal work, communication with the dead. It survived continuously into the early twentieth century and has been substantially recovered by contemporary scholarship.

The broader European folk-magical traditions — Italian stregheria, Slavic vedma traditions, Iberian and Mexican folk-Catholic magic, Irish folk practice, Norse and Germanic folk magic — each have their own distinct histories, primary sources, and contemporary practitioner communities. Engagement with these typically benefits from cultural and ancestral connection (your matrilineal line opens specific traditions more than others) but does not require it; many traditions have substantial published material in English.

Ceremonial magic and the Hermetic tradition

Western ceremonial magic descends from a long lineage — the late-antique Greco-Egyptian magical papyri, medieval grimoiric tradition, Renaissance Hermeticism (Marsilio Ficino, Pico della Mirandola, Cornelius Agrippa), the Rosicrucian tradition, and the Victorian-era synthesis in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (1888–1903). Contemporary ceremonial magic largely descends from the Golden Dawn, with substantial branches through Aleister Crowley's Thelema and the various derivative orders (OTO, A∴A∴).

What distinguishes the tradition: a highly structured ritual apparatus (banishing rituals, invocations, evocations, planetary and elemental correspondences); substantial integration with Kabbalah, astrology, and alchemy; an initiatory grade system; and a generally cosmological and theurgic frame. Ceremonial magic and witchcraft have substantial historical overlap — modern Wicca borrowed extensively from Golden Dawn ritual structure — but they sit in distinguishable registers. The ceremonial tradition is more elaborate, more formal, more philosophically systematized; the witchcraft tradition is more direct, more practical, more spirit-relational.

Chaos magic and the eclectic tradition

Chaos magic is a deliberately syncretic, post-modern tradition developed in the late 1970s and 1980s primarily through Peter Carroll and Phil Hine, drawing on Austin Osman Spare's earlier work. Distinguished by its operational rather than devotional orientation — the practitioner adopts and discards belief systems pragmatically, evaluating each by what it produces rather than by what it commits to — chaos magic has been substantially influential on contemporary witchcraft and ceremonial practice without being a tradition most practitioners commit to as a primary identity.

What distinguishes the tradition: a strong emphasis on technique over theology; the use of "paradigm shift" as deliberate practice (working in different magical frameworks for different operations); sigil magic as central technique; a generally non-devotional, non-deity-relational orientation; and an irreverent, often deliberately transgressive aesthetic. Useful as a body of technique for any practitioner regardless of primary tradition; less useful as a sole devotional commitment because it does not develop the depth of relational practice that the other traditions specialize in.

Other traditions — briefly named

Several other traditions deserve naming even where this document cannot survey them properly. Druidry — the contemporary revival of Celtic religious practice, with both reconstructionist and revival branches (the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids; the Ár nDraíocht Féin). Heathenry — contemporary Norse and Germanic polytheism, with substantial practitioner literature and an academically rigorous reconstructionist branch (note that the tradition has been targeted for appropriation by far-right movements; legitimate practitioner communities are explicit about their opposition to this). Feri — the American witchcraft tradition founded by Victor and Cora Anderson, distinct from both Wicca and traditional craft. Anderson Faery, Reclaiming, Stregheria — each with their own developed lineages and primary literature.

The traditions named only in passing because they require what solo book-practice cannot provide: Buddhist tantra in its various lineages (Tibetan, Japanese Shingon), Hindu tantric and devotional practice, Daoist internal alchemy, Yoruba and Lucumí, Vodou in its Haitian and Louisiana forms, Santería, Candomblé, traditional curanderismo, indigenous Native American religious practice in its many forms. These are real, living, often initiation-required traditions held by living lineages. They are not appropriate for solo entry from books, regardless of how the popular spirituality marketplace treats them. If any of these eventually call you, the appropriate path is to seek out living teachers in their lineage.

The discipline of the early years: read across these traditions broadly. Read the entry text in each that calls. Notice which traditions you keep returning to, and which you read once and put down. Notice which deities, figures, or practices recur in your dreams, your reading, your attention. After two or three years of this, the territory will have organized itself for you in a way that no advance decision could have produced.

  • The recurring figures, deities, symbols, or practices that show up across your reading regardless of which tradition you're reading at the time

§ XVII

Ancestral and matrilineal work

Ancestral veneration is among the most widely attested ritual practices in human cultures and one of the few areas where contemporary witchcraft engages with a continuous, cross-cultural, and well-documented practice. The serious literature is correspondingly substantial.

For solo practice in a Western context without an intact lineage tradition, the contemporary methodology is largely Daniel Foor's. Foor is a clinical psychologist (PsyD) trained both academically and through apprenticeship in Yoruba ancestral practice with Malidoma Patrice Somé. His framework is structured, conservative, trauma-aware, and explicitly designed for solo work. It is the closest thing the field has to a clinical-grade methodology. The serious version of ancestral work begins with reading Foor.

The clinical-empirical literature on epigenetic transmission of trauma across generations. Substantively grounds the matrilineal-inheritance claim in contemporary biology. Search "Yehuda transgenerational" on Google Scholar; many papers are open-access.

Practice

The matrilineal sit — Foor methodology, entry version

Monthly or quarterly · 30–60 minutes

The simplest serious ancestral practice: establish contact with one well ancestor (per Foor's stage-one methodology — at least three generations back, beyond living memory of immediate trauma, who feels bright and resourced when you sit with them). Build relationship through small offerings, conversation at the altar, dream attention.

Read the book before attempting. The full methodology (stages two through four — working with the well ancestor as ally to address the broader lineage) requires the Foor-trained practitioner network or substantial individual study before solo work is appropriate.

Build

Your matrilineal record — the line you've been told you carry

The ancestral work is, in part, a long-form research project. Build, over years:

  • A genealogical record of your matrilineal line as far back as you can trace — names, dates, locations, occupations, what they survived. Use Ancestry, FamilySearch, family records, oral history. The act of recovering the names is itself ancestral practice.
  • A dedicated section in your grimoire for ancestral work — every sit recorded, every dream that arrived, every sign you read, every name spoken
  • An ancestor altar, distinct from your primary working surface — photographs if you have them, named papers if you don't, an offering vessel, a candle reserved for ancestral work
  • A living document of what your line carried — the trauma, the gift, the trade, the place, the language, the food, the practice. Build this slowly. It becomes the substrate of the work.
Research thread

The historical witchcraft of your specific matrilineal line is worth investigating. The Italian stregheria tradition, Slavic vedma traditions, Mexican curanderismo, Irish folk magic, English cunning-folk practice, Scottish folk witchcraft — each has its own primary literature and its own contemporary scholarly treatment. If you can locate your matrilineal line geographically and culturally, the specific tradition opens up. Ronald Hutton's The Witch contains a useful chapter-by-chapter map by region.

§ XVIII

On lineage commitment — how it happens, when it happens

If lineage commitment ever happens for you, it will not happen in the early years and it will not happen by decision. It will happen by recognition. This section is for that eventual recognition, and for what to do in the years before it arrives.

The discipline of the early years has been named throughout this document: read across traditions, build the foundational practices, attend to what calls and what does not, do not rush to commit. What has not been named yet is what eventual commitment actually looks like, and what the practitioner's task is in the meantime.

The shape of the early years

Across the practitioner literature, the consistent description of the early years is something like the following. The practitioner reads broadly. Builds the foundational practices — a daily candle, a daily card pull, a weekly land sit, a sustained reading log, a developing grimoire. Tries practices from several traditions and notices what happens. Reads through several theological frames and notices which arguments hold and which fall away. Begins, slowly, to develop what could be called a working sensibility — an intuition about which kinds of practice produce real effect for them and which feel like going through motions.

The duration of this phase is typically not weeks or months. It is usually three to five years for a practitioner who is engaged seriously, and considerably longer for one whose engagement is more periodic. There is no rushing it. Practitioners who attempt to commit to a lineage in the first year almost universally end up either abandoning the commitment or having to undo it later. The commitment that holds is the one that arrives slowly, after enough material has been engaged for the recognition to be real.

What recognition looks like, when it comes

The literature describes the eventual recognition consistently across traditions. It is not an enthusiastic decision; it is a noticing. The practitioner notices that they have been returning to one set of texts more than others. That one deity has been showing up across their dreams, their reading, their attention, in ways that are no longer plausibly coincidence. That one tradition's practices have been the ones that consistently produce real effect rather than just producing the experience of practicing. That when they sit with the question of what they actually believe, one frame holds and the others fall away.

The recognition is usually accompanied by something like reluctance. The practitioner often does not want to commit, because commitment narrows the broad exploration that has been the work of the early years. There is a real loss in commitment — the other traditions become permanently the not-chosen, and the practitioner becomes someone who has narrowed. This reluctance is appropriate and is not a sign that the recognition is wrong. The practitioners who report no reluctance generally report later that they committed too early.

When the recognition holds and the commitment is made, the relationship to the practice changes. The early-years breadth is replaced by depth. The grimoire develops differently — Hekatean material accumulates in a way that other deity material no longer does, or the lunar work becomes specifically esbat work in a tradition's structure rather than personal observation. The reading list narrows. The community of other practitioners becomes possible to find — most lineages have at least informal networks. The practice deepens substantially as a result of the narrowing.

What to do in the meantime

The work of the early years has been laid out in the rest of this document. Position yourself with § I, build the grimoire (§ II), establish the foundational literacy and rhythm (§§ III–VI: altar, ritual, wheel, practice rhythm). Build the inner-foundation practices: the body (§ VII), engage the question of belief without trying to resolve it (§ VIII), open soul-memory work (§ IX), ground in the historical witch (§ X), survey the feminine divine (§ XI), develop animism (§ XII), the lunar architecture (§ XIII), sigil work (§ XIV), and tarot (§ XV). After substantial time on the inner foundations, survey the traditions (§ XVI) and develop ancestral and matrilineal work (§ XVII). Do not commit. Do not let anyone else's recognition become yours by proxy. Do not let the seductive depth of any single tradition's literature pull you into commitment before the recognition is yours.

What you are building, across all of this, is not a generic spirituality and not yet a tradition. You are building a practitioner — someone with the foundational skills, the broad reading, the developed sensibility, and the lived experience that any eventual lineage commitment will rest on. The practitioner is what carries across whatever tradition you eventually find yourself in. The practitioner is what you have to show for the early years.

If commitment never comes

It is worth naming directly: some practitioners never arrive at a single-lineage commitment. They develop instead what could be called an eclectic-grounded practice — sustained engagement with foundational practices, periodic deep engagement with specific traditions, devotional relationships with several figures across several traditions, ongoing reading across the field. This is a real and serious practitioner mode and is not a failure of commitment.

The distinguishing feature between a serious eclectic practice and the popular eclecticism the document warned against early is the depth of the foundational work. The serious eclectic practitioner has engaged the major traditions seriously, has built the foundational practices over years, has developed the metaphysical and practical sensibility that comes only from sustained work — and from that ground has chosen, deliberately, to remain plural rather than narrowing. This is a defensible position. Some of the most accomplished contemporary practitioners hold it.

Build

The recognition record — a long-form attention

Across the years of broad practice, maintain a section in your grimoire (or its own short notebook) for what could be called the recognition record. Quarterly, in a single sitting, write briefly:

  • What I have been reading most consistently this quarter
  • What practices have produced real effect rather than just the experience of practicing
  • What deities, figures, or traditions have shown up across my attention without me seeking them
  • What I currently believe, in two or three sentences (this echoes the metaphysical journal in § VIII but kept short here)
  • What I notice has fallen away — what was a pull a year ago that no longer is

After three or five years of these entries, the pattern will be visible to you in a way it cannot be in any single quarter. The eventual recognition, if it comes, will be the noticing of the pattern that has been there for years. The record makes the noticing possible.

Research thread

The sociological literature on religious commitment and "switching" — particularly the work on adult religious conversion and the trajectories of contemporary alternative religious practitioners — is worth engaging if the question of commitment becomes pressing. Lewis Rambo's Understanding Religious Conversion (Yale, 1993) is the standard text. For the specifically pagan context, Sarah Pike's New Age and Neopagan Religions in America (Columbia, 2004) provides the broader sociological frame.

§ XIX

Finding teachers and community — when solo work asks for company

Solo practice is the right shape for the early years and may remain the right shape indefinitely. But there are kinds of work that solo practice cannot hold, and there are points in a developing practice when finding actual teachers, practitioners, or community becomes part of the next move. This section is for those points.

The discipline of solo practice that the rest of this document develops is real and not lesser. Most contemporary serious practitioners spend substantial portions of their practicing lives working alone, with reading and writing and the slow accumulation of the grimoire as their primary companions. The contemporary practitioner literature is consistent that solitary practice can produce real depth across decades. But certain kinds of work — initiation-required traditions, lineage-transmitted material, deep ancestral repair where the lineage is complex or the inheritance is heavy, devotional work with figures whose tradition has living human carriers, and the simple human need to not be the only person doing this — eventually ask for company.

What follows is organized by the kind of company you might be looking for and by the streams this document has surveyed. The directories and organizations named are real and current as of writing; verify before reaching out, as practitioner communities reorganize over time.

For ancestral and lineage repair work specifically

For a practitioner working with complex ancestral material — including the kind of inheritance that involves descent from both enslaved and enslaver, or other ancestrally violent configurations — a trained ancestral practitioner can hold material that solo work cannot safely metabolize. The major networks:

The Ancestral Medicine network (ancestralmedicine.org) — founded by Daniel Foor, the practitioner whose methodology is treated in § XVII. Maintains a directory of trained ancestral lineage repair practitioners across the US and internationally. Many work via video call; the network is structured so that a complete cycle of foundational ancestral repair work can be done in a year of monthly sessions. The training is rigorous (multi-year), trauma-aware, and explicitly designed to handle the kind of complex inheritance configurations many contemporary practitioners are working with. For practitioners specifically working with inheritances of slavery, racial violence, and the tangled lineages produced by both, several Foor-trained practitioners specialize in this work — search the directory by specialization.

The Pocket Project (pocketproject.org) — Thomas Hübl's collective trauma framework. Substantial focus on collective and ancestral trauma; offers cohort-based courses on ancestral healing. Less individually clinical than Foor; more group-oriented. Worth knowing as alternative methodology.

For the inheritance of slavery specifically — Resmaa Menakem's body of work, named throughout this document, includes both his own clinical practice and the broader "Cultural Somatics" training network he has developed. The training is explicitly designed for somatic-ancestral work with racialized trauma, including the inheritance carried by descendants of both enslaved and enslaver. Find practitioners through the Cultural Somatics Institute (culturalsomaticsinstitute.com).

For the genealogical research itself — the African American Historical and Genealogical Society (aahgs.org) is the major organization for Black American genealogical research, with substantial expertise in tracing lines through enslavement records. Several professional researchers specialize in confirming or disconfirming connections between enslaver and enslaved family lines: Nicka Sewell-Smith (whocheckedout.com), Bernice Bennett (and the Research at the National Archives podcast), and the broader BlackProGen network. This is its own discipline; the practitioners do this work with rigor and care.

For the West African and African Diaspora traditions

For traditions that require initiation through living lineages, the appropriate path is to find legitimate elders and houses. The contemporary landscape is uneven — there are serious lineages and there are exploitative operations, and discernment matters substantially. Some general guidance:

Lucumí / Santería / Ifá — the diasporic Yoruba tradition as practiced in Cuba, the United States, and increasingly directly through Yoruba practitioners in Nigeria. Initiation is required for most substantive practice. Legitimate houses can be found through the Council of Ifá Priests of North America (cipnaifa.com) and through reputable Ilé (houses) — but the appropriate first move is sustained reading (Aina Olomo's Iyanifa, Migene González-Wippler's older introductory texts, the broader practitioner literature) and then attendance at public-facing events (bembés, drumming ceremonies open to the community) before any inquiry into initiation. The legitimate elders generally test for seriousness across years before bringing anyone toward initiation. The exploitative operators, by contrast, charge substantial sums to "initiate" people quickly. The difference is usually visible if you take time.

Haitian Vodou and Louisiana Voodoo — for Louisiana specifically, given your family ties: the New Orleans Voodoo Spiritual Temple (vodouspiritualtemple.org), founded by Priestess Miriam Chamani, has been a continuous practitioner community for over thirty years and is one of the legitimate public-facing temples in the Louisiana Voodoo tradition. The Voodoo Authentica Cultural Center and Collection in the French Quarter is another. For Haitian Vodou specifically — distinct from Louisiana Voodoo — initiation runs through Haitian houses, with practitioner networks in New Orleans, Miami, Brooklyn, and increasingly directly in Haiti. Mambo Sallie Ann Glassman's La Source Ancienne Ounfo (feyvodou.com) in New Orleans is a long-established Haitian Vodou house in the United States.

Hoodoo / Conjure — the African-American folk-magical tradition is less institutionalized than Lucumí or Vodou and has historically been transmitted through family and community. The contemporary practitioner landscape has substantial variety; the major contemporary practitioner-teacher is Stephanie Rose Bird (Sticks, Stones, Roots and Bones, 2004), whose work treats Hoodoo as both folk-magical practice and Black American ancestral inheritance. Lucky Mojo Curio Company (luckymojo.com), run by Catherine Yronwode, offers correspondence courses in Hoodoo that are widely used though contested in the practitioner community (Yronwode is white and the question of who can teach this tradition is internally debated). For specifically Black American transmission, the Conjure Crossroads festival in New Orleans and the broader work of Black Hoodoo practitioners like Hoodoo Sen Moise (the Conjure Cookbook author) are worth following.

For someone with documented Carolinas ancestry through enslaved family lines, the regional Black folk-magical traditions of the Carolina Lowcountry — particularly Gullah Geechee practice, distinct from the broader Hoodoo tradition — may be ancestrally specific to your line. The Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor Commission (gullahgeecheecorridor.org) maintains cultural and educational resources; the practitioner community is small and family-transmitted, but the cultural-historical material is accessible.

For the Welsh and broader Celtic traditions

The Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids (druidry.org) — the major contemporary international Druidic order, with substantial Welsh-language and Welsh-tradition material in its training program. The training is correspondence-based and runs three grades over multiple years; the order is non-initiatory in the strict sense and accessible to solo practitioners.

The Druid Network (druidnetwork.org) — UK-based, more loosely organized than OBOD, with strong emphasis on Welsh and broader British Celtic practice.

For Welsh specifically: the Anglesey Druid Order (angleseydruidorder.co.uk) and Kristoffer Hughes (its head, author of From the Cauldron Born and substantial writing on Cerridwen and the Welsh tradition) work explicitly within the Welsh stream. Hughes is one of the most substantial contemporary Welsh-tradition practitioner-teachers writing in English. For Rhiannon devotion specifically — given your call — Hughes's writing engages her substantively, as does Jhenah Telyndru's work (her Avalon Within and the broader Sisterhood of Avalon).

The Sisterhood of Avalon (sisterhoodofavalon.org) — an international women's spiritual organization specifically working with the Welsh-Brythonic goddess tradition. Founded by Jhenah Telyndru. Offers a structured program of study, sustained community, and devotional practice with the Welsh goddesses (including Rhiannon). For a woman with Welsh matrilineal lineage being called by Rhiannon, this is potentially a direct fit; worth investigating seriously.

For Welsh-language and Welsh-cultural grounding — the Welsh Government's Welsh language learning resources (learnwelsh.cymru) and the broader Welsh cultural institutions (the National Library of Wales, the National Museum of Wales) make access to the primary sources increasingly possible even for non-Welsh-speaking diaspora practitioners. Engaging with the language, even minimally, transforms the practice; reading the Mabinogion in Welsh (with translation alongside) is a different experience than reading it in English alone.

For the Western Hermetic and esoteric stream

If the Freemason inheritance on your father's matrilineal side eventually calls you to investigate, the contemporary landscape:

Mainstream Freemasonry (msana.com — the Masonic Service Association of North America) maintains directories of regular lodges. Membership requires petition and acceptance; the formal initiation process is multi-year. Worth knowing: traditional mainstream Freemasonry in most jurisdictions remains exclusively male; co-Masonry and women's Masonic orders exist (the Honorable Order of Universal Co-Freemasonry, the Order of Women Freemasons in the UK) but are smaller and less geographically distributed.

For the more esoterically-oriented Masonic and adjacent rites — the various Rosicrucian orders (AMORC, BOTA — Builders of the Adytum), the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn lineages (multiple successor organizations claim Golden Dawn descent; vet carefully), the OTO (Ordo Templi Orientis, in the Crowleyan / Thelemic stream). These are real organizations with real teaching traditions; the contemporary landscape is uneven and requires substantial vetting before commitment.

For Continental / European esoteric Freemasonry specifically — given the Dutch ancestral connection — the Memphis-Misraim rite, the Strict Observance, the Martinist tradition, and the various Continental Hermetic Masonic streams have their own histories and contemporary operating bodies. These are more specialized; if this becomes a direction the practice moves, the work is more research-heavy.

For non-initiated study of the Hermetic-esoteric tradition — without committing to any organization, substantial contemporary scholarly and practitioner communities exist. The Esoteric Book Conference, the journal Aries (academic study of Western esotericism), the Society for the Academic Study of Magic, and the broader online communities around contemporary occultism. Worth knowing as the parallel academic-and-practitioner community to the more lineage-bound organizations.

For broader pagan and witchcraft community

Solitary practitioners with community needs can find substantial connection through gatherings, festivals, and online communities without committing to any specific tradition. The major US pagan gatherings — PantheaCon (now defunct, but successor gatherings are emerging), PaganiCon, Mystic South, ConVocation, the Heartland Pagan Festival, and regional gatherings — bring practitioners across traditions together for several days of workshops, ritual, and community. The Cherry Hill Seminary (cherryhillseminary.org) offers academic-quality pagan studies coursework.

For online community — the contemporary pagan and witchcraft online landscape has consolidated substantially in the last decade. The major surviving forums (the Cauldron, Reddit's r/pagan and tradition-specific subreddits, various Discord servers), the witchcraft podcast network (Sabbat, Hermit's Lamp, Rune Soup with Gordon White), and the sustained Substack and blog community (Sarah Anne Lawless's archived work, Gordon White, Sarah Iles Johnston's academic-public work) collectively constitute a real practitioner community accessible without geographic constraint.

The principles for vetting

The contemporary spiritual marketplace contains both serious teachers and exploitative operations. Some general principles for distinguishing:

Legitimate teachers and traditions generally do not charge substantial sums for entry-level engagement, do not promise rapid initiation or rapid spiritual development, do not isolate practitioners from outside contact or other teachers, do not require sexual contact for spiritual progress, are open about their own teachers and lineage, and are accountable to communities beyond their immediate students. The opposite of any of these is a substantial warning sign.

Specifically for traditions with initiation requirements: the legitimate elders generally test for seriousness over years rather than welcoming quick commitment, and they typically require substantial reading and demonstrated understanding before any movement toward initiation. The exploitative operators, by contrast, are quick to embrace, quick to initiate, and quick to charge.

For trauma-informed work specifically: any practitioner who claims to do trauma work should be able to name their training (Somatic Experiencing, Sensorimotor Psychotherapy, NARM, EMDR, IFS, etc.), should hold appropriate credentials, and should be willing to discuss the limits of their competence. Anyone claiming to do trauma work without specific training and credentialing should be approached with substantial skepticism.

The work of finding teachers and community is its own practice and requires its own patience. The right people generally arrive when the practice is ready for them, not before. Many practitioners report years of solo work followed by what felt like an inevitable arrival of teacher or community when the practice had developed enough to actually receive what was being offered. Trust the timing; do the foundational work; the company will arrive when it arrives.
Build

Your practitioner and community map — a developing record

Across the years of practice, build a working file of practitioners, organizations, and communities you have learned of, vetted, or eventually engaged with. What it accumulates:

  • Practitioners and organizations across the streams you are working in — Foor-trained practitioners, Welsh teachers, Hoodoo and Diaspora practitioners, somatic and trauma clinicians, the eventual practitioner you find for the deeper work the document has named
  • Notes on what you have learned about each — credentials, lineage, how they were recommended, your impressions if you have engaged them
  • A specific shortlist of practitioners you intend to reach out to when you are ready, even if "ready" is years away
  • The practitioners or teachers who have appeared and been wrong for you — equally important data, kept so you do not return to them
  • Any communities, gatherings, or events you have attended or intend to attend — what was useful, what was not, whether to return

Building this list is itself part of the practice. The act of researching practitioners and communities, even before reaching out, develops the discernment needed to eventually find the right ones.

Research thread

The contemporary literature on cult dynamics, spiritual abuse, and the failure modes of teacher-student relationships is worth knowing as background — Janja Lalich's Take Back Your Life, Steven Hassan's broader catalogue, and the academic literature on coercive control in religious contexts. The point is not paranoia but discernment; understanding the actual mechanics of abusive religious dynamics helps protect against them. The Cult Education Institute (culteducation.com) maintains documentation on specific groups; the Spiritual Abuse Resources network is a smaller but useful resource.
