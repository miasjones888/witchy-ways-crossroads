§ IV.I the wheel running

The first complete turning

The first complete turning of the wheel is its own event. Across twelve months, you observes each of the eight stations as it arrives. Some land easily; some require translation; a few do not land in any way that feels satisfying, and those become research material for what specifically the local San Diego adaptation needs to develop. By Samhain of the second year, you have lived inside the year's rhythm once and the wheel begins to feel less like imposed structure and more like the actual shape time is doing.

What you notice in the first turning: the body responds to seasonal observance differently than to daily practice. The seasonal rite is felt as event in a way the daily candle is not. The first few stations may feel performed — you are doing what she has read about, not yet what the year is asking. By the third or fourth station, the year begins teaching you what its turnings actually feel like in her specific land. Imbolc in San Diego has California poppies beginning to bloom, hill-green at peak; Beltane is May Gray and the wet-to-dry transition with the fire-watch awareness beginning; Mabon is the Kumeyaay New Year and the acorn drop, with the marine layer breaking down into clear bright autumn light. The bioregional adaptation, which was conceptual research at chapter 2, becomes lived attention.

The second-year stations develop differently. You (if you) has observed Samhain once now observes Samhain again, with a year's accumulation of grimoire entries and seasonal practice between her and the previous Samhain. The pattern that emerges across the second turning is what the practice has been producing — what survived the year, what was released, what was carried forward. The wheel teaches its own architecture by being lived. No book engagement substitutes for this; the year is the teacher.

§ IV.II the lunar architecture

The second calendar running underneath

Lunar timekeeping is a calendrical discipline with deep roots in Mediterranean and European ritual practice. The serious version of working with the moon is not "moon vibes" but a sustained relationship with a second calendar that runs underneath the secular one — twelve to thirteen lunar months per solar year, with the new and full moons as the operative poles.

The technical distinction worth knowing: the dark moon, the new moon, and the first visible crescent are three different things. The Hellenic dark-moon working — the heneē kai nea, "the old and new" — is performed on the night of complete invisibility, not at the astronomical moment of conjunction or at the first visible sliver. Most modern lunar calendars conflate these. The serious practice is to distinguish them, regardless of which deity-frame eventually holds the dark-moon work.

Across the first year, the minimum lunar discipline is twenty-four marked nights — the dark moon and the full moon, twice per lunar month. Twelve to thirteen cycles unfold across the year. By the end of the first year of consistent lunar work, you have substantial accumulated material: the working diary of each dark moon and full moon, what was worked, what arrived after, the dreams that landed on the waning crescent and balsamic phases, the bodily and emotional patterns that align with the lunar cycle (or do not — the menstrual-lunar literature is contested, and your own observation across years is more reliable than any framework imposed in advance).

The dark moon admits multiple substantive frames; you explores rather than commits during the first year. The Hellenic-chthonic frame — the Hekatean Deipnon, traditionally an offering placed at a crossroads, including a small meal for Hekate and the restless dead — is one of the most theologically substantive contemporary articulations and is the frame most accessible through contemporary practitioner-scholarship (d'Este, Brannen, Johnston). The Welsh-Celtic frame holds the dark moon as Arianrhod's silver wheel turning, the Caer Arianrhod (the corona borealis) as the otherworld-realm; the Welsh line — you has direct lineage-access here. The Norse-Germanic frame holds the dark moon as Máni's hidden phase, with disir and the female-dead cluster of the wild-hunt traditions. The Yoruba and African Diasporic frame holds Yemoja and the lunar associations of the orisha. The goddess-spirituality and Wiccan frame holds the dark moon as the Crone aspect of the triple goddess, with substantial Reclaiming and women's-mysteries practice developed around it. The Vedic and Buddhist-tantric frames have their own substantive lunar-practice traditions (the amavasya, the new-moon practice). The first year is when you explores which frame, if any, the practice opens into. Don't choose in advance. What translates across all of them: an additional candle on the dark moon, an offering vessel, written work addressing what is being released, deliberate engagement with what the dark of the moon makes available to attention.

The full moon working in contemporary practice — the esbat in the Wiccan vocabulary, the various names in other traditions — runs differently. Divinatory operation is most common; sustained altar contact, oracular work, charging of waters and tools by leaving them in the moonlight overnight. The full moon's light makes what the dark moon held available accessible in different register. The two poles work together; neither alone is the practice.

→ dark moon ≠ new moon ≠ first crescent. distinguish them. serious practice begins here.
→ the menstrual-lunar relationship is contested in the literature (Cutler 1980, Helfrich-Förster 2021, Komada 2021). track empirically over years. develop your own framework.
practice · the two-pole working

The minimum lunar discipline

twice monthly · 30–60 minutes each

Twenty-four marked nights per year. Dark moon: extended sit at the altar, the additional dark-keyed candle (red or black), written work addressing what is being released or buried, the offering vessel filled and later poured to the earth. Full moon: full ritual at the altar using the three-part structure, three-card divinatory pull on a question being held, charging of water/stones/tools in moonlight overnight. Develop your own structure for each over time, drawing from Pearson, d'Este, and your own working diary. The discipline is consistency.

build · the lunar calendar

The year's twenty-four nights mapped

Map every dark moon and every full moon for the next twelve months in your actual calendar. Use a serious astronomical source — timeanddate.com or a printed almanac — not a moon-phase app, which often imprecisely conflates dark and new moon. Set reminders the day before each. Over time, build:

  • A wall-mounted lunar calendar or almanac for the year — Susan Baylies's lunar calendars, Llewellyn's Witches' Calendar, or your own constructed one
  • A lunar working journal — a separate notebook or section in your grimoire, with entries for each dark moon and full moon: phase, weather, what was worked, what arrived after, dreams that landed near the working
  • An ongoing record of correspondences you observe — what kinds of working go well at which phases, what kinds of dreams arrive, what your nervous system does. Over years, this becomes your own developed lunar lore.

§ IV.III body deeper

What surfaces when the container holds

The first weeks taught the regulation work — the somatic anchors, the long exhale, hand on heart, the pendulation principle. The first year is what happens when the regulation work has been running long enough that the body trusts the container and begins surfacing material that has been waiting for a place to land.

What surfaces depends entirely on you. Some practitioners surface grief that had been deferred; some surface anger that had been suppressed; some surface body memories that had been held below conscious access. For a practitioner with substantial CPTSD history, what surfaces in the first year is real and not generic — it is what her specific body has been holding. The container has now been built well enough that she can be in relationship with it without being overwhelmed. The pendulation principle governs all of this: brief contact, deliberate return to safety, brief contact again, never sustained immersion in the difficult material until practitioner support is in place.

The strict regulation-vs-excavation boundary holds throughout. What surfaces in the first year is to be witnessed, regulated, and held within the container the practice has built — not opened, not excavated, not pushed into. Excavation work belongs in chapter 6, with practitioner support explicit. The first year develops your capacity to hold what surfaces; it does not yet do the deep work of metabolizing it.

Ogden's Trauma and the Body framework — the window of tolerance, the hierarchical processing of trauma, the body as resource — is the contemporary clinical apparatus that supports this stage. The window of tolerance names the range within which you can hold what arises without flooding (hyperarousal) or shutting down (hypoarousal). The first year's body work develops your window of tolerance — through the daily practice, the regulation anchors, the candle-fire's parasympathetic effect, the sustained breath work, the consistent return to the same altar at the same time. The container the apparatus builds is what makes the larger window possible.

The grief work specifically: Francis Weller's The Wild Edge of Sorrow develops the framework of grief as ongoing ritual practice rather than as event to be processed and finished. For a practitioner whose lineage configuration carries inherited grief — the matrilineal Welsh inheritance from a colonized people, the West African Diaspora inheritance through enslavement, the Hermetic-Masonic inheritance entwined with the violence at its origin — the grief work runs alongside everything else. The Weller-derived grief tending rite (drafted in chapter 2) is the operative practice; the first year is when the practice begins doing what grief tending actually does.

→ what surfaces is yours specifically. not generic. the container holds your body's particular held material.
→ regulation-vs-excavation: held strictly. ch 6 is where excavation lives, with practitioner support. do not blur this.
practice · grief tending

The Weller-derived rite

quarterly or as needed · ~30 minutes

Set the altar with water in a clear bowl, salt in a small dish, a candle. Sit. Light. Name what you are grieving — aloud or in writing. Let grief move if it wants; do not perform tears that aren't there. After however long you stay, dissolve a pinch of salt in the water. Pour onto the earth (a plant, the garden). The salt-water carries the grief out. Read Weller for the framework.

§ IV.IV the question of belief — held lightly

Hold it open; do not try to resolve it

If you are entering this for something to believe in, the question of what you actually believe will arrive across the first year. This chapter does not engage the substantive philosophical apparatus — Edward Butler on Platonic polytheism, Iamblichus on theurgy, Hillman on archetypal psychology, Sophie Strand on the contemporary depth-mythopoetic. That apparatus is foundational and you will eventually engage it, but year one is not the time. Reading philosophy about something that has not yet happened experientially is reading at the wrong stage. The substantive engagement with the three positions a thoughtful practitioner can hold lives in chapter 6, where you have lived enough relationship with the deities to have an actual question about what those relationships are.

The honest position for the first year: most serious practitioners drift between several metaphysical positions over the arc of a practice. The drift is not failure of belief; it is the practice working on the believer. The deities and entities engaged through ritual become more real over time, in a sense that does not map neatly onto the literal-versus-metaphorical distinction the modern Western mind defaults to. Hold the question lightly. Do not try to resolve the metaphysics in advance. Whatever relationships the practice opens — with specific deities, with the dead, with the land, with the ancestors, with the elemental presences — they are operative regardless of what you currently believe about them. The practice does not require the answer. And the practice does not require, in year one, knowing which deity or tradition is yours. The wide-survey discipline holds.

practice · the metaphysical journal

A parallel record — kept across the years

monthly · 30–60 minutes

Once a month, in a dedicated section of the grimoire, write what you currently believe. Not what you should believe, not what the texts say, not what your readings have told you — what you actually believe right now. Be specific. Whatever deity or figure you have been addressing — if any — is she a real entity, a Platonic principle, an archetypal pattern in my own psyche, something else? The dead I name — are they continuing persons, fields of memory, archetypal presences? The land — does it know me? What am I doing when I light the candle? Which figures, traditions, or currents have I noticed myself returning to, and which haven't taken? Write a paragraph or two. Date it. Do not edit. Move on. Over months and years, you will produce a substantial record of your developing metaphysical position and of which currents have actually claimed your attention. Read back through it occasionally. The drift will become visible. By year three or four, the journal itself will tell you what frame your practice has actually been working within and which deities, if any, have arrived — at which point chapter 6's substantive philosophical engagement becomes useful rather than premature.

§ IV.V animism early

The world becoming inhabited

Animism is the foundational philosophical position underneath every serious witchcraft practice — that the world is alive, that the non-human is populated by selves with whom relationship is possible and necessary. It is not decoration on the practice; it is the ground on which everything else stands.

Graham Harvey's working definition, from Animism: Respecting the Living World (Columbia, 2005): animists are "people who recognize that the world is full of persons, only some of whom are human, and that life is always lived in relationship with others." The trees are persons. The rivers are persons. The dead are persons. The animals are persons. The land is a person, or many. The first year of practice is when your perception begins to shift — not as belief decided in advance, but as developed attention that comes from the land sit, the plant introduction, the animal augury practiced consistently for long enough that the world stops being scenery.

The land sit specifically: one place outdoors, weekly, the same place across at least a year, no phone, no book, no agenda. Notice what is alive there — trees, birds, insects, plants underfoot, weather, the quality of air. Over weeks and months, the place becomes specific rather than generic. The plants become specific plants. The birds become specific species whose calls you learn. The light becomes specific kinds of light at specific times of year. This is not meditation. It is the establishment of relationship. Over a year, the place becomes someone you know, and you become someone known by the place. This is the foundation of the animist sensibility — not as belief but as developed perception.

Plant introduction, one plant per season, three months each. The starting plant set depends on which currents the practice is opening into; you can begin from any tradition's plant-spine and let the relationships develop. European witchcraft-traditional plants (mugwort Artemisia vulgaris, yarrow Achillea millefolium, vervain Verbena officinalis, rosemary Salvia rosmarinus, rue Ruta graveolens, wormwood Artemisia absinthium) — the cunning-folk and traditional-witchcraft register, including but not limited to chthonic-keyed practice. Welsh and Celtic plants for the matrilineal Welsh line — you (hawthorn, oak, ash, rowan, mistletoe, vervain again, betony, hazel) — the Druidic-Celtic spine. African Diasporic and Hoodoo plants for the paternal line — you (High John the Conqueror root, Adam and Eve root, Five Finger Grass, Devil's Shoestring, Master of the Woods, Solomon's Seal, life-everlasting). Hellenic plants for you (if your) work opens classical (laurel, cypress, asphodel, pomegranate, myrrh, frankincense). Norse and Germanic plants for the Heathen-tradition (linden, ash, juniper, mugwort again, yarrow again, valerian). For your San Diego context, supplement with native chaparral plants — black sage (Salvia mellifera), white sage (Salvia apiana — sourced ethically given the appropriation question), California sagebrush (Artemisia californica), yerba santa (Eriodictyon californicum). Read the botanical and folk-magical descriptions. Find the plant growing wild near you if possible; if not, grow it. Sit with it. Sketch it. Notice when it flowers, when it fruits, when it dies back. Make a small offering at its base. Speak to it. Listen.

The indigenous-engagement question matters in the animist work. Most of what is called "shamanism" or "animism" in contemporary Western spiritual marketplace is appropriated from indigenous traditions in ways that are both ethically troubling and practically corrupting. The witchcraft tradition has its own animist inheritance — European, well-documented, recoverable — and that is the inheritance to work in. Where contemporary indigenous teachers have explicitly written for non-indigenous audiences and offered their teaching across cultures (Robin Wall Kimmerer in Braiding Sweetgrass, Tyson Yunkaporta in Sand Talk, Malidoma Patrice Somé), engagement is appropriate and welcomed. Where the material is being repackaged by non-indigenous teachers without lineage authorization, it is not.

For your San Diego context specifically: the Kumeyaay are the indigenous people of San Diego County. Knowing whose land you live on is the foundational move of animist practice in this register. Native Land Digital (native-land.ca) is the standard resource. Beyond naming: cultural-historical learning through the Museum of Us, Cuyamaca College's Kumeyaay Studies program, the public-facing tribal museums. The boundary held: substantive engagement with Kumeyaay traditional knowledge requires direct relationship to the Kumeyaay community, not borrowing from book-research. The cultural-historical engagement is appropriate; the borrowing of ritual structure or specific traditions is not.

Foundational animist practice

weekly · 30–60 minutes · the same place each time

Choose one specific place outdoors that you can return to weekly. A particular tree in a park, a stretch of beach, a corner of a garden, a hillside — anywhere outdoors that you have reasonable access to. The same place each time, ideally for at least a year. Sit there. Without phone. Without book. Without agenda. Notice what is alive there. Over weeks and months, you will begin to notice the place rather than just inhabiting it. The plants become specific plants you recognize. The birds become specific species whose calls you learn. The light becomes specific kinds of light at specific times of year. This is not meditation. It is the establishment of relationship.

§ IV.VI herbalism and the witch's garden

The medicinal, magical, and operative plant tradition

The plant introduction in § IV.V is the relational ground — sit with this plant, learn its name, watch it through the year. This section is where that ground develops into the systematic herbalist-witch tradition the practice eventually carries. Three distinct dimensions integrate: medicinal herbalism (the plant as remedy and clinical-vitalist agent), magical herbalism (the plant as operative material in the working register, with traditional correspondences and spell-craft applications), and wildcrafting (the relational and ethical practice of harvesting, growing, and making relationship with plants in their actual lives). The serious witchcraft practice eventually develops all three; the year-one entry is to begin the threads that will integrate across decades.

The wise woman tradition. Susun Weed (born 1946, founder of the Wise Woman Center in Woodstock, NY, founder of Ash Tree Press) is the principal figure of the contemporary Wise Woman tradition — a women-centered herbal-medicine framework that distinguishes itself from the heroic-medicine and scientific-pharmacological registers through its emphasis on nourishing-herbal-infusion, plant-allies, the body's own healing intelligence, and the cyclical-feminine register of women's health across the life cycle. Weed's Healing Wise (1989) is the foundational text. Wise Woman Herbal for the Childbearing Year (1985), Menopausal Years the Wise Woman Way (1992), Down There: Sexual and Reproductive Health the Wise Woman Way (2011), and Healing Wise together form the substantial corpus. Critical context required: Weed's teaching practices and her positions during the early AIDS crisis have been substantively criticized; her treatment of menopause as a women-only experience excludes trans and non-binary practitioners; the broader Wise Woman tradition has substantial internal critique. Read with awareness; Weed's foundational position in the field makes engagement worthwhile, but the critical literature is part of the responsible reading.

The accessible American herbalism tradition. Rosemary Gladstar (born 1948, founder of Sage Mountain Herbal Retreat Center, founder of the original California School of Herbal Studies) is the most accessible and most-recommended contemporary American herbalist for the introductory practitioner. Her work is gentle, well-grounded, women-and-family-centered, and has shaped a generation of herbalists. Rosemary Gladstar's Medicinal Herbs: A Beginner's Guide (Storey, 2012) is the foundational beginner-text. Herbal Healing for Women (Fireside, 1993). Rosemary Gladstar's Family Herbal (Storey, 2001). Herbs for Common Ailments (Storey, 2014). For you (if your) herbalism opens American-traditional and women-centered, Gladstar is the entry-point.

The Western traditional herbalism tradition. Matthew Wood (born 1954) is the principal contemporary scholar-practitioner of Western traditional herbalism — the tradition that integrates folk-medicine, vitalist-physiology, humoral theory, and plant-energetics within a coherent practitioner framework. The Earthwise Herbal: A Complete Guide to Old World Medicinal Plants (North Atlantic, 2008) and The Earthwise Herbal: A Complete Guide to New World Medicinal Plants (North Atlantic, 2009) are the substantial reference works for Old World and New World plants respectively. The Practice of Traditional Western Herbalism (North Atlantic, 2004) is the methodological-foundational text. Wood's work is demanding and rewarding; for you (if your) herbalism matures into substantive clinical-practitioner depth, foundational.

The plant-intelligence and plant-spirit tradition. Stephen Harrod Buhner (1952-2022) is the principal contemporary author bridging plant-intelligence as research subject and as relational reality. The Lost Language of Plants: The Ecological Importance of Plant Medicines for Life on Earth (Chelsea Green, 2002) is the foundational ecological-philosophical text. The Secret Teachings of Plants: The Intelligence of the Heart in the Direct Perception of Nature (Bear & Co., 2004) is you-philosophical text on plant communication. Plant Intelligence and the Imaginal Realm (Bear & Co., 2014) is the more substantial later synthesis. Buhner's clinical work — Healing Lyme (Raven Press, 2nd ed. 2015) and the broader chronic-illness corpus — is the substantial practitioner-clinical work. For you (if your) herbalism opens phenomenological-and-relational, Buhner is the principal voice.

The poison path. Coby Michael (contemporary practitioner-scholar, M.A. in religious studies) is the principal contemporary practitioner-author for the baneful-and-poison-plant tradition specifically — Datura, Belladonna, Hyoscyamus (henbane), Mandragora (mandrake), Aconite (monkshood), Conium (poison hemlock), and the broader nightshade and witching-herb apparatus. The Poison Path Herbal: Baneful Herbs, Medicinal Nightshades, and Ritual Entheogens (Park Street Press, 2021) is the foundational text. The poison path is not an exotic side-quest; it is the witchcraft-traditional herbalism — the witching herbs the historical witch trials repeatedly named, the flying-ointment apparatus, the operative-and-medicinal use of plants that contemporary herbalism otherwise treats only as toxic. Substantial caveat: baneful-plant work is genuinely dangerous; some of these plants can kill at small doses; engagement requires substantive study, careful sourcing, ethical clarity, and ideally direct teaching from an experienced practitioner. The chapter 6 § VI.XII develops the poison path at depth; this section names it as territory that develops across years, not material to engage in year one.

Substantial compendium of the thirteen most consequential European witching herbs (mugwort, yarrow, vervain, mandrake, henbane, belladonna, datura, wormwood, plus four others) with substantial folkloric and operative material. The book is one of the most accessible entries into the witching-herb tradition for practitioners who want the operative depth without the full poison-path apparatus.

§ IV.VII familiar relationships

The animal-spirit working bond

The augury practice from § IV.V establishes the recognition-level animal relationship — noticing, recording, attending to what arrives. The familiar relationship is the operative depth that can develop from sustained augury practice across years. The familiar in the historical witchcraft tradition is the specific animal-spirit (sometimes embodied in an actual animal, sometimes operating spectrally, sometimes both) with whom the witch develops a sustained working bond. The witch-trial archive contains substantial documentation of the familiar relationship — Emma Wilby's Cunning Folk and Familiar Spirits (Sussex Academic, 2005) is the foundational scholarly treatment, drawn from the Scottish, Welsh, and English witch-trial confession-records. The relationship is operative: the familiar provides counsel, divinatory perception, sometimes participates in workings, sometimes acts on the witch's behalf in the spirit-realm. The contemporary practitioner working in any tradition with cunning-folk or traditional-witchcraft inheritance can develop familiar relationships across years.

The familiar is not a pet. The household cat or dog can sometimes become an embodied dimension of the familiar relationship, but the familiar is a specific spiritual presence the witch has come into relationship with — sometimes through formal rite, sometimes through extended attention to a specific species or individual animal, sometimes through dream and trance, sometimes through ancestral inheritance (the familiar runs in some lineages). The witch-trial confessions record familiars taking forms across species: cats, dogs, hares, toads, mice, birds (specifically corvids, owls, ravens, hens), insects (bees, flies), reptiles (snakes, lizards). The contemporary practitioner's familiar tends to arrive through attention rather than through deliberate construction; you notice that one specific species or individual has been arriving repeatedly in dreams, in noticed signs, in the body's response, in the augury, and gradually develops the relationship as an explicit working bond.

§ IV.VIII dreamwork systematically

The dream as practice across traditions

The dream-record practice from chapter 2 — write down what you remember, dated, before getting out of bed — is the foundational discipline. This section is where dreamwork develops into systematic practice. Across virtually every tradition the document treats, dreaming is a developed religious-magical discipline: the Asclepian dream-incubation tradition in the Hellenic, the Welsh-Celtic dreaming literature (the Dream of Rhonabwy, the otherworld-arrival dreams in the Mabinogi), the African Diasporic dreaming tradition where dreaming is communal-ancestral practice (Stephanie Rose Bird's chapters develop this substantively), the Norse dreaming tradition (the dream-warnings in the saga literature), the Vedic and Buddhist dream-yoga traditions, the Christian dream-tradition (Joseph and the dreams of Pharaoh, the dreams of the saints, the dream-tradition of the desert fathers and mothers), the contemporary clinical-psychological dream tradition (Jung, Hillman, Bosnak, Aizenstat). The serious practitioner develops dreamwork as a parallel discipline to the daily card and the operative working — across decades, the dream-record becomes one of your substantial working-tradition documents.

The Asclepian tradition is the source-tradition; the contemporary practitioner-incubation is its descendant.

§ IV.IX bardic druidry — the parallel-tradition track

Welsh-Celtic druidic practice as parallel inheritance

The contemporary Druid revival is a substantive parallel tradition to the witchcraft register the document develops. Druidry and witchcraft are not the same practice — they descend from related but distinct streams of British and Celtic tradition, they have separate contemporary lineages and organizations, they hold different relationships to the deities, the natural world, and the operative-magical apparatus. For your matrilineal Welsh-Celtic line specifically, Druidry is direct lineage-access in a way that broader witchcraft is not — the Welsh and Irish primary literature is the foundational text-tradition both witchcraft and Druidry draw from, but Druidry treats it as the spine of the practice rather than as one source-stream among several. This document does not direct you to Druidry over witchcraft. It names Druidry as a parallel track running alongside the witchcraft register, accessible at any depth, integratable as the practice opens that direction.

Contemporary Druidry has a developed organizational and educational structure. Three principal contemporary Druid orders in the English-speaking world: The Order of Bards, Ovates, and Druids (OBOD) — founded 1964 in Britain by Ross Nichols; the largest contemporary Druid order; runs a substantial graded distance-learning curriculum in Bardic, Ovate, and Druid grades. Ár nDraíocht Féin / A Druid Fellowship (ADF) — founded 1983 in the United States by Isaac Bonewits; the principal North American Druid order; reconstructionist-leaning, with explicit polytheist devotional framework. The Ancient Order of Druids in America (AODA) — descends from a 19th-century American Druid lineage; smaller, with substantial focus on contemplative-and-philosophical Druid practice. Each order operates a graded curriculum loosely parallel to the historical bardic-ovate-druid sequence; the contemporary practitioner can engage at any depth (membership in an order is not required to read and practice the literature).

The bardic grade is the year-one Druidic engagement.
